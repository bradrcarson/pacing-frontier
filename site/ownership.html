Ownership and Industry Structure

This tab covers who owns and controls frontier AI today and what each family of proposals would require to change.

30 open and closed questions

The Question

Most proposals to pause, pace or contain frontier AI leave the companies in private hands and change who controls specific decisions. The proposals that change ownership come from two other debates. One concerns sharing AI’s gains, and the other concerns a lab that fails commercially.

  • Ownership and control are already separate. The OpenAI Foundation appoints every director of OpenAI Group PBC and held about 26% of its equity in October 2025 (OpenAI). Elon Musk holds about 85.1% of the voting power at SpaceX, which owns xAI (TechCrunch). Mark Zuckerberg holds 61.2% of Meta’s votes on 13.68% of its equity, per Wikipedia.
  • Three things can change hands. A proposal can move the economic return, the votes, or a veto over named decisions. Each can move without the others, so a stake proposal has to say which it wants.
  • Control has moved without ownership. In June 2026 the Commerce Secretary reportedly warned OpenAI not to release GPT-5.6 without approval. Export controls suspended access to two Anthropic models on June 12, 2026 and ended June 30; Fable 5 returned July 1 (Tom’s Hardware; Anthropic). The government held no share of either developer.
  • The two largest independent labs rent most of their hardware. OpenAI and Anthropic mostly rent compute from Microsoft, Oracle, Amazon and Google (Epoch AI). Rules that work through chips or data centers fall on those owners and their lenders.
  • Where ownership does change. Senator Sanders’s S. 4825 would place 50% of each covered company’s equity with the Treasury (bill text). Bruce Schneier and Nathan Sanders would nationalize OpenAI and Anthropic if markets reject them (schneier.com repost).

Across the four families

Most Stop and Pace proposals keep the labs private and add licenses and auditors. Contain and Channel proposals run from private labs in certified facilities to a single national or international project. Continue positions impose no change, though the federal government already holds equity in chipmakers and other suppliers (Council on Foreign Relations).

Compute Ownership

The two largest independent labs own little of the compute they use. A rule that works through hardware therefore reaches a different set of owners.

  • Owners. Epoch AI tracks chips owned by Google, Microsoft, Meta, Amazon, Oracle, CoreWeave, xAI and China as a bloc (Epoch AI, updated May 12, 2026). OpenAI and Anthropic mostly rent.
  • Share used by labs. OpenAI, Anthropic and xAI together controlled roughly 20% to 30% of operating AI compute at the end of 2025. The five best-resourced developers controlled less than half (Josh You, Epoch AI, May 20, 2026). The author calls the estimates tentative.
  • Sites. Epoch tracks 93 data centers totaling 13.8 GW, about 43% of deployed capacity (Epoch AI, October 5, 2026). The largest is SpaceX’s Colossus 2 in Memphis. Amazon owns the New Carlisle, Indiana site that Anthropic uses.
  • Stargate. SoftBank, OpenAI, Oracle and MGX fund the $500 billion project announced on January 21, 2025 (OpenAI). Epoch lists Oracle as owner of the Abilene, Texas site and OpenAI as its user (Epoch AI).
  • Lenders. The Hardwired Pause working paper (October 2026, unpublished draft) says private credit funds backed by pensions and insurers finance much construction (§6.1). CoreWeave carried about $35.1 billion of debt in June 2026, per Wikipedia.

  • Models stack. The MP Materials deal alone combines preferred equity, warrants, a price floor, an offtake contract, a loan and governance vetoes (MP Materials 8-K). The research notes infer that a lab arrangement could combine a license, a resident examiner, a golden share and warrants.
  • Many small levers, on one account. Deric Cheng and Corin Katzke argue that government will assemble control from many levers and is unlikely to buy the firms (Convergence Analysis). They describe the levers and do not recommend them, and the paper dates from August 28, 2024.
  • A staged path, on another account. Paul Triolo forecasts that Washington would move in stages: visibility, then conditional permission, then classified development streams (Cairo Review, June 17, 2026). He sees hard nationalization as a live option only after those stages fall short or a crisis hits.
  • A halt right. Arbel, Goldstein and Salib propose a government right to order temporary halts to training or deployment of powerful systems (AI Nationalization, August 1, 2026). Halt orders could address only catastrophic risk and “hard” corporate power, and the right would be broadly discretionary. They find nationalization unwarranted for national security failures, monopoly power and inequality (abstract page only).
  • A legal line sits at board control. In Lebron (1995) the Supreme Court treated a corporation created by special law, with a permanently government-appointed board majority, as part of the government (Justia). A lab under such a board could face constitutional limits that a private lab does not. No source found applies the ruling to AI.
  • Private versions already exist. OpenAI’s foundation, Anthropic’s trust and the Microsoft and OpenAI expert panel on AGI are in place today. A public trustee or director could attach to those bodies. That step is an inference in the notes, and no source proposes it.
  • The Two-Lane Plan’s rung. The plan (Brad Carson, unpublished, October 2026) takes equity or warrants in labs the government funds. It leaves open who owns the certified facilities.
  • By family (an inference). Most Stop and Pace proposals sit on the first rung, with structural rules added; MAGIC pairs a moratorium with one international developer. Contain and Channel proposals span minority stakes, government-owned facilities, consortia and international bodies. Continue positions keep today’s private ownership and mission-control arrangements.

What the Cases Suggest for AI Ownership

The readings below are inferences in the research notes unless a source is linked.

  • Portfolio size. Trackers count $26.7 billion to $28.4 billion of investment across 30 to 42 deals since January 2025 (Council on Foreign Relations; Fortune). The totals are sums invested or announced, and Fortune valued the Intel stake alone at about $42 billion in July 2026. No deal is in an AI model developer as of September 30, 2026 (The National).
  • Cost. The wartime and atomic cases put the capital cost on the Treasury and kept title public. The 2008 and 2009 cases bought securities in failing firms. AI capacity is already built with private and foreign money, so either model would require the government to buy or allocate costly existing assets.
  • Control. Four designs recur. The 1946 commission gave full public control, and conservatorship gives board powers without share ownership. COMSAT gave a minority of public directors, and the Intel stake gives economics with almost no control.
  • Exit. Securities in recovering firms were sold within about four to six years. Operating entities lasted longer: Conrail took 11 years and the Reconstruction Finance Corporation 25. Amtrak and the Tennessee Valley Authority were never sold.
  • Shareholders. Owners and lenders of failing firms recovered little. The suit over AIG’s rescue won no damages, and Chrysler’s secured lenders received 29 cents on the dollar (per Wikipedia). A solvent lab valued near $1 trillion starts from a different position.
  • Seizure. The Supreme Court struck down the 1952 steel seizure for want of a statute (Youngstown). It held in 1951 that seizing and operating a coal mine was a taking and that the government owed the operating losses (Pewee Coal).
  • Statutes that permit operation. At least three statutes let the government operate private facilities, each with compensation. They are Atomic Energy Act §108 (licensed facilities), Communications Act §706 (stations) and Military Selective Service Act §18 (plants whose owners refuse mandatory orders). The first two require war or a declared emergency, and under the first Congress must declare it.
  • Oversight. TARP had a statutory inspector general, quarterly reports to Congress, an oversight panel and GAO audits. Fortune reports that the 2025 to 2026 portfolio has none of these and no consolidated public ledger.
  • Licensing. One study found that the 1956 Bell decree raised follow-on innovation only outside telecommunications, Bell’s own market (Watzinger et al., abstract only).
  • International laboratories. CERN gives each of its 25 member states one vote (CERN). ITER fixes cost shares by treaty, and Europe pays about 45.6% of construction (ITER).
  • Closest fit to containment. The contractor-operated laboratory model has been stable for 80 years, and it governs facilities the government built. Applied to AI, the government would own or lease data centers and contract labs to run them. That is close to the certified facilities in the Two-Lane Plan.

Notes on the Rows

The points below are inferences from the research notes unless a source is cited.

  • Stop. Revenue would shift from frontier progress to inference on a fixed set of models. The Hardwired Pause working paper (October 2026, unpublished draft) estimates that existing accelerators cost about $500 billion. It uses $1 trillion as an illustrative buyback budget (§5.3.2).
  • Pace. OpenAI and Anthropic rent their compute, so an allocation rule has to bind the cloud owner as well as the lab. The access required is closer to bank examination than to current AI oversight.
  • Contain and Channel, and the Two-Lane Plan. If nothing leaves containment until the gate is passed, government contracts become the main income. Warrants could be set to vest only when a lab’s systems clear the gate, as Intel’s warrants vest on a contingency.
  • Embargo Plus U.S. Pause. The research notes contain no ownership analysis of this proposal. Its breakout clause restarts contained development, so the Two-Lane Plan’s facility and stake questions would arise at that point (an inference).
  • National project. The leading labs rent their compute, so consolidation would fall mainly on Microsoft, Amazon, Google, Oracle, SpaceX, CoreWeave and Stargate’s partners.
  • Continue. Public ownership could still arrive through a rescue of heavily indebted infrastructure. AIG, GM and Conrail came into public hands that way.

Shareholders and Contract Holders

  • A pause or gate takes no title. Shareholders bear the loss unless a court finds a regulatory taking. The notes’ reading is that pending IPOs strengthen the expectations argument and published safety rationales weaken it.
  • A release gate changes the business model. If nothing leaves containment until a gate is passed, commercial revenue from frontier systems stops. The notes infer that valuations would move toward those of defense contractors or regulated utilities. How those valuations compare is Not confirmed.
  • Directors have room to accept a pause (notes’ inference). Delaware law tells directors of a public benefit corporation to balance stockholders’ financial interests, affected parties and the stated public benefit (Delaware Code). Only holders of at least 2% of the shares can sue to enforce that balance. For listed companies the threshold is $2 million of stock if that is less.
  • Listing spreads the loss. SpaceX is already public. If OpenAI and Anthropic list, index funds and retirement savers would hold the equity. The Hardwired Pause working paper (October 2026, unpublished draft) expects a pause might delay those listings and thinks abandonment less plausible (§6.1).
  • Contract rights depend on continued development. Microsoft holds model intellectual property rights through 2032 and a $250 billion Azure purchase commitment from OpenAI (OpenAI).
  • Foreign investors are the hardest fit for containment (notes’ inference). SoftBank and MGX fund Stargate (OpenAI), and Singapore’s GIC and the Qatar Investment Authority hold Anthropic stakes (per Wikipedia). The MP Materials deal lets the Defense Department restrict foreign ownership above 14.9% (MP Materials 8-K). Investment-treaty protection for these holders is Not confirmed.

Employees

  • Exposure. An unofficial estimate puts OpenAI’s employee pool near 16% (Trending Topics, an aggregator). A $6.6 billion employee tender took place in 2026 (Private Market View, an aggregator). S. 4825 would tax later share issuances at 50%, and that covers conversions of employee compensation.
  • Under nationalization. Schneier and Sanders would move staff to civil-service pay (schneier.com repost). Cheng and Katzke note that U.S. labs pay largely in equity, which a takeover would disturb (Convergence Analysis).
  • Bargaining power. The project’s context brief reports that more than 1,000 frontier lab employees signed the “Pacing the Frontier” letter of July 28, 2026. The letter itself was not opened for this tab. Clearance or citizenship rules would collide with the labs’ international workforces (notes’ inference).

Creditors and Hardware Owners

  • Lenders. Data center debt sits largely with private credit funds backed by pensions and insurers, per the Hardwired Pause working paper (§6.1). The paper’s view is that a freeze adds little default risk if inference demand holds. It recommends limits on bank and insurer exposure, which the Two-Lane Plan also includes.
  • Hardware owners. The working paper compensates them through buybacks, continued inference revenue and insurance (§§5.3.2, 6.3.4). It offers AI companies nothing for lost frontier development.

What Practice Shows

  • Stakes so far were negotiated. No case was found since 2025 in which the government took equity from an unwilling company. S. 4825 would be the first compulsory transfer (notes’ inference).
  • Legal footing is uneven. Fortune reports that only the Development Finance Corporation has clear authority to own equity. Defense Production Act §303, as read, does not mention equity (50 U.S.C. 4533). OpenAI’s reported plan may need an act of Congress (CNN), and S. 4825 is itself new legislation.
  • Two design poles exist. Intel’s terms give economics with passive voting, and U.S. Steel’s give hard vetoes with no economics. A lab stake could copy either.
  • S. 4825 reaches beyond the labs. It covers firms with over $200 million of gross receipts in AI data centers, computing infrastructure, AI services or advanced robotics (Sanders bill text). A seven-member commission would vote the shares and name board representatives. Covered firms must separate AI from other businesses within 90 days.
  • Who governs the fund. The Independent Commission for Democratic AI would sit in the Treasury, with seven members on five-year terms and at most four from one party (S. 4825). It must manage the fund to promote worker welfare, public safety, fair competition, environmental sustainability and financial solvency. Its board representatives must act to advance the same goals, and the commission must seat as many as the fund’s shares allow.
  • Payout rule. The bill authorizes yearly appropriations equal to 5% of the fund’s average market value, less administrative costs (S. 4825).

Advocates

  • Sam Altman. He has pitched the administration since 2025 on ceding OpenAI equity to a public wealth fund (Semafor). The Washington Sun reported that on July 29, 2026 he told conservative economists he opposed the government itself taking OpenAI shares (September 9, 2026). OpenAI told the paper it backs a government-administered public wealth fund and would not support a government ownership stake.
  • President Trump and his advisers. The President cited the Intel stake as precedent (CNN). Treasury Secretary Bessent favored seeding Trump Accounts, and Commerce Secretary Lutnick favored a sovereign-wealth-style fund (Semafor, June 17, 2026).
  • Senator Sanders and Steve Bannon. Sanders’s bill would take 50% and frames the transfer as a tax, with no compensation clause. Bannon said AI companies should be forced to surrender 50% (Fortune).

Critics

  • Republican senators. Senator Cruz opposed federal equity stakes in private companies, and Senator Hawley objected to pairing bureaucrats with a Big Tech monopolist (Daily Signal). Senators Lummis and Kennedy voiced skepticism (Semafor).
  • Companies. Microsoft and Meta rejected the idea (Semafor). A source told Reuters that Anthropic and the administration had not discussed a stake (The Next Web).
  • Cato Institute. Tad DeHaven argues that government cannot be a neutral regulator of companies it owns (Thomson Reuters Checkpoint).
  • Transparency. Fortune reports no consolidated public ledger, and stakes in private companies produce no securities filings.
  • Payout doubts. Data Center Dynamics notes that the large AI companies are losing money, so near-term payments are unlikely. One report cites a $7 trillion estimate for the Sanders fund and does not name its source (The Next Web).
  • Leicht and Ball. They argue that government stakes in frontier labs bet on one view of where AI’s value will land (Leicht and Ball). This item comes from companion research notes and was not opened for this tab.

Fit With the Four Families

  • Stop and Pace. Neither company’s paper ties gain-sharing to a pause, a gate or a change in control. The AI Futures Project pacing options do not discuss government as owner, investor or customer.
  • Contain and Channel. The Two-Lane Plan pairs equity or warrants with containment and a release gate.
  • Continue. No change is imposed, yet OpenAI has reportedly offered equity with no pause attached.
  • A tension. Senator Sanders’s stop bill and S. 4825 pull against each other financially, on the notes’ reading. A fund holding half of each AI company depends on value that a development ban would reduce.

Concentration of Power and Safeguards

Writers who disagree on almost everything else agree that control of frontier AI by a single actor is a first-order danger. They hold that the danger exists whether the actor is a company or a government, and they split on the remedy.

Safeguards Sorted by What They Divide

The Forethought paper on AI-enabled coups holds that company and government control need similar safeguards. The research notes sort its list into four groups, and ownership is only one of them.

What Is DividedSafeguards Proposed

OwnershipSeveral projects; a public-private form if one project is created
  • The 1946 benchmark. The atomic settlement used a five-member civilian commission, a nine-member scientific advisory committee, a military liaison committee and a joint committee of Congress (DOE/OSTI). The notes suggest measuring any national AI project against that list.
  • One-person vetoes. The President personally holds the U.S. Steel golden share while in office (AP via Spotlight PA). By the Forethought criteria, a veto held by a multi-member body would fit AI better (notes’ inference).
  • Committees can go either way. In AI 2027 a joint company and government committee votes 6 to 4 to slow down in one ending (AI 2027). It votes 6 to 4 to continue in the other (race ending).
  • Two routes to one federal control point. S. 4825 gives a commission half the votes in every covered company, and AI 2027 consolidates compute under one lab with a joint committee. The concentration critique applies to both (notes’ inference).
  • Political weight of the firms. The Hardwired Pause working paper (October 2026, unpublished draft) reports heavy political spending by frontier firms. It cites about $265 million pledged to political groups in the 2026 cycle (§§7.1, 7.4). That figure comes from a Wall Street Journal report that was not opened.

By Family

  • Stop. The Hardwired Pause authors say a pause keeps the prize from being created. They concede it could lock in incumbents’ market power and the values built into whitelisted models (§§6.4, 6.5).
  • Pace. The pacing options leave several private firms in place under embedded auditors. The company-side safeguards would then apply (an inference).
  • Contain and Channel. Critics hold that a single national project maximizes concentration and may speed China’s response (Forethought). The Two-Lane Plan’s adversary finding would need safeguards against misuse by a single official (notes’ inference).
  • Continue. Concentration proceeds by private means: a handful of labs, each tied to one or two hyperscalers (notes’ inference). AI Now argues that these firms are already structured to be too big to fail (AI Now).
