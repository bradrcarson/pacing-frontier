Uses of Authority in 2026

  • February, Defense Production Act threat. On February 24, 2026 Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth threatened to invoke the Act against Anthropic (Rozenshtein). He demanded that it drop usage restrictions on autonomous weapons and mass surveillance by February 27 or 28 (sources differ; the D.C. Circuit gives February 27). Anthropic refused on February 26. On February 27 the President and the Secretary denounced the decision, and the Secretary began removing Claude from the Defense Department’s supply chain (D.C. Circuit opinion).
  • May and June, testing agreements and order. On May 5, 2026 CAISI announced agreements with Google DeepMind, Microsoft and xAI (NIST/CAISI). Executive Order 14409 of June 2, 2026 directs agencies to design a voluntary framework for pre-release government access of up to 30 days. Its Section 3 authorizes no mandatory licensing, preclearance or permitting requirement (see the Pace: Slowdowns and Gates tab). Earlier, Axios reported talk of pre-clearance requirements for new models (Axios).
  • June, export controls. A June 12, 2026 Commerce Department directive applied export controls to Claude Fable 5 and Mythos 5 for all foreign nationals, wherever located (Anthropic). Legal commentary reads the letter as an “is informed” license requirement that cited 15 C.F.R. § 744.22 and 50 U.S.C. § 4817(b)(1) (Harvard Law Review Blog; Mayer Brown). Anthropic removed access for every user, and the Commerce Secretary withdrew the controls on June 30 (his statement, via Fox Business; The Next Web). The directive covered foreign nationals only, yet U.S. users lost access too. After government approval on June 26, Mythos 5 returned for a set of U.S. organizations. Fable 5 returned on July 1, 2026, 19 days after the directive (Anthropic).
  • June, court challenge. Legion LegalTech Corp. sued on June 23, 2026 in the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia (MLex). The complaint alleges the directive exceeded the export control statute, IEEPA and the Administrative Procedure Act. Legion dropped the suit in early July 2026, after the controls were withdrawn, and no court ruled on the directive (Law360 via MLex). The case was No. 1:26-cv-02225, before Judge Richard J. Leon (Law360 case page).
  • March, supply-chain-risk designation. The Defense Secretary made a formal determination against Anthropic on March 3, 2026 under 41 U.S.C. § 4713 (D.C. Circuit opinion). The notice reached Anthropic on March 4, and a Department memo of March 6 ordered its products removed within 180 days (same opinion). Anthropic’s statement of March 5 says the letter cited 10 U.S.C. § 3252 (Anthropic).
  • August and September, court rulings on the designation. The Northern District of California set aside the § 3252 designation on August 27, 2026, as the D.C. Circuit’s opinion describes it. The D.C. Circuit upheld the § 4713 exclusion on September 25, 2026, by 2 to 1 (opinion).

Commentary

  • Export law. Greenberg Traurig calls the directive the first known export control action against a particular frontier model (client alert). Alex Zhao finds the directive’s legal basis novel and uncertain (Harvard Law Review Blog). Mayer Brown also reports that Commerce relied on 15 C.F.R. § 744.22 and 50 U.S.C. § 4817(b)(1) (client alert, June 30, 2026). The letter itself was not opened.
  • Defense Production Act. Alan Rozenshtein writes that Title I maps awkwardly onto AI. Kevin Frazier and Dean Ball doubt it supports mandatory pre-deployment vetting and call the Communications Act implausible (Cato). James Lewis of CSIS said the authorities could be clearer but a solution could probably be assembled from existing law (Fox News).
  • Negotiated conditions. Commerce lifted the controls after Anthropic made commitments; sources differ on which (The Next Web; Startup Fortune).
  • Authority to operate private facilities. Three statutes pair government operation of private facilities with compensation. They are Atomic Energy Act § 108, Communications Act § 706 and Military Selective Service Act § 18. The first covers licensed nuclear facilities once Congress declares war or a national emergency. The second covers communications stations after a presidential proclamation of war or emergency. The third lets the President take over a plant that refuses a defense order.
  • Across the families. Stop proposals need a power to halt domestic training, which no listed authority was written to give. Pace and Contain and Channel proposals can build on testing agreements and federal contracts. Continue positions rely on voluntary cooperation, as Frazier and Ball do.

Constitutional Constraints

The constitutional questions are open and mostly untested for frontier AI. No court has ruled on whether model weights or training decisions are speech, and no AI-specific takings analysis was found.

  • Speech: model weights. Alan Rozenshtein argues there is no general First Amendment right to distribute model weights (Lawfare, April 4, 2024). He reads Bernstein, Junger and Corley as protecting source code that people read, and treats weights as machine-readable parameters. A civil-society comment he cites argues the opposite by analogy to source code.
  • Speech: training choices. Rozenshtein writes that compelled retraining raises unsettled questions about whether training choices are protected editorial expression (Lawfare, February 25, 2026). Moody v. NetChoice recognized some algorithmic curation as protected, and the justices voiced doubts about its scope.
  • Speech: disclosure mandates. Bahrad Sokhansanj and Mackenzie Arnold conclude that laws such as California SB 53 remain viable and face more First Amendment risk than before (analysis). In xAI v. Bonta a district judge denied a preliminary injunction against California AB 2013 on March 4, 2026. The judge applied the stricter Central Hudson test and said xAI could prevail; an appeal is pending in the Ninth Circuit (No. 26-1591).
  • Speech: prior restraint. Alex Mark and Aaron Scher write that an administrative prior restraint must put on the government the burden of justifying a denial (arXiv 2509.05361, September 2025). They cite Freedman v. Maryland. They judge model algorithms likely protected speech and model weights likely unprotected. They treat limits on open release or API access as an administrative prior restraint.
  • Statutory speech limits. Export regulations exclude published information and fundamental research, and IEEPA exempts informational materials (CRS R46814; CRS R45618).
  • Major questions. The doctrine demands clear congressional authorization for agency action on questions of vast economic and political significance (CRS IF12077). In Learning Resources v. Trump, decided February 20, 2026, three justices applied it to presidential action under an emergency statute (opinion). Three concurring justices called it unnecessary, and three dissenters favored broader readings of national security delegations.
  • Due process. Anthropic said the June 2026 directive gave it hours to comply and rested on verbal evidence (Anthropic; NBC News). The FRONTIER Act would require written findings, notice where imminence allows, an expedited hearing with the burden on the Secretary and judicial review (section-by-section). The Sanders and Casar bill pairs charter revocation with appellate review and forfeiture of intellectual property and assets (bill text).
  • Takings. No analysis applying the Takings Clause to chips, weights or a training moratorium was found. CRS notes that courts have heard takings and due process challenges to IEEPA actions (CRS R45618). The Hardwired Pause working paper (October 2026, unpublished draft) proposes an insurance fund that pays book value for stranded model-specific chips (§§ 5 and 6).
  • Searches and inspections. The working paper points to U.S. rules implementing the Chemical Weapons Convention as a template (footnote 9). Those rules allow inspection by consent, by administrative warrant or by criminal warrant on probable cause. The Sanders and Casar bill makes full government access a charter condition, and the FRONTIER Act gives private verifiers unrestricted access.
  • Separation of powers and federalism. Executive Order 14365 directs the Justice Department to challenge state AI laws (White House). The GUARDRAILS Act (H.R. 8031) would declare that order void (Covington). No opened source treats nondelegation or the dormant Commerce Clause in depth.
  • Inferences for design. A statute that expressly authorizes a pause would answer the major questions objection that an executive order would invite. Limits on conduct, such as compute thresholds, sit on firmer speech ground under Rozenshtein’s analysis than rules dictating outputs. Both points are inferences, the speech point is contested, and no court has ruled.
  • Across the families. Stop proposals that strand chips or forfeit assets raise the takings question most directly. Pace proposals turn on the process attached to gates and emergency orders, and Contain and Channel proposals on inspection law. Continue positions invoke speech protections, as Executive Order 14365 does against state mandates on model outputs.

Preemption

  • Established. Congress has enacted no preemption of state AI law. The House passed a broad moratorium in May 2025, and the Senate stripped it 99 to 1 (Bullock).
  • Executive branch. Executive Order 14365 of December 11, 2025 directs suits against state AI laws and conditions some broadband funds (White House). The White House framework of March 20, 2026 would bar states from regulating AI development (Gibson Dunn).
  • Pending bills. The Great American AI Act draft would preempt state model-development laws for three years. The FRONTIER Act would bar new state frontier duties; the Sanders and Casar bill has no preemption clause.
  • Contested. In a July 23, 2026 statement, Americans for Responsible Innovation objects that FRONTIER’s preemption starts at once and has no sunset. It adds that drafting issues create a risk that the preemption is applied more broadly than intended (ARI). Charlie Bullock favors narrow preemption matched to the federal rules enacted (Bullock).
  • Across the families. The Sanders and Casar bill is the only Stop text found. FRONTIER and S. 2938 are Pace texts, and S. 2938 asks for options up to nationalization. The White House framework states the Continue position.

  • Emergency orders in FRONTIER. Section 8 would let the Secretary of Commerce suspend development, deployment or internal use on written findings of imminent catastrophic risk. Provisional orders expire after 45 days and final orders after 90 days, renewable in periods of up to 90 days on a new finding. At a hearing the Secretary must show that adequate evidence supports the order (§ 8(j)(2)). Section 8(l) makes the section the exclusive means for any federal actor, the President included, to restrict a frontier developer on those grounds (introduced text).
  • Shutdown in the Sanders and Casar bill. The Secretary must disconnect systems showing “superintelligence precursor characteristics” and disable them within 30 days unless the Secretary verifies that the characteristics are gone (bill text, § 10).
  • Access terms. Sanders and Casar charter holders must give full access to systems, processes, staff and physical infrastructure. S. 2938 requires code, training data, weights and architecture details on request. FRONTIER verifiers get unrestricted access and must refer imminent catastrophic risk within 72 hours.
  • Existing practice. CAISI already tests unreleased models, some in classified settings, under voluntary agreements (NIST/CAISI). Defense Production Act Title VII allows voluntary industry agreements with antitrust protection (CRS R43767). For prior art on embedded oversight, see the Two-Lane Plan: Full Design tab.
  • Who holds the gate. The Sanders and Casar bill gives a new department approval over every advanced model. S. 2938 conditions deployment on federal evaluation. FRONTIER leaves release decisions with developers outside an emergency order, the point ARI criticizes.
  • Thresholds. Bill texts use 1025 operations (Sanders and Casar) and 1026 operations (S. 2938 and FRONTIER). FRONTIER adds revenue and spending tiers.
  • Insurance. The Hardwired Pause working paper proposes mandatory insurance for operators of model-specific chips (footnote 76). It notes that nuclear plant operators must buy insurance as a license condition.
  • Not adopted. No pending bill opened uses “born secret” classification or secrecy orders on the Invention Secrecy Act model. The UN scientific panel reviewed aviation, nuclear and cybersecurity safety regimes and made no recommendation (thematic brief).
  • Established and contested. The bill provisions are established, from bill texts and, for FRONTIER, the introduced text and the sponsor’s section-by-section summary. The mapping to older regimes is a reading of those texts, and sponsors do not all name the analogies.
  • Across the families. Stop proposals pair a charter with approval before any deployment. Pace proposals use audits, evaluation gates and bounded emergency orders, and Contain and Channel proposals rely on embedded examiners. Continue positions favor sector regulators with no new agency, as the White House framework does (Gibson Dunn).

What the Polls Show

  • Slowing has broad support. The pause option led 48 to 31 in the Politico poll. In the CBS poll, 55 percent chose to slow down or stop development.
  • The tilt is partisan. Harris voters backed a pause 58 to 28, and Trump voters 44 to 40. In Pew’s data, Democratic concern rose from 46 percent in 2023 to 56 percent. Republican concern fell from 59 percent to 49 percent.
  • The China race also has majority support. Republicans back developing AI faster than China at 74 percent, and Democrats at 45 percent. Self-described liberals are at 36 percent (CBS News/YouGov topline).
  • Trust in institutions is low. In the CBS poll, 61 percent doubt AI companies will ensure appropriate use. Pew finds 67 percent lack confidence in government regulation, while CBS finds 63 percent think government is able to regulate. One question asks about confidence and the other about ability.

What the Polls Do Not Show

  • A single level of pause support. Wording moves the result. A two-statement choice yields 48 percent for a pause, and a four-way choice yields 12 percent for the stop option.
  • Support for a specific design. No poll recorded here tests a named bill, a deployment gate or a pause tied to the lead over China.
  • A trend. Polls on a pause from 2023 to 2025 were not opened: Not confirmed. Politico’s own page stayed blocked on October 7, 2026. Yahoo quotes the two statements at greater length than the working paper does, so the exact wording is unsettled.
  • A link to the U.S. network. The pollster Public First is a London firm unrelated to the U.S. political network of that name.
  • Across the families. The CBS options map loosely onto families: stop 12 percent (Stop), slow down 43 percent (Pace), keep pace or speed up 44 percent (Continue). No question recorded tests Contain and Channel designs.

Political Feasibility and Sequencing

Pause legislation looks unlikely to pass before the November 2026 midterms, on the evidence opened. Until then the federal venues are executive action and must-pass bills.

Three Legal Routes

  • Executive action. Existing tools reach export controls, voluntary CAISI testing, Defense Production Act reporting and procurement terms. Commentators doubt that they support a mandatory pause or gate. Executive Order 14409 of June 2, 2026 orders a voluntary framework for pre-release access and authorizes no mandatory licensing (see the Pace: Slowdowns and Gates tab; earlier reporting: Axios).
  • Statute. Pending bills treat a mandatory pause, a charter, an approval gate, audits and bounded emergency orders as matters for legislation. The Hardwired Pause working paper names an act of Congress or an executive order as routes to an urgent halt (footnote 28). It offers no legal analysis.
  • Treaty or agreement. A pause that binds China needs an international agreement, and the working paper expects informal steps first (§ 3). James N. Baker adds that agreements need domestic implementing law (survey).

What Two Proposals Would Need

  • Two-Lane Plan (Brad Carson, unpublished, October 2026). Executive action could supply chip controls, CAISI testing and federal contracts. Mandatory containment, resident examiners, the deployment gate, the agent registry and the liability regime would likely need a statute. No treaty is required by design, and authority for the presidential “adversary finding” is unanalyzed.
  • Strongest objections to that route. The gate puts the burden of proof on developers, where FRONTIER § 8 puts it on the Secretary. An executive order version would meet the major questions objection, and takings and speech claims are untested for AI. The President and the Speaker reject a slowdown (NPR via MPR News).
  • Loss-of-Control Tripwire Bill (Brad Carson, unpublished, October 2026). It requires a statute. The text would need definitions of its three tripwires, a fact-finder and procedures for the rebuttable presumption. The nearest pending text is the list of “superintelligence precursor characteristics” in the Sanders and Casar bill (bill text).
  • Strongest objections to that route. FRONTIER’s exclusive-mechanism clause would limit a parallel federal route if enacted first (section-by-section). No opened source tests a presumption against the developer under due process. The three main bills share no sponsors, which leaves no ready vehicle.
