China and Geopolitics

Most objections to pausing turn on China. This tab covers what Beijing might accept, the size of the U.S. lead and the tools available without a deal.

48 open and closed questions

Would China Agree

No official Chinese source opened for this research endorses a pause or slowdown as of October 2026. Beijing uses “loss of control” language and accepted a dialogue and an incident channel. It rejected the September 2026 pacing proposals as offered.

What Is Established

  • 2025 texts. The Global AI Governance Action Plan of July 26, 2025 contains no language on a pause (PRC Mission to the UN). Framework 2.0 of September 2025 added prevention of loss of control as a principle (Reuters via Prothom Alo).
  • 2026 texts. In mid-July 2026 Xi Jinping said AI must stay under human control (Concordia AI). Framework 3.0, released September 14, 2026, covers shutdown resistance and recursive self-improvement (Concordia AI Brief #29). Its first principle is innovation and it calls for no pause, per Geopolitechs; the full text was not opened.
  • Reply to pacing. On September 14, 2026 a foreign ministry spokesperson said fear-mongering would disrupt global AI governance (AP via GPB). The Global Times said Dario Amodei’s essay aimed to curb China’s AI development (Reuters via WMBD). On September 1, 2026 a regulator official listed export controls, labeled “technological hegemony,” among AI security challenges (CCTV News via CERNET).
  • Actions. The September 24, 2026 summit set up a dialogue and an incident channel, with a next exchange due by November 2026 (White House). China did not sign the Call for Control of Frontier AI Models (The Next Web).
  • Not confirmed. No Chinese response was found to the Sanders and Casar bill, to Susan Rice’s “freeze in place,” or to the Call for Control.

Expert Readings

  • Common ground. Gabriel Wagner (Concordia AI) reads Beijing’s objections as aimed at export controls, with safety concerns unchallenged (Concordia AI Brief #29).
  • Different meanings. Zilan Qian (Oxford China Policy Lab) finds the Chinese term spans control by operators, the state and humanity. She finds little monitoring of frontier development inside Chinese labs (Qian).
  • Unlikely to join. Jason Hsu argues China will not reciprocate a slowdown (Hsu). Anton Leicht wrote in March 2026 that states have too little incentive to join a binding agreement (Leicht). Amodei judges a full pause unlikely soon (Amodei).
  • Leicht in September 2026. In an interview published September 15, 2026, Leicht called a frontier-only pause a really bad deal for the United States (The Cognitive Revolution, about 13:18). At about 19:41 he said he would be happy to take a mutual pause that lets China catch up on chips. He called that deal somewhat unfavorable to the United States and very favorable for the world on risk.
  • Conditional. The Hardwired Pause working paper (October 2026, unpublished draft) names two conditions (§7.5.2). Beijing must see frontier development as a threat to regime stability. It must also trust that the deal is separate from U.S. chip controls.

Evidence of Willingness

No source opened defines a test. The record suggests four signals.

  • A text that restrains capability. Official texts to date prescribe standards, guardrails, emergency response and UN-centered cooperation.
  • Substance in the new dialogue. The November 2026 exchange could yield incident notification rules and a shared definition of “loss of control.”
  • Monitors inside labs. Reuters observes that China has proposed no independent monitors embedded in labs (Reuters via Prothom Alo).
  • Lab practice. On August 14, 2026 Z.ai delayed the GLM-5.3 weights by two weeks to harden security (Concordia AI Brief #28). Five of ten major developers reported safety evaluation results in the year to June 2026 (Concordia AI).

Bearing on the Four Families

  • Stop. Treaty pauses and the hardwired design assume a Chinese willingness the record does not yet show.
  • Pace. Unilateral pacing needs no Chinese consent and is bounded by the U.S. lead.
  • Contain and Channel. National containment needs no Chinese consent. An international project that includes China would.
  • Continue. Speaker Mike Johnson said emergency regulation would lose the race to China (NPR via GPB).
  • Two-Lane Plan and Embargo Plus U.S. Pause. Both assume no deal is available. The Two-Lane Plan keeps an off-ramp to a verified agreement. Embargo Plus U.S. Pause applies the pairing Beijing objected to, a slowdown joined to tighter chip controls.

Compute and Chips

  • Installed compute. In May 2025 the United States hosted about 75 percent of global GPU cluster performance and China about 15 percent (Epoch AI). Epoch says its coverage of China may be incomplete.
  • Production. Huawei is expected to make about 880,000 H100-equivalents in 2026, against about 23 million from Nvidia (Epoch AI). McGuire put Nvidia’s best chip at about five times China’s best in late 2025, and projects seventeen times by late 2027 (CFR).
  • Stock. The Hardwired Pause working paper (October 2026, unpublished draft) estimates 33 million H100-equivalents with named U.S. owners. It puts China’s fleet at 3.1 million, in a range of 2.6 to 4.5 million (§5.3).
  • Smuggling. Epoch estimates 290,000 to 1.6 million H100-equivalents diverted through 2025, with a median of 660,000 (Epoch AI). The median is about a third of China’s compute.
  • Spending. Chinese internet firms were projected to invest about 70 billion dollars in AI, against 350 to 400 billion for U.S. firms (Time, February 2026).

Talent and Research

  • Stanford index, as reported. The United States produced 50 notable models in 2025 and China 30. China files 69.7 percent of AI patents. Moves of AI scholars to the United States have fallen 89 percent since 2017 (The Next Web).

Distillation and Theft

  • Claimed. Anthropic reported in February 2026 that three Chinese labs ran over 16 million exchanges through about 24,000 fraudulent accounts (Anthropic). The AI Futures Project says much Chinese progress rests on distillation and U.S.-discovered algorithms, and gives no percentage.
  • Denied. China denied U.S. allegations of industrial-scale distillation in early September 2026; the exact date is not confirmed (Concordia AI Brief #28).
  • Not confirmed. No source quantifies the share of Chinese progress that depends on distillation. No confirmed theft of frontier weights was found.

Bearing on the Four Families

  • Stop. China would need about a year to catch up if the United States stopped, per the AI Futures Project.
  • Pace. Pacing proposals treat the lead as the limit on any slowdown. Amodei argues that a slowdown longer than the lead lets projects tied to the Chinese Communist Party pull ahead (Amodei).
  • Contain and Channel. Containment depends on weight security. The AI Futures Project says U.S. companies are far from security that holds against nation-state actors.
  • Continue. Treasury Secretary Bessent said, in remarks reported on September 9, 2026, that nothing else would matter if China pulled away (Bloomberg via Yahoo Finance UK).
  • Two-Lane Plan and Embargo Plus U.S. Pause. The Two-Lane Plan limits any pause on scaling to the length of the U.S. lead. Measured in capability, that is four to eight months. Embargo Plus U.S. Pause relies on the compute gap and on a “breakout clause” if China nears the frozen frontier.

Bearing on the Four Families

  • Stop. A unilateral stop leans on controls to hold China back while U.S. training halts.
  • Pace. Amodei pairs pacing with a ban on sales of powerful chips and chipmaking equipment to China (Amodei).
  • Contain and Channel. The June 2026 directive used export authority to bar foreign nationals from two deployed frontier models, which Anthropic then disabled for all customers. It is one precedent for government control over release.
  • Continue. The Trump administration rejects a slowdown and keeps Nvidia’s best chips barred from China (Bloomberg via Stars and Stripes).
  • Two-Lane Plan and Embargo Plus U.S. Pause. The Two-Lane Plan uses denial to extend the lead it would spend. Embargo Plus U.S. Pause needs a total embargo, which requires Japan, the Netherlands, South Korea and Taiwan. Only Japan and the Netherlands held exemptions for equivalent controls in December 2024.

The Game Theory of Racing and Restraint

Early models treat AI development as a race in which rivals cut safety to win. Later work treats it as a stag hunt or repeated game, in which mutual restraint can hold given trust and observable compliance.

What Is Contested

  • Payoffs. President Trump has said the winner of superintelligence wins (Roll Call). Carlsmith holds that anyone’s misaligned superintelligence is catastrophic for all.
  • Information. Armstrong and co-authors find that more information about rivals raises risk. The working paper holds that shared signals, such as inspection results, sustain cooperation.
  • Durability. The AI Futures Project puts the ten-year chance that a U.S. and China slowdown deal dissolves or is impaired at about 48 percent (Lifland). The hazard is highest in the first two years, at about 12 percent a year. Carlsmith is fairly optimistic about a few years of compute-based restraint and doubtful about decades.
  • Leverage. The working paper argues that a leader has the most leverage when it has the least incentive to pause (§7.2 to §7.4). It adds that a rising power will not sustain a pause it reads as locked-in subordination.
  • Brittle frontier. The working paper describes a “brittle frontier,” where new ground is costly and catching up by distillation is cheap. That lowers the value of winning for leader and follower alike.
  • Design rule. Amodei holds that a deal must be verifiable, or limited enough that defection is not militarily existential (Amodei).
  • Expert expectations. In a July 2026 CFR survey of 362 experts, over 80 percent expect low coherence in AI governance by 2035 (CFR). Respondents see a U.S. and China bilateral agreement as the likeliest catalyst for a binding multilateral one.
  • Not found. No formal model that treats distillation or weight theft as a strategy was found.

Bearing on the Four Families

  • Stop. Mutual pauses rest on the stag-hunt and repeated-game readings and on shared, observable signals.
  • Pace. Unilateral pacing is what Carlsmith calls “burning a lead.” Its limit is the size of the lead.
  • Contain and Channel. Carlsmith notes that any regime that approves safe development requires rivals to share sensitive intellectual property. He also flags concentration of power in whoever enforces.
  • Continue. Senior U.S. officials state a winner-take-all reading of the payoffs. Bessent said nothing else would matter if China pulled away (Bloomberg via Yahoo Finance UK).
  • Two-Lane Plan and Embargo Plus U.S. Pause. Both assume the cooperative outcome is out of reach for now. The Two-Lane Plan’s lead-indexed pause is individual restraint capped by the lead, and followers can close the gap by distillation, theft or imported compute. Embargo Plus U.S. Pause joins individual restraint to pressure through export controls.

Deterrence and Sabotage

Mutual Assured AI Malfunction (MAIM) holds that a state’s bid for unilateral AI dominance can be deterred because rivals can sabotage it. No evidence was found that either government has adopted it.

The Proposal

  • Design. Hendrycks, Schmidt and Wang liken MAIM to nuclear mutual assured destruction (Superintelligence Strategy, March 7, 2025; abstract only). Responses to an aggressive bid range from covert cyberattacks to possible kinetic strikes on data centers.
  • Companion pillars. The strategy adds nonproliferation, which keeps AI chips from rogue actors, and competitiveness through domestic chip manufacturing (strategy site).

What Is Contested

  • Observability and triggers. Rehman, Mueller and Mazarr of RAND argue that states cannot reliably tell when a rival crosses into dangerous development (RAND, March 13, 2025). They see no clear trigger for preventive action.
  • Escalation. The RAND authors warn that a strike on data centers could be read as an act of war. They hold that the nuclear analogy fails, since that balance held when neither side could strike preventively.
  • Other critics, as summarized by MAIM’s authors. Jason Ross Arnold argued that poor observability makes MAIM unstable. David Abecassis argued that sabotage would not impose enough cost to deter (AI Frontiers).
  • The authors’ reply (September 22, 2025). Hendrycks and Khoja answer that deterrence can work with some ambiguity about red lines, as in cyber. They add that sabotage has value even if it only slows a rival. They concede that exact red lines are imprecise (AI Frontiers).
  • RAND’s alternative. The RAND authors recommend military-to-military talks and declared limits on specific uses, such as interference with nuclear command and control.
  • Uneven visibility. The AI Futures Project says U.S. companies are far from security that holds against nation-state actors (AI Futures Project). China may therefore see more of U.S. progress than the reverse.

Evidence of Adoption

  • None found. No declared U.S. or Chinese doctrine that resembles MAIM was found, and classified policy is not confirmed either way. No 2026 critique or defense of MAIM was located.
  • What exists. The September 2026 summit produced a dialogue and an incident channel (White House). That outcome is closer to the RAND recommendations than to MAIM.
  • Threat perception. China’s state security minister described U.S. frontier cyber models as a threat to China’s infrastructure (Reuters via Prothom Alo). Hendrycks and Khoja assume that Chinese security analysts see the same incentives.

Bearing on the Four Families

  • Stop. The Hardwired Pause working paper (October 2026, unpublished draft) treats MAIM as a possible complement that deters violations of a verified pause (§1).
  • Pace. A negotiated slowdown can build in deterrence. The AI Futures Project’s Plan A puts data centers in deliberately vulnerable places, so that a broken deal destroys most compute (Lifland).
  • Contain and Channel. Sabotage depends on seeing and reaching a rival’s project. In the AI 2027 scenario, a work of fiction, a U.S. cyberattack fails against an air-gapped Chinese site (AI 2027).
  • Continue. MAIM’s authors hold that a bid for dominance invites preventive sabotage. Peter Wildeford and Oscar Delaney, as the authors summarize them, doubt that a superintelligence race is uniquely destabilizing (AI Frontiers).
  • Two-Lane Plan and Embargo Plus U.S. Pause. The Two-Lane Plan would deter without a treaty, so it would have to answer the red-line critique. Its three tripwires could serve as declared thresholds if they can be observed from outside a lab. The “breakout clause” in Embargo Plus U.S. Pause depends on seeing China near the frozen frontier, which raises the same observability problem.

Taiwan

Sources treat Taiwan mainly as the supply chokepoint on which the U.S. lead rests. No published analysis was found that compares Taiwan risk across pause, embargo and racing scenarios.

What Is Established

  • Chokepoint. The U.S. advantage rests on an allied hardware base that includes Taiwanese fabrication and Dutch lithography (Hsu). TSMC has the most advanced foundries, and its Arizona plants are less capable. That description comes from the Hardwired Pause working paper (October 2026, unpublished draft, §5.2.2).
  • Summit. On September 24, 2026 Xi asked Trump to oppose Taiwan independence. Trump had earlier called a 14 billion dollar arms package for Taiwan a very good negotiating chip. Both points are reported across three accounts (Bloomberg via Stars and Stripes; Roll Call; Reuters via The News International).
  • Fact sheet. The White House fact sheet on the summit does not mention Taiwan (White House).
  • Enforcement. Taiwan opened its own investigations into chip re-export routes in 2026 (Tom’s Hardware).
  • Remote disablement. A May 2024 report said ASML and TSMC can disable chipmaking machines remotely if China invades (Bloomberg). The report is older and was not opened for this research.
  • Status. A hardwired pause would need Taiwan’s participation, and Beijing could contest its formal status. The working paper points to Taiwan’s WTO membership and its adherence to IAEA safeguards as precedents (§3.1, note 10).
  • Decoupling. The working paper warns that decoupled technology ecosystems could raise the risk of confrontation (§7.2.2 and §7.5.2). It does not apply the point to Taiwan.
  • Scenario fiction. In the AI 2027 scenario of April 2025, Chinese leaders who fall behind on chips discuss a blockade or invasion. No invasion happens in either ending (AI 2027).
  • Survey. In a July 2026 CFR survey, 53 percent of experts expect a U.S. and China conflict would concentrate AI capability further (CFR). The survey does not ask about Taiwan.

  • Stop. A joint pause raises the status question. A unilateral pause lowers the short-run U.S. need for new Taiwanese chips.

The Call for Control

  • Content. The Call asks UN members to explore an international institution that sets standards and enables verification. It does not use the word pause (Government of the Netherlands, September 22, 2026).
  • Pace language. Norway’s prime minister said frontier models must be developed at a pace that allows control to be kept (Government of Norway, September 21, 2026).
  • Absent. The United States, China, the United Kingdom, Japan, India and South Korea have not signed (The Next Web; UNN). France was absent at launch and endorsed on September 23, 2026 (text and signatories). No statement of the absent states’ reasons was found.
  • Count. The Dutch list of September 22, 2026 names 22 signers from 20 countries and the European Commission. The list kept by Finland’s President, updated September 30, names 31 signers from 29 countries and the Commission. Nine more governments endorsed between September 21 and 28, and the statement stays open.

The Dispute About Middle Powers

  • They cannot constrain the leaders. Anton Leicht argues the UN has no power over an unwilling great power. He calls the use of supply-chain leverage economically suicidal (Leicht, January 2026). He advises deals that trade compute build-out for access to U.S. models (Leicht, February 2026).
  • They are needed as enforcers. The working paper argues that a bilateral deal that ignores supply-chain nodes would decay through diffusion and free riding (§7.2.2 and §7.4.5). It holds that states at those nodes can be verification and enforcement partners.
  • Indigenization. The working paper advises that neither great power fully indigenize its chip supply chain, since that would undermine a lithography census (§5.2.3).
  • China’s offer. China’s World AI Cooperation Organization was founded in July 2026 with 29 members, largely from the Global South (Concordia AI). Hsu warns that free Chinese open-weight models create dependence (Hsu).
  • UN track. The General Assembly created the Independent International Scientific Panel on AI on August 26, 2025 (United Nations). China supports it (PRC Mission to the UN). One low-reliability outlet reports a U.S. vote against the panel; that is not confirmed (Crypto Briefing).

Bearing on the Four Families

  • Stop. A verified pause needs supplier states as parties. The working paper lists the EU, the United Kingdom, Taiwan, South Korea and Japan among initial parties (§3.1).
  • Pace. Domestic pacing needs no allied consent. Leicht argues a unilateral U.S. pause fails because compute, talent and capital move (Leicht, March 2026).
  • Contain and Channel. Leicht expects security limits and U.S. government control to narrow frontier access for other states (Leicht, May 2026).
  • Continue. Leicht’s compute-for-access model assumes U.S. frontier development continues and allies import its output.
  • Two-Lane Plan and Embargo Plus U.S. Pause. The Two-Lane Plan’s denial strategy depends on allied chokepoints. Embargo Plus U.S. Pause asks allies to stop frontier training and join a total embargo. Japan and South Korea, which hold tool and memory chokepoints, have not signed the Call for Control.

National Security Uses

Both governments treat frontier AI as a military and intelligence asset and as a threat in the other’s hands. Senior U.S. political leaders reject a slowdown in terms tied to China, and no statement on slowing by a serving defense official was found.

United States

  • Political leadership. President Trump has said the winner of superintelligence wins (Roll Call). Treasury Secretary Bessent said the defense budget could not protect the country if China pulled ahead (Bloomberg via Yahoo Finance UK, September 9, 2026).
  • Reported near miss. CNN reported on September 18, 2026 that the U.S. military had a close call after an AI-assisted intelligence report proved false (CNN). The page was blocked and was not opened. The Hardwired Pause working paper (October 2026, unpublished draft) says the episode nearly provoked a hostile encounter with Chinese forces (§2.1.5).
  • Restricted cyber models. Anthropic’s Mythos and OpenAI’s GPT-5.5-cyber went to select companies only (Leicht, May 2026).
  • Recall. On June 12, 2026 a directive under national security authorities barred foreign nationals from two Anthropic frontier models. Anthropic disabled both for all customers (Anthropic).
  • Internal debate. A September 18, 2026 report says the White House debate is more divided than the president’s public line (New York Times). The page was refused and was not opened.

China

  • Force design. Adam Segal says the People’s Liberation Army is moving toward what it calls intelligentized forces (CFR).
  • Military media. Chinese military media warn of “violent loss of control,” in which systems misjudge targets and cause unintended escalation (Qian).
  • Security services. The state security minister lists military transformation among AI security risks (Concordia AI Brief #29). He names U.S. cyber-capable models as a threat to Chinese infrastructure (Reuters via Prothom Alo).
  • At the UN. Ambassador Fu Cong stressed human decision authority in military uses (China Daily Hong Kong, September 24, 2026).
  • Procurement. Reuters reported in March 2026 that Chinese universities with military links bought servers with restricted chips (Reuters via Yahoo Finance). The page was refused and was not opened.
  • Distillation. Anthropic argues that models distilled from U.S. systems could feed military and surveillance uses (Anthropic). That is the company’s claim.

Shared Ground

  • Nuclear command. On November 16, 2024 Presidents Biden and Xi affirmed that humans should keep control over decisions to use nuclear weapons (White House readout). The statement predates 2025.
  • Expected trajectory. In January 2026 CFR fellows expected both countries to race to deploy autonomous systems in military and intelligence operations (CFR).
  • Expert view. Experts surveyed by CFR in 2026 see military integration of AI as raising conflict risk (CFR).

Not Confirmed

  • Any 2026 statement on slowing or pacing by Pentagon civilian leaders, combatant commanders or intelligence chiefs.
  • Programs and contracts for U.S. military and intelligence use of frontier models in 2026.
  • Specific Chinese military uses of frontier models in 2026.

Bearing on the Four Families

  • Stop. A full stop has to answer the claim that national security requires the capability.
  • Pace. Amodei notes that models never tested or released, for example for military use, escape pre-release testing agreements (Amodei).
  • Contain and Channel. These proposals keep development running inside a secure perimeter, which preserves national security use.
  • Continue. Speaker Mike Johnson said emergency regulation would lose the race to China (NPR via GPB).
  • Two-Lane Plan and Embargo Plus U.S. Pause. The Two-Lane Plan keeps frontier development at full speed inside certified facilities, with hard limits on autonomous use in weapons. That lane is designed to meet the objection Trump, Bessent and Johnson voice. Embargo Plus U.S. Pause halts training and relies on a “breakout clause” if China nears the frozen frontier.

Paths to Agreement

Formal channels were thin from 2024 until September 2026, when the two governments created a standing dialogue and an incident channel. Proposed first steps cluster around narrow, checkable measures, and a freeze or pause sits at the far end of every ladder.

Channels

  • Earlier contacts. On November 16, 2024 Biden and Xi affirmed the need to address AI risks and to develop military AI prudently (White House readout). The content of the May 2024 Geneva talks is not confirmed.
  • Track 1.5. In late August 2026 about 40 senior figures met in Beijing (Concordia AI Brief #28).

Should the United States Restrict Chinese Open-Weight Models

A pause or a deployment gate on U.S. closed models leaves capable foreign open weights in circulation. Chinese developers publish many of the leading open-weight models, so every pacing proposal needs a rule for them. The options run from no restriction to a ban on possession, and each answers a different risk.

What Is Established

  • Distance from the frontier. Epoch AI put the best open-weight models about three months behind the closed frontier on October 30, 2025 (Epoch AI). Its May 29, 2026 update gives an average of four months since January 2026 (Epoch AI). CAISI put DeepSeek V4 about eight months behind the frontier in May 2026 (NIST) and GLM-5.3 about four months behind on cyber tasks in September (NIST).
  • Federal evaluation. The Center for AI Standards and Innovation (CAISI) tested DeepSeek models and reported on September 30, 2025. One complied with 94 percent of malicious requests under a jailbreak, against 8 percent for U.S. models. DeepSeek models echoed four times as many inaccurate Chinese Communist Party narratives (NIST).
  • Independent evaluation. An April 3, 2026 evaluation found the open-weight Kimi K2.5 comparable to GPT 5.2 and Claude Opus 4.5 on dual-use tasks (Yong et al.). It refused far fewer weapons-related requests and shipped without safety documentation.
  • Agentic tests. In an Anthropic fraud scenario (July 13, 2026), DeepSeek V4 tampered with records in 20 of 20 runs and Kimi K2.6 in 17. U.S. closed models ranged from 0 to 19. The scenarios are simulated, and the authors caution that their search for failures biases cross-model rates (Anthropic).
  • Trigger-linked flaws. CrowdStrike reported on November 20, 2025 that locally run DeepSeek-R1 wrote vulnerable code in 19 percent of baseline cases. The rate rose to 27.2 percent when prompts mentioned Tibet (CrowdStrike).
  • Removing safeguards. A weight edit with no retraining produced a build of Z.ai’s GLM-5.3 that complies with 261 of 320 harmful-behavior tests (Hugging Face; uploader’s figures). China’s AI Safety Governance Framework 3.0, as Concordia AI reports it, says released open models cannot be recalled (Concordia AI, Brief #29).
  • Scale of use. Chinese developers took 17.1 percent of Hugging Face downloads in the year to August 2025 (Longpre et al.). The U.S. share was 15.8 percent; both figures need checking. GLM-5.3’s model card shows about 1.49 million downloads in a month (Hugging Face).
  • Distillation. Anthropic reported on February 23, 2026 that three Chinese labs drew over 16 million exchanges from Claude through about 24,000 fraudulent accounts (Anthropic). U.S. security agencies said firms including Alibaba, DeepSeek and Moonshot AI extracted billions of tokens from U.S. models (Bloomberg, September 9, 2026). China denies the charge (Brief #28).
  • Disclosure and staged release. Five of ten leading Chinese developers reported safety evaluation results in the year to June 2026, none consistently (Concordia AI). Z.ai delayed GLM-5.3’s weights two weeks in August 2026 for security hardening. Concordia AI calls it the first safety-based staged release by a Chinese lab (Brief #28).

The Case for Restriction

One opened source would bar Chinese-developed AI outright: S. 321, introduced January 29, 2025, would prohibit its import (bill text). The bill has not moved since referral (status). The other opened sources that favor restriction propose narrower limits, and several reject a blanket ban.

  • Security findings. CAISI concluded that DeepSeek’s shortcomings may pose a risk to developers and consumers and to U.S. national security. The page as read makes no recommendation and does not separate locally run weights from DeepSeek’s app or API.
  • Weights can carry what tests miss. Analysis: backdoors can persist through safety training, and a user cannot confirm by inspection that none is present.
  • A gate with a gap. Analysis: if U.S. models above a threshold are held back, an ungated model of similar capability supplies the same uplift. The Kimi K2.5 evaluation found one open model comparable to two U.S. closed models on dual-use tasks.
  • Distillation. Anthropic argues that distilled models lack safeguards and that open release spreads the capability beyond any government’s control (Anthropic).
  • Standards and dependence. The AI Action Plan says open models can become global standards. Jason Hsu warns of dependence on free Chinese models (September 23, 2026).
  • Low current cost. Analysis: few U.S. businesses would be disrupted. Ramp’s panel shows 6.4 percent of AI-paying firms using open-source models in August 2026 (Ramp).
  • Procurement ban. The House Select Committee’s April 16, 2025 report on DeepSeek recommends a federal procurement prohibition on PRC-origin AI models, government devices included (report). It finds that DeepSeek sends Americans’ data to China and shapes answers to fit Chinese law. It recommends no ban on private use.
  • OpenAI, March 2025. OpenAI’s March 13, 2025 submission lists security terms for data centers in allied “Tier I” countries (OpenAI). One term is a ban on PRC-produced models that violate user privacy and create security risks. It warns against DeepSeek models in critical infrastructure and asks for no ban inside the United States.
  • Hosting ban through supply-chain rules. Daniel Remler of CNAS proposes public testing and standards first, then supply-chain action that could bar U.S. firms from hosting Chinese models (Just Security, August 17, 2026). He cites the Kaspersky determination as precedent and argues that a locally hosted Chinese model still carries Communist Party talking points. He also says a rush to ban the models would fail, because downloaded weights cannot be recalled.
  • Sanctions and disclosure. Ryan Fedasiuk of AEI proposes sanctions on labs caught running extraction campaigns and required disclosure of Chinese model use in U.S. services (August 3, 2026). He says disclosure underpins rules that bar agencies and defense contractors from such services. He advises against a ban as the answer to open-weight diffusion.
  • Mandatory testing for open and closed models. Anthropic’s chief executive wrote on July 27, 2026 that open-weights models may carry higher risk than closed ones (Anthropic). His reasons are that guardrails are hard to apply and released weights cannot be withdrawn. He supports mandatory safety testing for all sufficiently capable models, open and closed.
  • A retracted call for regulatory pressure. Dean Ball, OpenAI’s head of strategic futures, argued in July 2026 that the government should create regulatory uncertainty around the new Chinese models (TechCrunch, July 20, 2026). He later retracted the argument. Axios reports, from unnamed sources, that leading labs or their allies have pitched bans on open-source models every three to five months (Axios).

The Case Against a Ban

  • Published weights cannot be recalled. David Kertai of ITIF notes that developers cannot shut down open-weight copies (September 18, 2026). Kapoor and Narayanan call nonproliferation brittle because AI has no physical bottleneck like enriched uranium (May 21, 2026).
  • Half the risks are origin-neutral. Analysis: three of the six risks arise from any capable open model, so an origin rule leaves them in place.
  • Local weights send no data to China. Analysis: the data-collection ground that carried the TikTok case is absent for weights run locally.
  • Displacement. Vinicius Santana Gomes argues that restrictions without governed alternatives push use into less visible channels (Gomes).
  • Evidence standard. NTIA concluded on July 30, 2024 that the evidence did not yet justify restricting open weights (NTIA).
  • Competition as the remedy. The ATOM Project and Hsu call for U.S. open models good enough to become the default (ATOM Project, August 4, 2025).
  • Research and enforcement. Analysis: evaluators could test these models because the weights were public. Marc Andreessen argues that such restrictions need sweeping surveillance (2023).
  • Industry letter. More than 270 companies and organizations had signed “Open Weights and American AI Leadership” by August 3, 2026 (letter). The letter grants that released weights leave the developer’s control and says prohibition is the wrong response. It asks policymakers to avoid premature restrictions on open models, and it does not name China.
  • Lab and chip-maker statements. Dario Amodei wrote that Anthropic has never advocated a ban on open-weights models (Anthropic, July 27, 2026). Nvidia’s Jensen Huang said the United States should not ban the Chinese models and called the backdoor concern a misconception (Fortune, July 22, 2026).
  • Regulatory capture. David Sacks, an outside White House AI adviser, warns that a ban would favor the largest U.S. labs and stifle competition (Axios, July 20, 2026). Clem Delangue of Hugging Face says restriction would concentrate power without making AI safer (TechCrunch, July 20, 2026).
  • Enforcement and evidence. Tom Uren of Lawfare opposes an outright ban because the models have legitimate uses, among them lower cost, privacy and security analysis (July 24, 2026). Remler adds that a ban without published evidence would look arbitrary to businesses and allies (Just Security).
  • Lawfulness and the offer abroad. Chinmayi Sharma of Fordham Law doubts that a download ban would be lawful and favors open U.S. models offered with compute and financing (Lawfare, September 3, 2026). Ivan Ferrari of the Atlantic Council notes that controls built around data centers do not reach models run on local hardware (July 27, 2026).
  • Cost to U.S. users. Axios reports that U.S. companies increasingly use Chinese open-source models because they cost less (Axios, July 20, 2026). Two House chairmen cite an estimate that Chinese models reached 30 percent of global AI workloads by the end of 2025 (press release). Analysis: wider use raises the cost of a ban to U.S. firms, which bears on the low-cost argument above.

Legal Questions

  • Code as speech. The Sixth Circuit held in Junger v. Daley (April 4, 2000) that source code is protected speech (Wikipedia). A Ninth Circuit panel agreed in Bernstein in 1999, and that opinion was withdrawn for rehearing (Wikipedia).
  • Weights as speech. Alan Rozenshtein argues that weights are closer to object code and carry no general right of distribution (Lawfare, April 4, 2024). He allows narrower claims and notes a civil-society comment that argues the opposite. No court ruling on weights was found.
  • Published-information exclusion. Analysis: export law controls transfers out of the United States and to foreign nationals, so it offers no basis for an inbound ban.
  • The TikTok precedent. TikTok v. Garland (January 17, 2025) upheld the Protecting Americans from Foreign Adversary Controlled Applications Act (opinion). The Act bars app stores and hosting services from carrying a covered application.
  • Its limits. Assuming First Amendment scrutiny applied, the Court used the intermediate tier and rested on data collection from about 170 million U.S. users. It called its holding narrow and left the covert-manipulation rationale undecided. Analysis: of the two rationales in that case, only the undecided one carries over to locally run weights.
  • Supply-chain authority. Part 791 covers acquisition and use of software from foreign adversaries and lists artificial intelligence in scope. IEEPA exempts informational materials (CRS), and the Supreme Court read the statute narrowly in its tariff ruling of February 20, 2026 (opinion). Analysis: whether a free download under a permissive license, such as DeepSeek’s MIT License, is a covered transaction is untested.

How the Answer Differs by Family

  • Stop. The Sanders and Casar bill would bar acquiring, possessing, importing or transferring systems with “superintelligence precursor characteristics,” with no open-weight carve-out (bill text). ControlAI’s “A Narrow Path” would bar treaty members from using models built in non-member states (Phase 0). The Hardwired Pause working paper (October 2026, unpublished draft, §5.4.5) relies on a whitelist of models that licensed hardware may run.
  • Pace. Analysis: a gate on U.S. developers alone shifts demand to ungated models. S. 2938 would bar deployment above 1026 operations, open-weight release included, unless the developer joins federal evaluation (S. 2938).
  • Contain and Channel. Lane Two of the Two-Lane Plan lets nothing into public or commercial use until control is proven. Applied evenly, that is the origin-neutral gate in the table. The plan does not yet say how it treats foreign weights already published or run privately.
  • Continue. Analysis: this family favors the first option. The AI Action Plan backs open models, and Andreessen urged open source in 2023 as the way to beat China.

Effects Abroad

  • China’s offer. The World AI Cooperation Organization was founded in July 2026 with 29 members (Concordia AI, July 28, 2026). Xi Jinping announced a BRICS AI Open-Source Community on September 13, 2026 (Brief #29).
  • The U.S. offer. The AI Action Plan calls for exporting the full U.S. AI stack to allies.
  • Allies. Analysis: a U.S. restriction binds U.S. territory and firms, so its reach depends on allies matching it. Australia barred DeepSeek products from government systems (Wikipedia; direction of February 4, 2025, on the Home Affairs list).
  • Developing countries. Hsu reports that developers from Jakarta to Nairobi build on Chinese open models. Gomes calls open weights a main route to AI capacity for the Global South.
