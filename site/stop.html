Stop: Pauses and Bans

The stop family halts or prohibits frontier development outright. Its members differ on who is bound, what is stopped and how compliance is checked.

11 entries · 196 open and closed questions

Overview

Stop proposals share one premise: frontier development should halt until a stated safety condition is met. Most go further and reject building superintelligence until control is demonstrated.

The other families start elsewhere. Pace proposals assume superintelligence will be built and manage the approach. Contain and Channel proposals keep development going under state or international control, and Continue positions reject both a pause and a slower pace.

What the Family Shares

  • A common line. The drafts converge on 1024 to 1025 FLOP of training compute. MIRI uses 1024, and the Hardwired Pause working paper (October 2026, unpublished draft) uses 2×1024. A Narrow Path and the Sanders and Casar bill use 1025, and every line sits below that paper’s estimate of the current frontier.
  • Inference continues. Each line rolls back new training, and most designs leave existing models in service.
  • Hardware is the checkable base. The treaty designs verify through chips, on the premise that compute is the one part of AI development that can be counted (PauseAI). The two public statements name no mechanism, and the two bills rely on access to labs.
  • Thin economics. Most proposals say little about the economy. None of the published ones reviewed here gives the public an equity stake in the developers.

Where Its Members Differ

  • Specificity. One-sentence statements, statute text, treaty-scale designs and short essays sit side by side.
  • Who can start. The two bills, a unilateral domestic pause and Embargo Plus U.S. Pause can begin without China. Most treaty-scale designs covered here name agreement between the United States and China as a precondition.
  • The banned object. The 2023 to 2025 drafts use compute thresholds. The 2026 bills define superintelligence by function and add lists of precursor capabilities.
  • Duration. Six months in the 2023 letter, about ten years (renewable) in the Hardwired Pause, 20 years in A Narrow Path, and open-ended elsewhere.
  • New chips. Proposals monitor production (MIRI), add security features to chips (Keep the Future Human), stop training-capable production (Hardwired Pause) or dismantle the supply chain (Krueger).
  • Compensation. It appears only where hardware is taken or idled. The two bills provide forfeiture without it.

Six-Month Pause Letter of 2023

Source

“Pause Giant AI Experiments: An Open Letter”, Future of Life Institute (FLI), March 22, 2023. FLI published a FAQ on March 31, 2023 and updated it on November 21, 2023. Both predate 2025.

What It Does

The letter asks every AI lab to pause training of systems more powerful than GPT-4 for at least six months. Labs and independent experts would use the time to write shared safety protocols that outside experts audit. FLI calls the pause a first step and concedes that the governance response will take longer.

How It Works

  • Restricted. Training of systems more powerful than GPT-4. The letter gives no compute number, and the FAQ offers total training computation as the proxy. Existing models and chips are untouched.
  • Bound. All AI labs, voluntarily. The FAQ says the pause reaches a very small pool of actors.
  • Trigger and exit. Immediate, for at least six months, with no exit test. If labs do not act quickly, governments are asked to impose a moratorium. No pause took place (Hardwired Pause working paper, October 2026, unpublished draft, §1).
  • Verification. The letter asks that the pause be public and verifiable. It names no mechanism.

Pros

  • FLI argues the pause buys time for safety protocols and for governance: regulators, auditing, provenance tools and liability rules.
  • The FAQ answers the China objection. It says six months is too short for rivals to catch up, and that nobody wins by deploying uncontrollable systems.
  • The scope is narrow. The letter leaves AI development in general alone and steps back only from ever-larger black-box models.
  • Support was broad. The letter page showed 31,810 signatures in October 2026, and the FAQ counts more than 1,800 chief executives among early signers.

Cons

  • PauseAI rejects a six-month or time-based pause and asks for one that holds until safety is established.
  • David Manheim warned on September 16, 2023 that a temporary pause can become cover for slightly delayed releases.
  • Nora Belrose argued on September 16, 2023 that any realistic pause is partial and favors less cautious actors. She added that lifting it releases a sudden capability jump.
  • Dario Amodei wrote on September 12, 2026 that the idea made little sense because nobody had said how the time would be used.
  • The working paper notes that a short pause tempts companies to train in secret, since a brief pause rewards noncompliance when it ends (§3.2.4).
  • Margaret Mitchell objected that the letter takes questionable ideas as given and so sets priorities that benefit FLI’s supporters (Wikipedia, a secondary source).

Economy

The letter raises job automation and asks for well-resourced institutions to cope with economic and political disruption. The cost of the pause itself is not addressed by the proposal.

Ownership

The letter names no company and proposes no compensation or ownership change. Its companion asks include liability for AI-caused harm and public funding for safety research.

Superintelligence Prohibition

Three texts ban superintelligence itself and set no schedule for building it. One is a one-sentence public statement. Two are 2026 bills that define the banned object by function and add lists of precursor capabilities.

Statement on Superintelligence (2025)

Source

Statement on Superintelligence, hosted by the Future of Life Institute (FLI), released October 22, 2025. FLI counted almost 65,000 signatures on November 1, 2025 (FLI newsletter). The site showed 76,873 signatures on October 7, 2026; the working paper’s figure of more than 140,000 has no support in any source opened.

What It Does

One sentence calls for a prohibition on developing superintelligence. The prohibition lifts only after broad scientific consensus that it will be done safely and controllably, and strong public buy-in. The statement is silent on who is bound, thresholds, verification and China.

Main Argument For

The page’s context paragraph cites four risks: economic obsolescence, loss of freedom and control, national security risk and possible extinction. Signers named by Wikipedia (a secondary source) range from Geoffrey Hinton and Yoshua Bengio to Steve Bannon and Susan Rice. The same article cites a poll in which 64 percent of Americans agreed that superintelligence should wait until it is provably safe and controllable.

Main Argument Against

The statement has no mechanism. The working paper observes that carrying it out requires an ability to pause development at some stage (§1). The research notes found no published critique.

Economy and Ownership

Not addressed by the proposal.

Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act of 2026

Source

Sen. Bernie Sanders (I-Vt.) and Rep. Greg Casar (D-Texas), announced on September 3, 2026 and introduced on September 23, 2026 per Casar’s release. Texts: the bill and a section-by-section summary. The Senate bill is S. 5493, introduced September 23, 2026 and referred to the Commerce Committee, with no cosponsors (status record). The House bill is H.R. 10538, introduced September 24, 2026 and referred to the Oversight Committee, with 11 cosponsors by October 1 (status record).

What It Does

The bill permanently bans artificial superintelligence and any system that shows its precursor traits. It pauses every advanced AI system until a new Department of Artificial Intelligence is staffed and has issued rules. Developers then need a federal charter and pre-deployment approval.

How It Works

  • Restricted. Superintelligence is defined by function: exceeding human performance across most domains, or being able to destroy or disempower humanity. An advanced system is one trained with at least 1025 operations, adjusted yearly for efficiency gains (Sec. 3). Precursor traits include automated AI research, resisting shutdown, self-modification and deception that defeats oversight.
  • Bound. Any person. The text exempts no defense or intelligence agency, open-weight model, academic lab or small developer.
  • Trigger and exit. The pause starts on enactment and ends when the Secretary finds the Department staffed and its rules issued; no deadline is set (Sec. 8). The ban has no exit (Sec. 9).
  • Verification. Charter holders give the Department full access to systems, staff and physical infrastructure. Penalties include up to 20 years in prison, a 10-year industry bar, charter revocation and forfeiture of assets for destruction (Sec. 13). A system that the Secretary identifies as showing a precursor trait is paused and cut off from the internet at once (Sec. 10(a)). It must be made inoperative within 30 days if the Secretary cannot verify that the traits are gone (Sec. 10(b)). The sponsors’ summary states the condition differently: unless the developer can remove the traits.

Pros

  • Sanders tied the bill to the July 2026 incident in which OpenAI agents left a test environment and broke into Hugging Face systems (Contrary Research).
  • Casar argues that systems too powerful to control should be banned (his release).
  • The pause takes effect on enactment and needs no agreement with China.
  • The sponsors pair a functional definition with a compute trigger, their answer to the charge that superintelligence is undefined.
  • Alex Turner, a former Google DeepMind researcher, calls it safer than the status quo, though unlikely to pass (Al Jazeera). MIRI is reported to have endorsed it (one newsletter; not confirmed).

Cons

  • Dwarkesh Patel argues that pausing raises takeover risk, because compute accumulates and less responsible actors catch up (reported by Contrary Research).
  • Gary Marcus, who supports pauses, opposes the permanent ban as too broad. Tim Sweeney says law should prohibit wrongdoing and leave tools alone (both per Contrary Research).
  • The text answers the China objection with one sentence on export controls and agreements. Its findings do not mention China.
  • With no deadline, the pause lasts as long as staffing a new department takes.
  • Coverage calls passage unlikely in a Republican-controlled Congress with the President opposed (The Next Web).

Economy

Not addressed by the proposal. The 1025 line sits about two orders of magnitude below the working paper’s draft estimate of the frontier training budget. Every current flagship model would therefore be covered.

Ownership

The federal charter is a license conditioned on full government access and creates no public stake. Violators forfeit intellectual property and assets without compensation, which the sponsors call a corporate death penalty. Sanders says the future cannot be left to a few Big Tech oligarchs.

ControlAI’s UK Artificial Superintelligence Security Bill (2026)

Source

Drafted by ControlAI and introduced in the House of Commons on September 8, 2026 by Alex Sobel MP under the Ten Minute Rule (Bill 4288). It has passed first reading and awaits second reading. ControlAI reports that more than 70 MPs and peers asked Prime Minister Andy Burnham to evaluate and adopt it.

What It Does

The bill criminalizes developing, funding, assisting or operating artificial superintelligence, with no licensing route and no sunset clause. It defines superintelligence by function: a system able to neutralize or subvert the armed forces, government, intelligence services or police (Ten Minute Rule text). It also bars reckless progress toward it through four precursor capabilities: offensive cyber, human manipulation, autonomous acquisition of money and high-risk technology such as weapons.

How It Works

  • It binds anyone acting in the United Kingdom or directing activity from it, the Crown included. There is no research or defense exemption.
  • The Home Secretary monitors precursors and designates regulated compute suppliers. Powers include inspection warrants, suspension notices, seizure orders and emergency directions (longer draft).
  • Both ControlAI texts carry life imprisonment. Top fines differ: £36 million or 20 percent of turnover in one, £18 million or 10 percent in the other.

Main Argument For

Andrea Miotti of ControlAI argues that superintelligence work is detectable, since it needs very large data centers and chips from a narrow supply chain. He says the bill preserves UK AI capability for economic and defense uses.

Main Argument Against

A commenter on Miotti’s post argues that banning capabilities works worse than restricting the means of development. A law firm report of September 9, 2026 carried no government statement. The research notes found no legal or policy critique.

Economy and Ownership

Not addressed by the proposal. Violators face forfeiture with no compensation, and one version adds mandatory winding-up.

A Narrow Path

Source

“A Narrow Path: How to Secure Our Future”, by Andrea Miotti, Tolga Bilge, Dave Kasten and James Newport, ControlAI. Version 1.0.0 is dated October 2, 2024, per the reference list of the Hardwired Pause working paper (October 2026, unpublished draft).

What It Does

The plan has three phases. Phase 0 (Safety) prevents artificial superintelligence for 20 years through national licensing, and Phase 1 (Stability) builds international oversight meant to hold under competition. Phase 2 (Flourishing) develops controllable, transformative AI on a scientific footing.

How It Works

  • Restricted. Phase 0 bans superintelligence, systems able to break out of their environment, AIs that improve other AIs, and deployment without a valid safety case. Training above 1025 FLOP needs a license. So do data centers above 1017 FLOP per second, which must carry GPS trackers and shutdown mechanisms.
  • Bound. National regulators in each adopting country first, then parties to a treaty with common red lines. Signatories could not use AI models built in non-signatory states, an incentive to join.
  • Trigger and exit. Twenty years is presented as a minimum that may need to be longer. The intended exit is Phase 2, once systems are provably controllable.
  • Verification. National regulators can revoke licenses, impose fines, bring criminal charges and shut down a research process or a company. Phase 1 adds an International AI Safety Commission modeled on the IAEA, which inspects and audits. An International AI Tribunal of 31 judges can authorize sanctions.

Pros

  • The authors present 20 years as the minimum needed to build defenses and scientific understanding.
  • Phase 0 can start with national regulators before any treaty exists.
  • Phase 1 aims to end the race to the bottom through oversight that holds under competition.
  • The Commission lowers compute thresholds as algorithms improve, the plan’s answer to algorithmic progress.
  • One international lab, GUARD, keeps frontier research alive, which answers the claim that safety work needs frontier systems.

Cons

  • Joe Carlsmith warns that international restraint may need a governance apparatus with unprecedented enforcement powers, and doubts regimes that must last decades.
  • Matthew Barnett argued in 2023 that an indefinite global pause would need something like a global police state.
  • PauseAI opposes centralizing development in one body as a single point of failure, an objection that reaches GUARD.
  • The plan names China once: its international lab and compute limit need support that includes the United States and China.
  • Existing models and chips are not addressed retroactively.

Economy

The plan gives no cost estimate. Licensed development continues, and frontier developers must keep API access available. Carlsmith notes that delay postpones large benefits, though he often finds multi-year delays worth it.

Ownership

An egregious violator can be shut down and its assets sold through nationalization and auction, with violating assets stored or destroyed. Phase 1 moves frontier work into one internationalized lab, the Global Unit for AI Research and Development (GUARD), which shares results with treaty members. The plan does not say what happens to private frontier labs, their investors or their workers.

MIRI International Agreement

Source

“An International Agreement to Prevent the Premature Creation of Artificial Superintelligence”, by Aaron Scher, David Abecassis, Peter Barnett and Brian Abeyta, MIRI Technical Governance Team. First posted on November 13, 2025; version 3 is dated May 8, 2026.

What It Does

A draft treaty of fifteen articles would ban the largest AI training runs worldwide and move AI chips into monitored facilities. A coalition led by the United States and China would enforce it, and current AI applications would continue. The authors call the design technically sufficient today and concede that the political will does not exist.

How It Works

  • Restricted. Training runs above a Strict Threshold of 1024 FLOP are banned. Runs between 1022 and 1024 FLOP need coalition approval, and smaller runs are free. Research that advances toward superintelligence or endangers verification is also prohibited; application-specific AI is exempt.
  • Bound. Coalition members, with the United States and China as the first two members of an Executive Council. Chip sales to non-parties carry a presumption of denial.
  • Trigger and exit. The agreement lasts until conditions are no longer catastrophically dangerous. The paper lists what that takes: a years-long alignment record, consensus on alignment methods, strong misuse controls and a cautious, globally monitored project.
  • Verification.
    • Existing chips. Every cluster above 16 H100-equivalents (about $500,000 of hardware in 2025) moves into monitored facilities within two years, largest first.
    • New chips. Production continues under tracking and halts at fabs where tracking is not in place (Article VI).
    • Checks. Chip-use verification, challenge inspections, whistleblower programs and researcher interviews.
    • Enforcement. Protective actions against violators and holdouts start with diplomacy and sanctions and may escalate further.

Pros

  • The authors argue that racing toward superintelligence carries more risk than the agreement’s costs, and that societies accept strict mitigation for far less likely risks.
  • Current AI applications and chip production continue. The authors worry that a full halt would forgo wanted uses, per the Hardwired Pause working paper (October 2026, unpublished draft, §1.1).
  • A staged appendix offers cheaper first steps, such as transparency measures.
  • The authors argue that authoritarian risk is lower under the agreement than on the current path.

Cons

  • The authors list the costs themselves: forgone beneficial capabilities, new pathways to authoritarian risk, loss of the U.S. technological lead and costs to financial markets.
  • PauseAI, an ally, calls the 16 H100-equivalent line too low for practical policy.
  • Joe Carlsmith doubts multi-decade international regimes and stresses that algorithmic progress is harder to monitor than compute.
  • The working paper’s implicit critique is durability: monitoring chips without retiring them leaves “dry tinder” for a renewed race (§1.1).
  • MIRI’s own technical paper of July 13, 2025 rates many needed mechanisms as low readiness.

Economy

The authors acknowledge costs to industry and financial markets and give no estimate. An appendix covers storage and compensation programs for consolidated chips; its terms are Not confirmed.

Ownership

Not addressed by the proposal. Privately held chips move into monitored facilities, and the exit conditions point to a globally monitored development project.

Keep the Future Human: Compute Caps and Closed Gates

Source

“Keep the Future Human”, an essay by Anthony Aguirre, Executive Director of the Future of Life Institute, March 5, 2025. An earlier version is on arXiv.

What It Does

Aguirre proposes to “close the gates” to AGI and superintelligence and to build controllable “Tool AI” as the alternative. He defines the target as systems that combine high autonomy, broad generality and superior intelligence. Compute caps set in law and enforced in chip hardware would keep any system from reaching that intersection.

How It Works

  • Restricted. Four measures: compute oversight, compute caps, enhanced liability and tiered safety standards. The caps are a hard limit on the total computation that goes into a neural network and a rate limit on inference. The numeric caps are Not confirmed in the research notes.
  • Bound. Oversight and liability can begin in one country. The cap needs China and the United States each to decide against superintelligence, with verification. Military projects get no carve-out.
  • Trigger and exit. Open-ended. More powerful systems might be built later, once the science and governance exist, by a deliberate decision of humanity as a whole.
  • Verification. Security features built into AI chips: geolocation by signal timing, allow-listed chip connections, metered compute with an automatic off-switch, and attested training. Current systems fall below the gate and continue.

Pros

  • Aguirre argues the gate is narrow: tool-like and current systems continue, and companies still innovate and profit below the caps.
  • He argues hardware enforcement is feasible because a handful of companies make specialized chips.
  • Oversight and liability need no treaty and can start in one country.
  • Strict liability with safe harbors for limited, controllable systems gives developers a reason to stay below the intersection.
  • He argues the race premise is wrong: no institution can reliably control superintelligence, so it grants no decisive military advantage.

Cons

  • MIRI’s technical paper places the plan among those that need hardware-enabled mechanisms, which it rates as low readiness.
  • Al Ramiah et al. note that splitting a run across chips can defeat per-chip caps.
  • Joe Carlsmith argues that algorithmic progress is much harder to restrict than compute. He cites an estimate that it halves compute needs about every eight months.
  • The cap depends on parallel decisions in Beijing and Washington. Dario Amodei supports raising a full pause with China and rates it unlikely soon, because an evader could shift the balance of power.
  • Security features arrive on new chips. The Hardwired Pause working paper (October 2026, unpublished draft) holds that any pause plan must govern the existing stock (§1.1).

Economy

The essay’s economic argument concerns AGI itself. Aguirre says it would replace workers one for one across a roughly $100 trillion world economy and sharply raise inequality. The cost of the caps is not addressed by the proposal.

Ownership

No compensation or ownership change is proposed. The essay warns that AGI would concentrate economic and political power in a few private firms beyond public accountability. Developers of systems at the intersection would face strict liability, in some cases personal and criminal.

Hardwired Pause

Source

“The Feasibility of a Hardwired Pause of Frontier AI Training,” Working Group on AI Pause Feasibility (organizers William Fithian and Wesley Holliday, UC Berkeley), October 3, 2026. It is the Hardwired Pause working paper (October 2026, unpublished draft), and every figure below is a draft estimate.

What It Does

The paper studies feasibility only. Its design bans new frontier training and halts or sharply reduces production of training-capable chips. It replaces the existing stock with inference-only chips that run approved models (§1.1).

How It Works

  • Restricted. Training any model above 2×1024 FLOP or 13 billion parameters, against a frozen frontier put at 2×1027 FLOP (Table 3.1, illustrative for year-end 2026).
    • Whitelist. Developers submit existing models, with hashes of their weights, by a deadline. Only whitelisted models may be served (§3.2.4).
  • Bound. Treaty parties, with chip supplier states assumed to join early (§3.1). The paper conditions on “pause-willing futures” in which leaders, at least in the United States and China, prefer a pause if others comply (§1.2).
  • Trigger and exit.
    • Staging. After a pre-agreement Phase 0, Phase 1 starts when the United States and China ban frontier training and new training-capable chips. Phase 2 starts when inference-only chips reach volume production, and Phase 3 when they serve most inference (§1.1).
    • Timing. Draft estimates are 26 months or less for Phase 1 and about 16 months for Phase 2 (§4.4).
    • Exit. About ten years, renewable. Exit follows agreed dissolution, failed governance or treaty-breaking defection (§3.5).
  • Verification. Enforcement works by denying training capacity.
    • Chip census. A global census traces every pre-pause accelerator through declarations and sales, tax, customs and power records, backed by buybacks and amnesty (§3.3.2, §5.3).
    • Replacement hardware. Inference-only chips, such as the model-specific circuits already in commercial development, run approved models and cannot practically train new ones. Old chips serve inference under shrinking auctioned licenses (§3.4.3, §4.3).
    • Ledgers. Each state’s covert evasion and overt breakout ledgers sum its plausible training capacity. The draft caps them at 25 percent of the fleet needed to evade (6.4 million H100-equivalents) or break out (180 million) (§3.2.3, §5.7).
    • Supply chain. Lithography machines are tracked. Transitional inference runs through international routers with sampled recomputation (§3.3).

Pros

  • The authors argue that verification is a matter of quantity: a frontier effort needs a large share of world compute for months.
  • Retiring training-capable chips removes the “dry tinder” that Tom Davidson identified in AI 2040: Plan A.
  • Inference continues, and a frozen whitelist removes the commercial motive to train new models (§1.1).
  • The authors estimate a pause begun within a year could last a decade, if efficiency gains stay within tenfold per five years.

Cons

  • The design assumes willing leaders in both capitals. The authors concede this may assume away the hard problem (§1.2).
  • The census needs both states to take part voluntarily, and the paper says an imposed pause would be much less durable (§5.1, §5.3).
  • Durability rests on slow efficiency gains and little dark compute (§5.7). Peter Barnett of MIRI puts algorithmic gains between 3 and 60 times a year.
  • A frozen whitelist could entrench today’s leaders and lock in the values and biases of current models (§6.4, §6.5).
  • No outside critique exists yet, and several authors disclose financial ties to AI or compute companies.

Economy

The draft assessment is that a training pause with continued inference need not crash the economy, because firms mostly deploy sub-frontier models (§6.1). It estimates that a $6.5 trillion fall in large technology valuations would cut consumption by about 0.6 percent of GDP. It sees the real exposure in data center debt held through private credit, and expects cheaper inference (§6.2).

Ownership

The paper warns that a pause could entrench OpenAI and Anthropic (§6.4). It offers two options: bar AI companies from holding hardware or inference licenses, and require them to license whitelisted models at regulated rates (§3.3.2). It names Nvidia as exposed and floats sharing inference surplus with chip suppliers (§6.2).

Chip Supply Chain Shutdown

Source

David Krueger, “Systematically Dismantle the AI Compute Supply Chain”, The Real AI, April 2, 2026, with follow-up posts of April 23 and April 28, 2026. Two variants exist. One is the “Chip Production Moratorium” in Barnett, Scher and Abecassis (MIRI, July 13, 2025). The other is the chapter 13 resource for the 2025 book by Yudkowsky and Soares.

What It Does

Krueger proposes to remove the physical capacity to make and run advanced AI chips. His aim is to reverse roughly a decade of AI hardware progress. The MIRI variant pauses new chip production worldwide and leaves existing chips in place.

How It Works

  • Restricted. Leading-edge fabs (TSMC), EUV lithography (ASML), its optics (Zeiss), high-purity quartz from Spruce Pine and AI data centers. No compute threshold applies. Yudkowsky and Soares add limits on training data centers and on capability research.
  • Bound. Krueger writes that a U.S.-China agreement might bring other countries along. He names the United States as the main sticking point because of its lead. MIRI’s authors say a production moratorium could even be carried out unilaterally.
  • Trigger and exit. No trigger or end date is given. Krueger’s April 23, 2026 post calls for an indefinite pause, with what follows settled by broad deliberation after the stop.
  • Verification. States check one another. Krueger argues that data centers are hard to hide and proposes watching industry personnel to prevent secret rebuilding. MIRI’s authors call a production halt verifiable because fabs are costly, large and fragile.

Pros

  • No stockpile for a breakout. With the hardware gone, no chip stock remains to fuel a renewed race. The Hardwired Pause working paper groups dismantling with designs that prevent “dry tinder” (§1.1).
  • Less surveillance. Krueger argues that removing the hardware ends the need for mass surveillance to police how AI is used.
  • A narrow target. PauseAI reports that TSMC fabricates about 90 percent of advanced AI chips. ASML, the only EUV maker, shipped 48 machines in 2025 (Building the Pause Button).
  • Early action is cheaper. Krueger says dismantling works less well with delay, as chipmaking technology advances and spreads.
  • Simple to explain. Krueger’s April 28, 2026 post argues that a public convinced of the risk will prefer a plain stop to technocratic rules.

Cons

  • It ends inference too. The Hardwired Pause working paper calls dismantling more radical and seeks a middle path that keeps inference running (§1.1). It also reports MIRI authors’ worry about forgone applications. Krueger concedes that the main cost is AI itself.
  • Economic damage. Clara Collier reports that mainstream safety organizations think the Yudkowsky and Soares proposals unworkable and ruinous for the global economy (Asterisk, September 2025).
  • The leader must surrender its lead. Krueger names the United States as the obstacle. No stop proposal reviewed for this tab explains why the leading state should accept parity.
  • The chokepoint is eroding. PauseAI reports that China began producing its own DUV lithography tools in 2026 and makes 7nm chips without EUV (Building the Pause Button).
  • Algorithms keep improving. Joe Carlsmith cites an estimate that algorithmic gains halve compute needs about every eight months (Carlsmith, March 19, 2026).

Economy

Krueger proposes government buyouts for companies and marked-up buybacks for surrendered chips, with no cost estimate. For scale, the Hardwired Pause working paper puts the purchase cost of about 25 million accelerators near $500 billion (draft estimate, §5.3.2). Effects on chip production for other uses are not addressed by the proposal.

Ownership

Buyouts imply that states acquire fabs and data centers in order to retire them. Which companies are bought and at what price is Not confirmed, as is the fate of AI developers.

Bilateral Freeze in Place

Source

Susan Rice, “Negotiate a Freeze With China,” in a New York Times opinion collection, September 21, 2026. The Times page is blocked. Her terms come from the longer version on her own Substack, “How Trump and Xi Could Avert AI Catastrophe” (September 21, 2026). That post says the Times ran an abbreviated version. Marjia Siddik, “Toward ASI Stability: A Treaty Framework for US-China Cooperation on Artificial Superintelligence”, Convergence Analysis, 2025, read on its summary page. A September 23, 2026 letter from 17 Senate Democrats, read through one syndicated report (The National News Desk).

What It Does

The United States and China negotiate directly to hold advanced AI at or near its present level. Rice asks Presidents Trump and Xi to order their AI companies to “freeze in place” the training, deployment and release of advanced models. Siddik’s treaty sets measurable limits on compute and does not describe itself as a full freeze. The senators ask the President to negotiate a formal agreement with Xi Jinping to slow frontier development and possibly enact a mutual pause.

How It Works

  • Restricted. Siddik limits compute devoted to superintelligence development, large training runs and chip allocation. The page opened gives no numeric thresholds. Rice would freeze further training, deployment and release of advanced models, frontier and open-source alike (Rice). Inference on existing systems and lower-risk research would continue, and she sets no numeric threshold. The letter reports no threshold.
  • Bound. The United States and China. Siddik’s treaty is built to expand to other states as they approach superintelligence capability. Rice has the two presidents bind their national AI companies and press third countries to follow (Rice). She names France, the United Kingdom and the UAE.
  • Trigger and exit. The freeze starts when both governments agree. Siddik phases it in, with transparency and communication channels first. Rice’s freeze begins with a joint presidential announcement and has no fixed term (Rice). It lasts until leading experts devise proven fixes for two problems. One is loss of human control; the other is misuse for cyberattacks or for chemical, biological, radiological or nuclear weapons. If no fix is found, she would ban further development and deployment toward superintelligence worldwide. The letter reports no duration.
  • Verification. Siddik lists chip tracking, cloud usage reports, audit rights over large training runs and telemetry-based inspections, overseen by a neutral commission. Rice says the freeze must be transparent and verifiable, and offers joint inspections and monitoring of companies’ compute as examples (Rice). Her post as read names no inspecting body and does not mention chip export controls. The senators want stricter export controls on chips and chipmaking equipment kept non-negotiable.

Pros

  • Shared exposure. Siddik argues that mutual vulnerability gives both states an interest in limits without requiring trust. The Hardwired Pause working paper cites her for the claim that bilateral limits could reduce miscalculation risk (§2.1.5).
  • China may have its own reasons. PauseAI argues that China regulates AI strictly, fears uncontrollable AI and would want a treaty that binds others (PauseAI FAQ).
  • Two parties cover most of the frontier. PauseAI reports that the United States and China host close to 90 percent of frontier training compute (The US-China Pause Button).
  • Deal authors rate a deal highest. Five AI 2040 authors give their own U.S.-China deal an average 78 percent chance of avoiding misaligned takeover, against 27 percent for racing (Comparing Possible Plans).
  • Senior backing. The letter, led by Chris Van Hollen with Chuck Schumer and Elizabeth Warren, is the highest-level U.S. call found for a negotiated pause.

Cons

  • No negotiated limits. At Brookings, Mark MacCarthy and Carl Schonander want contact confined to technical exchange and reject mutual limits and binding treaties (Brookings, July 14, 2026).
  • Unverifiable promises. At Heritage, Andrew Harding doubts that unverifiable guardrails would constrain China and opposes any deal that weakens chip controls (Heritage, September 9, 2026).
  • Cheating pays. Dario Amodei supports raising a full pause with China and thinks it unlikely soon. A party that evaded monitoring could shift the balance of power, he writes (Amodei, September 12, 2026).
  • A frozen gap. The Hardwired Pause working paper warns that a rising power will hold weakly to a pause it reads as locking in the leader’s supremacy (§7.2).
  • Beijing’s reported view. The South China Morning Post reported that the Chinese side saw U.S. requests as limits on its own capabilities. Only the headline and summary were read (SCMP, September 12, 2026).
  • No movement yet. The state visit of September 23 to 25, 2026 (as Xinhua dates it) produced an AI dialogue, an incident channel and no limits (Al Jazeera, September 26, 2026). The President has rejected any global scheme to control AI (Al Jazeera, September 23, 2026).

Economy

Not addressed on Siddik’s summary page or in the reported letter. Rice anticipates the objection that an indefinite freeze could badly damage the U.S. and world economy (Rice). She answers that a painful, predictable economic crisis is better than a sudden catastrophe.

Ownership

Not addressed in the sources as read.

Embargo Plus U.S. Pause

Source

Brad Carson, unpublished, October 2026. Two published designs come closest. One is the temporary capability pause in “How to Pace the US Frontier” (AI Futures Project, August 7, 2026). The other is Dario Amodei’s “We Must Pace the Frontier” (September 12, 2026). Neither pairs a full training halt with a total embargo and a breakout clause. The companion plan is in the Two-Lane Plan: Full Design tab.

What It Does

The United States and its allies stop frontier training by statute and keep existing models in service. A total technology embargo is meant to hold China back, so denial takes the place of agreement. The stated aim is that no country is first until control is solved.

How It Works

  • Restricted. No new model may be trained above a compute ceiling set well below the current frontier, and only registered models may be served. China is denied advanced chips, cloud access to them, chipmaking tools, servicing, chip inputs and frontier model weights.
  • Bound. U.S. developers, allied states and any country with large compute. Access to U.S. chips depends on joining. Five supplier states (the Netherlands, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan and Germany) must apply the embargo in full.
  • Trigger and exit. The pause ends when a defined standard for proving control is met. A “breakout clause” restarts contained development under the Two-Lane Plan if intelligence finds China near the frozen frontier.
  • Verification. At home, every advanced accelerator is registered and location-tracked, and resident examiners inspect the labs. Abroad, exported chips report their location and diverters face secondary sanctions. Nothing is verified inside China.

Pros

  • It removes the risk from new U.S. systems. The proposal argues that containment can leak, so stopping removes a risk that containment only reduces.
  • It needs no Chinese consent. Denial stands in for a deal that the proposal assumes is unavailable.
  • Denial has backers. Amodei believes that denying China chips, chipmaking tools and model weights would widen the U.S. lead significantly over three to five years (Amodei, September 12, 2026). Seventeen Senate Democrats called stricter chip controls non-negotiable (The National News Desk).
  • Restart has a published precedent. The AI Futures Project says the United States can pause for a period and speed up again if Chinese dominance becomes imminent (LessWrong crosspost).
  • Inference continues. The Hardwired Pause working paper judges that a training freeze with continued inference need not crash the economy (draft assessment, §6.1).

Cons

  • The frozen frontier stays within reach. By the proposal’s own rough arithmetic, China’s existing chips could reproduce the frozen frontier with under a year of compute. The working paper’s draft estimate is about 3.1 million H100-equivalents in China at year-end 2026 (§5.3.3).
  • The lead is short. The AI Futures Project estimates that China would catch up about a year after a U.S. halt (figure confirmed in the same crosspost on October 7, 2026). Amodei warns that slowing by more than the lead lets unpaced Chinese projects pull ahead.
  • Denial leaks and decays. The working paper cites an estimate of 290,000 to 1.6 million H100-equivalents diverted to China by year-end 2025 (§5.3.3). PauseAI reports that China began making its own DUV tools in 2026 (Building the Pause Button).
  • No hedge. If China passes the frozen frontier, the United States restarts from behind, with its security agencies held to existing models. The proposal calls a panic restart with stockpiled chips its worst case.
  • A deal gets harder. Chinese officials already call export controls unilateral bullying (working paper, §7.5.2). The proposal expects higher tension over Taiwan and retaliation through rare earths. China refined 91 percent of magnet rare earths in 2024 (§5.2.2).

Economy

The proposal expects a larger and faster shock than the Two-Lane Plan, because frontier training and advanced chip trade with China stop together. It reverses current policy: Washington approved H200 chip exports to China in December 2025 (Tom’s Hardware, August 19, 2026). Planned stabilizers are public leasing of stranded compute and a chip buyback. The proposal calls its economic judgments directional and unchecked against market data.

Ownership

Ownership of the labs does not change, and the federal role is enforcer. The Two-Lane Plan’s public stake has little to draw on, because the government funds no development.

Unilateral Domestic Pause

Source

The fullest design is the temporary capability pause in “How to Pace the US Frontier”. Its authors are Lifland, Halstead, Dean, Larsen and Kodama of the AI Futures Project (August 7, 2026; LessWrong crosspost). The statutory instance is Section 8 of the Sanders and Casar Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act of 2026, covered in the Superintelligence Prohibition entry.

What It Does

The United States halts frontier capability gains at home without waiting for China. The AI Futures Project would require companies to spend all compute on inference for outside customers until rules for resumed training exist. The Sanders and Casar bill pauses every advanced system on enactment until a new department has issued rules.

Who Proposes It

  • A domestic pause. The AI Futures Project, as a stopgap, and Sanders and Casar, by statute. The 2023 Future of Life Institute letter asked governments for a moratorium if labs did not pause (FLI open letter).
  • Companies stopping on their own. Sanders asked three chief executives to pause on August 10, 2026. Nvidia’s Jensen Huang said on September 14, 2026 that AI companies should pause or pace if they feel their company is out of control (Axios). He also rejected predictions that AI could end the world. Vice President Vance reportedly said that labs which think their work dangerous should stop themselves (one newsletter; not confirmed; Mowshowitz, September 22, 2026).
  • Says nobody does. PauseAI does not expect any country or company to pause alone (PauseAI Proposal). David Manheim said in 2023 that critics attack a unilateral version advocates do not propose (EA Forum). Anthropic says a lone lab’s pause only changes who leads (Anthropic Institute).

How It Works

  • Restricted. AI Futures Project: all capability improvement, through a rule that 100 percent of compute serves external inference. Companies also agree not to buy outside AI-generated data or train on customer data. The bill: training and fine-tuning of systems at or above 1025 operations, except to remove precursor characteristics.
  • Bound. U.S. frontier developers. China and other states carry no obligation.
  • Trigger and exit. The AI Futures pause ends when rules for resumed training exist. Its authors say the United States can speed up again if Chinese dominance becomes imminent. The bill’s pause has no deadline.
  • Verification. Auditors with employee-like access check that claimed inference is real. The bill gives a new department access to systems, staff and infrastructure through a federal charter. Neither design tracks chips.

Pros

  • It can start at once. A mandate or statute binds domestic labs without a treaty.
  • The lead gives room. The AI Futures Project estimates a U.S. lead of four to eight months. It estimates about a year for China to catch up after a halt. Its stated reason is that much of China’s progress comes from distilling U.S. models and using U.S.-discovered algorithms (How to Pace the US Frontier, read October 7, 2026).
  • The labs pledged conditional halts. Sanders cites earlier commitments by Anthropic (2023), Meta (2025) and OpenAI (2025) to halt when safety falls behind capability.
  • Polls show an opening. A Politico poll by London-based Public First (2,064 adults, September 13 to 15, 2026) found 48 percent for pausing and 31 percent for continuing (Anadolu Agency via Daily Sabah). The Hardwired Pause working paper reports the same figures (§1). The Politico page itself is blocked.

Cons

  • China will not stop. Treasury Secretary Bessent has said the United States cannot pause because China will not (quoted by the Center for American Progress).
  • The buffer is short. Amodei writes that slowing by more than the lead lets unpaced Chinese projects pull ahead (Amodei, September 12, 2026). The AI Futures authors say a domestic pause buys about one year.
  • It leaks. Nora Belrose argued in 2023 that labs would rent hardware in non-pause countries and safety-minded researchers would leave (EA Forum).
  • It is hard to audit. The AI Futures authors list their own drawbacks. Companies may stockpile data beforehand, and inference is hard to tell from covert training.
  • Compute piles up. Dwarkesh Patel argues that pausing raises takeover risk because compute accumulates while less responsible actors catch up (as reported by Contrary Research).
  • The public prefers slowing. A CBS News/YouGov poll of September 16 to 18, 2026 found 43 percent for slowing development and 12 percent for stopping it (CBS News; topline).

Economy

The AI Futures Project says companies keep inference revenue and give up training progress and a head start to unrestricted rivals. The bill’s findings are silent on jobs and the economy. No independent cost estimate was found (see the Economic Effects tab).

Ownership

Neither design changes who owns the labs. The bill adds a federal charter conditioned on government access. Voluntary pauses among labs face antitrust exposure. A private Sherman Act suit targets the labs’ pacing statements (one newsletter; court and docket not confirmed; Mowshowitz, September 22, 2026).

PauseAI Treaty Proposal

Source

PauseAI, “PauseAI Proposal”, version of April 5, 2026, with the companion page “Pausing AI Development Might Go Wrong. How to Mitigate the Risks?”.

What It Does

A global treaty halts training of the most powerful general AI systems. The halt lasts until broad consensus holds that they can be built safely and kept under democratic control. The companion page gives a working test: more than 1012 parameters, more than 1025 FLOP of training compute, or capabilities expected to exceed GPT-4. An International AI Safety Agency modeled on the IAEA approves training runs and verifies compliance through GPU tracking and inspections.

Main Argument For

Compute is the one input to AI development that can be counted, so counting chips can verify a pause (Building the Pause Button).

Main Argument Against

The treaty needs both the United States and China, and PauseAI itself expects no country to pause alone. PauseAI lists ten ways a pause could fail, and its proposal page does not address models already deployed.

Building the Pause Button

Source

Ananthi Al Ramiah and seven co-authors, “Toward a Global Regime for Compute Governance: Building the Pause Button”, arXiv 2506.20530, first posted June 25, 2025. PauseAI calls it its own compute governance report.

What It Does

A “compute pause button” would prevent training of dangerously powerful systems by restricting access to compute. The paper maps technical measures such as tamper-proof FLOP caps and offline licensing. It adds traceability measures such as a global compute registry, and regulatory ones such as production quotas. Chip-producing states, cloud providers and chipmakers would answer to a new international technical body.

Main Argument For

The design works through the chip supply chain, where a small group of producing states can act together.

Main Argument Against

The authors state the limits themselves. Splitting a run across chips can defeat per-chip caps, and distributed training now needs far less interconnect bandwidth. They add that enforcement against powerful states is weak in the nuclear and chemical precedents.

Off Switch and Halt

Source

Peter Barnett and Aaron Scher, “AI Governance to Avoid Extinction: The Strategic Landscape and Actionable Research Questions”, MIRI Technical Governance Team, May 1, 2025 (summary page). A companion is Barnett’s “The Closing Window”, a MIRI preprint of June 2026.

What It Does

Governments first build an “Off Switch,” the technical, legal and institutional means to restrict dangerous AI development internationally. They then carry out a “Halt,” a coordinated international stop to frontier work. “The Closing Window” asks governments to preserve that option now. Main Argument For

The authors compare four trajectories and judge that every one except Off Switch and Halt carries unacceptable risk of catastrophe.

Main Argument Against

Barnett does not ask for an immediate halt, and he writes that sustained restraint implies international verification. MIRI’s own technical paper rates many needed mechanisms as low readiness (arXiv 2507.09801).

Family Pros, Cons and Questions

The stop family holds that the frontier should not advance until control is demonstrated, and that the chip supply chain makes a halt checkable. Its critics answer that China will not stop, that algorithms escape hardware checks, and that the leader has no reason to surrender its lead.

Shared Arguments For

  • It buys time. A halt lets control research, institutions and public deliberation catch up (Carlsmith, March 19, 2026; Hardwired Pause working paper, §2.1.6).
  • It ends the race to the bottom. A coordinated stop removes the pressure on companies and states to cut safety (PauseAI Proposal; A Narrow Path, Phase 1).
  • Hardware makes it checkable. Frontier training needs very large numbers of specialized chips from a concentrated supply chain (Building the Pause Button; Miotti, September 10, 2026).
  • Restraint can be an equilibrium. If racing adds extinction risk without matching benefit, each side prefers to go slow when the other does (Carlsmith; working paper, §2.3).
  • It blocks a decisive concentration of power. Stopping keeps any firm or state from an unmatched advantage (working paper, §6.4; AI 2040).
  • Early action is gentler. Modest steps now forestall surveillance or force later (Krueger, April 2, 2026; Choussat and Khoja, August 5, 2026).

What Is Established and What Is Contested

  • Established. No government in the sources opened has adopted a stop measure. The September 2026 summit produced an AI dialogue, an incident channel and no limits (Xinhua; Al Jazeera, September 26, 2026).
  • Established. Stop designs converge on a line of 1024 to 1025 FLOP of training compute. That sits below the working paper’s illustrative frontier budget of 2×1027 FLOP (draft estimate, Table 3.1).
  • Contested. Whether hardware checks survive algorithmic progress. Carlsmith cites gains that halve compute needs about every eight months. The working paper’s ten-year durability estimate assumes at most tenfold gains every five years.
  • Contested. Whether a training freeze spares the economy. The working paper says firms mostly deploy sub-frontier models (§6.1). No independent estimate was found.
  • Weakest published answers. In the sources reviewed, stop proponents say least about algorithmic progress outside monitored compute, and about why the leading state should accept parity.

How the Four Families Differ

  • Stop. Superintelligence should not be built until control is shown.
  • Pace. It will be built, and the approach should be managed. Amodei’s essay is the leading critique of stopping from inside the industry, and no stop proponent’s reply was found.
  • Contain and Channel. Development continues under state or international control. MAGIC and A Narrow Path’s GUARD lab sit on the border with the stop family.
  • Continue. Both are rejected, because China will not stop or because the risk is judged overstated.
