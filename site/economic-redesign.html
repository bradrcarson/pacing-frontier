Issues

Economic Redesign

This tab asks what would have to change in the U.S. economy under each family of proposals, from the tax base to the safety net.

30 open and closed questions

The Question

The U.S. economy taxes work and insures people through their jobs. Its stock index funds are concentrated in AI-linked firms. Advanced AI strains each arrangement, and the order of the strains depends on what happens to the frontier.

Seven systems are in play: labor markets, the tax base, income support, public stakes, competition rules, capital markets and the power grid. Forecasts run from a productivity gain under 1 percent over ten years (Acemoglu, 2024) to wage collapse under full automation (Korinek and Suh, 2024). That spread is the planning problem every proposal in this tab tries to handle.

Why the Answer Differs by Family (Inference)

  • Stop. Frontier training halts while released models stay in use. Displacement from those models continues, and the financial risk peaks when a halt is announced.
  • Pace. The same tools are needed, and the state partly sets the schedule. Time becomes available to build them in steps.
  • Contain and Channel. The state becomes both funder and anchor customer of the frontier labs. Capability built in containment reaches the public economy in steps, at each release decision.
  • Continue. The same tools are needed in the least time. Triggers written into law in advance carry the most weight, because new legislation would lag events.

Contained development raises the concern in The Intelligence Curse most directly. A state with frontier capability inside a perimeter depends less on its citizens’ labor and taxes.

What the Evidence Supports

No published analysis compares redesign needs across the four families. The Hardwired Pause working paper (October 2026, unpublished draft) covers a stop, and the AI 2040 economics supplement covers a paced path. Nothing comparable exists for contained development with gated deployment.

Two unpublished plans by Brad Carson have their own rows in the family table: the Two-Lane Plan and Embargo Plus U.S. Pause. No outside economic assessment of either exists.

The cross-family comparisons in this tab are therefore inference from the research notes and should be labeled as original analysis. The sources were read through a tool that condenses pages, so each figure needs checking against its original before publication.

Labor Markets and Displacement

The measured effect through mid-2026 is a hiring shortfall for workers aged 22 to 25 in AI-exposed occupations. Occupation-level government data show no aggregate displacement, and forecasts cover almost the full range of outcomes.

What Is Established

  • Entry-level hiring. Stanford researchers used ADP payroll records through June 2026 in a paper revised August 12, 2026. Employment of 22-to-25-year-olds in the most exposed occupations fell about 11 percent from November 2022. In the least exposed it grew about 10 percent, and the authors put the gap at 19 percent.
  • Mechanism. The gap comes from reduced hiring, and separations did not rise. The authors call the facts descriptive and make no causal estimate.
  • Whole occupations. The Budget Lab at Yale (May 7, 2026) found the effect on exposed occupations close to zero through March 2026.
  • Young workers’ unemployment. St. Louis Fed economists (June 30, 2026) studied April 2023 to December 2025. Demand for AI-related skills explained roughly one third of the rise among 18-to-24-year-olds. The broad decline in job openings was the larger factor.

The research notes read these findings as compatible. A small entry cohort can lose hiring while whole occupations show almost no effect.

How It Differs by Family (Inference)

  • Stop. Displacement from released models continues and may quicken as inference gets cheaper. The extreme scenarios recede while the stop holds.
  • Pace. Displacement arrives on a schedule the state partly sets. The AI 2040 supplement projects 99 percent automation around 2035 under its plan, against early 2031 otherwise (advocates’ scenario). Another passage on the same page puts it in the late 2030s.
  • Contain and Channel. Public displacement follows what clears the gate, and risk bunches at each clearance.
  • Continue. The need is most urgent and the time is shortest.

What Is Unknown

  • Whether AI caused the entry-level shortfall.
  • How large displacement would be under a contained lane. No published estimate exists.

The Tax Base and the Federal Budget

Federal revenue rests on labor income, so a falling labor share weakens the budget even when output grows. Most analysts in the notes prefer broad capital, profit or consumption taxes, while members of Congress have introduced AI-specific levies.

What Is Established

  • Revenue mix. CBO (February 2026) projects fiscal 2026 revenue of $5,596 billion. Individual income taxes supply $2,751 billion and payroll taxes $1,826 billion, about 82 percent together by the research notes’ calculation.
  • Labor’s share of revenue. RAND (August 31, 2026) puts it near two thirds. Korinek and Lockwood (January 8, 2026) put it near three quarters.
  • Trust funds. Payroll taxes supplied 91.3 percent of Social Security’s revenue in 2025 (CRS, June 25, 2026). The 2026 Trustees project depletion of the retirement fund in late 2032.
  • Growth and revenue. The Budget Lab at Yale (July 20, 2026) finds rapid adoption adds up to $216 billion of revenue in 2030. The gain would roughly double if labor and capital shares held at 2026 levels. Capital income is taxed more lightly: top federal rates are 37 percent on labor income and 23.8 percent on capital gains.

What Is Contested

  • Which threat is larger. Patel and Gordon (Brookings, August 7, 2026) say the larger fiscal threat is debt already on the books. They cite CBO debt of 101 percent of GDP now and 175 percent by 2056.
  • General revenue and trust funds (inference). Higher output could lift general receipts modestly while the payroll-funded trust funds weaken.

  • Continue. The shift is most urgent. The Anthropic Institute’s extreme case cuts the labor share 14.8 points by 2030.

Safety Net and Income Support

Proposals in 2026 have moved from universal cash toward support that switches on at measured thresholds. Their shared precondition is delivery capacity in the state unemployment systems.

What Is Established

  • Cash experiment. The OpenResearch study paid 1,000 adults $1,000 a month for three years (NBER w32719, revised August 2026). Income apart from the transfer fell about $1,900 a year, and labor market participation fell 4.2 points. The authors found no effect on job quality (figures confirmed on the NBER page, October 7, 2026).
  • Delivery capacity. Anthropic (June 2026) notes that many state unemployment systems buckled under pandemic claim volumes. NELP (September 2026) calls those systems threadbare.
  • Federal warning. Fed Governor Barr (February 17, 2026) said a rapid “jobless boom” would require a restructured safety net.

What Is Contested

  • What the cash result means. Critics read less work with no better jobs as a case against universal income today. Supporters can answer that a market with jobs says little about one where work is scarce (the notes’ inference). OpenResearch’s own summary stresses greater agency in job decisions and more interest in entrepreneurship.
  • New programs or repaired ones. Gimbel argues for stronger unemployment insurance over elaborate new programs. Anthropic, OpenAI and investor Alap Shah propose new layers that activate at thresholds.
  • Universal income. Anthropic lists it only for its most severe tier. Leicht and Ball say polling shows citizens are uninterested in it.

  • Stop. Unemployment insurance repair and entry-level measures stay urgent. Universal floors are less urgent.
  • Pace. Support is still required, and labor triggers can double as gate criteria.
  • Contain and Channel. Transition programs need funding and staff before each gate clearance.
  • Continue. Automatic triggers matter most, because legislation would lag events.

What Comes First (Inference)

  • Before a pause or gate. Modernized unemployment systems and trigger legislation, so support can scale without a new act of Congress.
  • Able to follow. Universal income floors and large public employment programs, which the lab frameworks reserve for severe scenarios.

What Is Unknown

  • Costs. Estimates are absent for nearly all 2026 proposals, the Anthropic tiers and OpenAI triggers among them.
  • Lead time. No estimate exists for modernizing state unemployment systems.
  • Negative income tax. No 2025 or 2026 proposal tied to AI was found.

Sharing the Gains: Public Stakes, Funds and Dividends

Proposals for a public claim on AI returns take three forms: a tax paid in equity, negotiated or purchased equity, and a voluntary pledge. They differ in who bears the dilution and in whether the state gains control or only cash flow.

What Is Established

  • The Intel stake. On August 22, 2025 the government bought 9.9 percent of Intel for $8.9 billion. The stake is passive, with no board seat, and came with conditional five-year warrants for a further 5 percent.
  • Dividend scale. Alaska paid $1,000 per recipient in 2025, about $620 million across 618,863 approved applications.
  • June 2026 exchange. Altman met Sanders and endorsed public equity in principle (Fortune, June 6, 2026); Sanders’s office said Altman committed to none of the senator’s requests. President Trump spoke that week of a possible partnership that benefits Americans (Associated Press). Whether White House meetings followed is Not confirmed.
  • July to September 2026. The Financial Times reported on July 2 that OpenAI had discussed a 5 percent stake for the government, as relayed by Reuters. In that account, leading U.S. AI firms would each place 5 percent of equity in a vehicle like the Alaska Permanent Fund. At an $852 billion valuation, a 5 percent share of OpenAI would be worth about $42.6 billion (CNN). Euronews reported that Sanders considered the offer far too small. The Washington Sun later reported that Altman privately opposed the government holding OpenAI shares (September 9, 2026). The paper added that he backed some version of redistributing company shares to the public.
  • Fund plan. A February 3, 2025 executive order required a sovereign wealth fund plan within 90 days (later status Not confirmed).

What Is Contested

  • Whether the state should hold stakes. Leicht and Ball (June 4, 2026) say government stakes in frontier labs bet on one trajectory for where value lands. Their essay also rejects narrow token taxes, job guarantees and universal income.
  • How large, and who controls. Sanders pressed for 50 percent so the public would hold decision power, and Altman declined that figure. Under S. 4825, a seven-member Independent Commission for Democratic AI would govern the fund and vote its shares. The Intel terms sit at the passive end.

The Two-Lane Plan Beside the Published Proposals

  • Model. It is negotiated equity, like the Intel stake, taken as a condition of federal funding.
  • Use of returns. Returns go to wage insurance and transition support. The Sanders bill authorizes direct payments to the public, and OpenAI says its fund’s returns could go directly to citizens.
  • Closest published relative. New York Assemblyman Alex Bores proposed an “AI Dividend” (Searchlight, June 9, 2026). The federal government would take out-of-the-money warrants in major AI companies. Returns would go to direct payments, workforce transition and public infrastructure for governing AI safely. It has no procurement element and names no wage insurance (see the prior-art review in the Contain and Channel tab).
  • Timing (inference). Bargaining power over equity or warrants is highest before the state commits funding.
  • Strongest objection (inference). Leicht and Ball’s argument applies: a stake bets that value lands at the labs.

How It Differs by Family (Inference)

  • Stop. Windfall mechanisms have less to capture. An approved-model list creates regulatory rents, which supports license fees or a stake set when the list is granted.
  • Pace. Windfall capture stays relevant, and permit auctions become a natural revenue source.
  • Contain and Channel. The claim is strongest, because public money and public risk-bearing support equity as a condition of funding.
  • Continue. The claim must be legislated against industry resistance.

What Is Unknown

  • No total market value for federal corporate stakes was found. Fortune (July 26, 2026) counts about $26.7 billion invested across 30 equity or quasi-equity deals, led by the Intel stake. The Ownership and Industry Structure tab covers the individual stakes.
  • No legal or market analysis of S. 4825’s equity levy was opened.

Competition and Industrial Policy

The notes infer that each family changes what competition policy is for. A frozen list of approved models would turn today’s leaders into licensed incumbents. A contained lane would make entry depend on certification and contracts.

What Is Established

  • Partnership ties. An FTC staff report (January 17, 2025) covered the Microsoft, Amazon and Alphabet partnerships with OpenAI and Anthropic. Cloud providers hold equity and revenue-sharing rights, and developers committed much of the investment to the partner’s cloud.
  • Price pressure. Ramp (September 9, 2026) reports token prices down 41 percent since March 2026.
  • State-backed channel. Executive Order 14363 (November 24, 2025) launched the Genesis Mission. It joins national laboratory supercomputers with private partners for AI-driven research.

What Is Contested

  • Private plurality or public structure. Leicht (March 31, 2026) counts development by several private companies in democracies as a favorable feature of the present. Sitaraman and Pascal argue that utility regulation is compatible with innovation and cite Bell Labs.
  • Restraint and centralization. Carlsmith (March 19, 2026) worries that international restraint regimes push toward centralized development and concentrated government power.
  • Entrenchment under a freeze. The Hardwired Pause working paper (October 2026, unpublished draft) says a whitelist could entrench the leading developers, perhaps permanently. It adds that the whitelisting body would be open to capture.

How It Differs by Family (Inference)

  • Stop. Competition policy becomes franchise regulation: rate regulation, mandatory licensing, separation of model owners from inference providers, and a transparent approval body. The working paper expects rivalry to move to cost and speed, with lower token prices once inference-only chips arrive (§6.2, §3.4.1). Makers of training hardware lose, and owners of approved models gain a protected position.
  • Pace. Compliance costs for compute floors and safety cases favor large labs.
  • Contain and Channel. Certification and anchor procurement decide who can be a frontier developer. The lane would resemble defense procurement or a regulated utility, and public-option arguments move to the center of the design.
  • Continue. Conventional antitrust on partnerships and vertical integration remains the main tool.

What Is Unknown

  • No published analysis covers competition among labs under a contained lane with gated release. The Two-Lane Plan is unpublished, so none is expected yet.
  • Antitrust enforcement actions on AI in 2026 were not searched.
  • The working paper cites a SemiAnalysis estimate that two labs devote 62 percent of compute to research and training (§6.2). That source was not opened.

Capital Markets, Pensions and Savings

Savers are exposed to AI through two channels: index concentration and private credit, where pensions and insurers are major funders. No published dollar estimate of pension, 401(k) or insurer exposure to AI-linked assets was found.

What Is Established

  • Index concentration. The Magnificent Seven are 34.9 percent of S&P 500 market value (Motley Fool Research, updated October 5, 2026). A saver in an S&P 500 index fund therefore holds about a third of that money in seven firms.
  • Return concentration. Cembalest (J.P. Morgan, January 1, 2026) counts 42 AI-related companies behind 65 to 75 percent of S&P 500 returns since November 2022.
  • Build-out. Goldman Sachs (May 1, 2026) puts 2026 AI capital spending at $765 billion. Its 2026 to 2031 total is near $7.6 trillion.
  • Private credit. The Federal Reserve (May 2026) sizes private credit at about $1.4 trillion, or 10 percent of nonfinancial corporate debt. About half of the 20 market contacts it surveyed in March and April 2026 cited AI as a stability risk.
  • Who funds it. A 2024 Fed note put pension funds at 31 percent of private credit capital and insurers at 9 percent. Those shares date from late 2021.

What Is Contested

  • Equity losses from a pause. The Hardwired Pause working paper (October 2026, unpublished draft) models a Magnificent Seven fall to early-2025 values, about $6.5 trillion. It estimates spending would drop about $195 billion, or 0.6 percent of GDP, and calls that an upper bound (§6.1). The scenario is a thought experiment.
  • Where the danger sits. The same paper locates it in debt markets. It lists data center loans from private credit funds, bank credit lines to those funds, and securitized tranches with unknown holders.
  • Whether a pause is the only trigger. Fed Governor Barr (February 17, 2026) describes a stall in which overinvestment fails to earn its return. No pause is involved.
  • Revenue under a freeze. The working paper argues data center revenue may hold, because most enterprise demand is for inference below the frontier. Ramp (September 9, 2026) shows the shift to cheaper models and also revenue pressure from price cuts.

What Is Unknown

  • Dollar exposure. No source quantified pension, 401(k) or insurer holdings of AI-linked equities or data center debt.
  • Fall 2025 survey. The Fed report says more contacts cited AI in spring 2026 than in fall 2025. The fall share is unsettled: machine readings of its charts gave zero and about 35 percent.

Energy and Infrastructure

Data centers used 4.4 percent of U.S. electricity in 2023, and forecasts for 2030 approach 12 percent. Grid plans assume that growth continues, so a stop or strict pace would raise the question of who pays for stranded capacity (inference).

What Is Established

  • Demand. The 4.4 percent and 12 percent figures come from Axios (August 25, 2026). The reading did not name Axios’s source, and the 2030 figure is not reconciled with the Lawrence Berkeley report.
  • Global share. The IEA (2025) puts global data center use at 415 terawatt-hours in 2024, with 45 percent in the United States. It projects about 945 terawatt-hours by 2030.
  • Frontier sites. Epoch AI (updated October 5, 2026) tracks 93 frontier AI sites with 17.9 gigawatts of total facility power.
  • Prices. EIA (October 6, 2026) expects residential prices of 18.2 cents per kilowatt-hour in 2026, against 17.3 cents in 2025. Axios reports that capacity prices in PJM, the largest grid operator, rose elevenfold in recent years.
  • Local opposition. Axios counts at least 75 projects worth about $130 billion delayed in the first quarter of 2026. Data Center Watch counted $64 billion blocked or delayed from May 2024 to March 2025. A typical large project brings about 50 permanent jobs.
  • Share of GDP. Computing infrastructure investment reached about 1.5 percent of GDP in the first quarter of 2026 (Epoch AI, June 5, 2026).

What Is Contested

  • Who pays. Senator Hawley wants data center development halted until companies pay for their own power, grids and water (Associated Press, June 2026). The AI Action Plan (July 2025) takes the opposite emphasis and calls for faster permitting.
  • The forecasts. The IEA says about 20 percent of planned projects could be delayed by grid constraints. Its 2035 range runs from 700 to 1,700 terawatt-hours.
  • Obsolescence. Goldman Sachs (May 1, 2026) flags the risk that equipment becomes obsolete before it is depreciated.
  • Energy under a freeze. The Hardwired Pause working paper (October 2026, unpublished draft) expects inference-only chips to lower energy cost per token (§6.2).

  • Stranded costs. No source estimates them under a stop, or says who would bear them.
