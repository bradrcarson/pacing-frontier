About This Research

The question set behind every entry, what the research covered, and the limits of its evidence.

Standard Question Set

Every proposal should be able to answer these questions. Each has an identifier, so an answer anywhere in the document can point back to its question.

A. Definition and Scope

  • A1. What exactly is restricted: hardware, training, AI-run research, internal use, or public deployment?
  • A2. What threshold defines a covered system, and who sets and revises it?
  • A3. Who is bound: named companies, all developers in one country, a coalition, or every state?
  • A4. Does it cover open-weight models, academic work and government programs?
  • A5. What happens to models and chips that already exist?
  • A6. What stays legal, and is that enough to keep public support?

B. Trigger, Timing and Exit

  • B1. Does it start now, at a capability milestone, or after a defined event?
  • B2. Who decides that the trigger has been met, and on what evidence?
  • B3. How long does it last, and what ends it?
  • B4. What is the standard for proving that it is safe to resume?
  • B5. What happens at exit, and does stockpiled compute produce a sudden jump?
  • B6. Can it be tightened or loosened without new legislation or a new treaty?
  • B7. What does it do if models hide the trigger by underperforming on tests?

C. Verification and Enforcement

  • C1. What must be observed to know the rule is being followed?
  • C2. Which verification methods does it rely on, and are they available today?
  • C3. Who inspects, with what access, and who inspects the inspectors?
  • C4. How much cheating could go undetected, and for how long?
  • C5. What is the penalty for a violation, and who imposes it?
  • C6. Does enforcement require surveillance of chips, data centers or individuals?
  • C7. How does it handle algorithmic progress that shrinks the compute needed?
  • C8. How does it treat internal deployment inside the labs?

D. China and Other Rivals

  • D1. Does it need Beijing’s agreement, and what would Beijing gain from giving it?
  • D2. If China does not join, how long before China passes the constrained U.S. frontier?
  • D3. What does it do about China’s existing chips, domestic production and smuggling?
  • D4. Does it rely on export controls, and how much do those depend on allies?
  • D5. How does it change the risk of conflict, including over Taiwan?
  • D6. What is the response if a rival is found to be racing?
  • D7. Does it make a later agreement easier or harder?

E. Allies and Third Countries

  • E1. Which allied governments must act, and what does it cost them?
  • E2. Can frontier work relocate to a country outside the regime?
  • E3. What do middle powers and developing countries get?
  • E4. Which international body, if any, runs it?

F. National Security

  • F1. What capability do defense and intelligence agencies gain or forgo?
  • F2. Is there a carve-out for government work, and how is it policed?
  • F3. Does it improve or weaken protection of model weights?
  • F4. How does it treat offensive cyber and biological capability?
  • F5. Who in government controls the most capable systems, and under what oversight?

G. Technical Soundness

  • G1. Does it address AI systems that speed up AI research?
  • G2. Does it depend on evaluations that models can game?
  • G3. What does it assume about the pace of algorithmic progress?
  • G4. Does it reduce the chance of losing control, or only delay it?
  • G5. What does it do about models already deployed?
  • G6. What safety research does it enable or block?

H. U.S. Economy

  • H1. What happens to AI investment and data center construction?
  • H2. What happens to technology equity valuations and household wealth?
  • H3. Which lenders and investors are exposed if revenue falls short?
  • H4. What happens to productivity growth, and when?
  • H5. How does it change the speed and pattern of job displacement?
  • H6. What does it cost the federal budget?
  • H7. Which firms and regions gain, and which lose?

I. Global Economy

  • I1. What happens to chip and equipment exporters?
  • I2. Who supplies AI to the rest of the world?
  • I3. Does it split the world into technology blocs?
  • I4. What retaliation is available to states that lose from it?
  • I5. How do financial shocks in the United States transmit abroad?

J. Economic Redesign

  • J1. Does the tax base have to shift away from labor income?
  • J2. What income support or transition programs does it assume?
  • J3. How are the gains from AI shared, and through what instrument?
  • J4. What happens to competition among AI firms, and who regulates prices?
  • J5. What changes for pensions and retirement savings tied to technology stocks?
  • J6. What has to be in place before it starts, and what can follow?

K. Ownership and Industry Structure

  • K1. Who owns and controls frontier developers under the proposal?
  • K2. Does government take equity, a veto, a board seat or outright control?
  • K3. Are firms merged, separated, licensed or frozen in place?
  • K4. Are shareholders, employees and lenders compensated for lost value?
  • K5. Does it entrench today’s leaders, and how is that power checked?
  • K6. What happens to planned public offerings and existing investor agreements?
  • K7. Who owns the chips and data centers?

L. Law and Constitution

  • L1. Under what legal authority can it be done: existing statute, new statute, or treaty?
  • L2. Can the executive branch act alone, and would that survive in court?
  • L3. Does it raise First Amendment, takings or due process claims?
  • L4. Does it preempt state law?
  • L5. Which agency administers it, and does that agency have the staff?

M. Politics

  • M1. Who supports it, who opposes it, and how intensely?
  • M2. What does polling show, and how stable is that support?
  • M3. Does it need a focusing event to pass?
  • M4. Can it survive a change of administration or of control in Congress?
  • M5. How does industry respond, and how much can industry spending change the outcome?

N. Risk and Failure Modes

  • N1. What is the most likely way it fails?
  • N2. What is the worst outcome if it fails?
  • N3. Can it be reversed, and at what cost?
  • N4. Does it create a single point of failure or a single controlling actor?
  • N5. What does it do to civil liberties and privacy?
  • N6. Does a failed attempt make a later attempt harder?

O. Fit With Other Proposals

  • O1. Which other proposals does it require, and which does it rule out?
  • O2. Can it serve as a first step toward a stronger regime?
  • O3. Which parts are worth adopting even if the whole is rejected?

Status and Caveats

The document is a complete draft, built on October 6 and 7, 2026. A second round on October 7 finished most of the checks that the search limit had cut short and corrected the tabs. The document is reliable as a map of the research files behind it. No page has yet been checked by eye against its original.

What Was Done

  • Research. Thirteen research files cover the proposals and the cross-cutting issues. A fourteenth covers Chinese open-weight models. Eleven second-round files hold the checks run on October 7, 2026.
  • Sources. The Bibliography’s topic sections hold 1,280 linked sources: 1,021 opened, 200 listed and 59 taken from other reference lists or blocked. The second round added 505 sources, 438 of them opened. The Bibliography also lists 176 unopened leads from the Hardwired Pause working paper.
  • Drafting. Each tab was written from the research files.
  • Checking. A separate reviewer checked each research tab, the Two-Lane design and this overview against the research files and corrected them. About 240 corrections were made. Most restored a caveat or labeled an inference. After the second round, a separate reviewer rechecked every tab against that round’s notes and made more than 500 further edits.

Limits

  • Machine summaries. Every web page was read through a tool that returns a summary. The document therefore uses no direct quotations, and each figure needs checking against its source before public use.
  • Search ran out. The search budget was exhausted during the first round. The second round ran the prior-art searches and settled many items marked “Not confirmed.” Each tab lists what stayed open.
  • Blocked sources. Several newspapers and official pages refused automated access. The Bibliography lists them under Sources Still to Obtain.
  • An unpublished draft. The Hardwired Pause working paper is unfinished, and its figures are draft estimates.
  • Inference. Comparisons across the four families in the economic, ownership and history tabs are this document’s own inference, and they are labeled. Almost no published work compares the families.
  • The unpublished proposals. Details of Brad Carson’s proposals beyond their stated terms were drafted in this project and await his confirmation.
  • Claims of absence. A statement that no source was found reflects public web search. Private drafts, paywalled pages and legal databases were out of reach.
