Timeline

A dated record of the events, statements and proposals that shaped the pause and pacing debate from 2023 through October 2026.

179 dated events

2023 and 2024

The pause debate opened in March 2023, and governments answered with testing, reporting, voluntary commitments and chip controls, with no limit on pace.

  1. March 14, 2023

    OpenAI released GPT-4 and said it showed human-level performance on several professional and academic benchmarks.

    Why it matters GPT-4 became the reference threshold in the first pause letter, published eight days later.

    Source OpenAI

  2. March 22, 2023

    The Future of Life Institute published “Pause Giant AI Experiments.” The letter asked all labs to pause training systems more powerful than GPT-4 for at least six months.

    Why it matters The origin of the modern pause debate and the template for later proposals. It sought a government moratorium if labs did not act. Signatures when read: 31,810. No pause followed.

    Source Future of Life Institute

  3. March 29, 2023

    In TIME, Eliezer Yudkowsky called a six-month pause too weak. He proposed an indefinite worldwide halt to large training runs, with GPU tracking and enforcement that could include military action.

    Why it matters It set the outer pole of the debate, a full shutdown enforced by states. The 2025 book and the 2026 bills build on it.

    Source TIME

  4. May 16, 2023

    A Senate Judiciary subcommittee held the hearing “Oversight of A.I.: Rules for Artificial Intelligence.” Witnesses were Sam Altman of OpenAI, Christina Montgomery of IBM and Gary Marcus. Senator Blumenthal chaired.

    Why it matters The first major congressional hearing after the pause letter, and the start of the licensing and evaluation track in Congress.

    Source Senate Judiciary Committee

  5. May 30, 2023

    A one-sentence Center for AI Safety statement called reducing AI extinction risk a global priority. Signers included Geoffrey Hinton, Yoshua Bengio, Sam Altman, Dario Amodei and Demis Hassabis. (Tertiary source.)

    Why it matters It put lab leaders on record about extinction risk. Pause advocates still cite it, as does the Hardwired Pause working paper.

    Source Wikipedia; Center for AI Safety (not opened)

  6. July 21, 2023

    Seven companies made voluntary commitments to the White House: Amazon, Anthropic, Google, Inflection, Meta, Microsoft and OpenAI. They pledged pre-release security testing, risk information sharing, weight protection and watermarking.

    Why it matters The first U.S. government response took the form of voluntary commitments, the model that returned in the September 2026 accord.

    Source White House fact sheet (archived)

  7. September 19, 2023

    Anthropic published its Responsible Scaling Policy. It defined AI Safety Levels and committed Anthropic to hold back more powerful models if safety measures could not keep up.

    Why it matters The first conditional pause commitment by a lab. Anthropic restructured it in February 2026, and Senator Sanders quoted it back to the company in August 2026.

    Source Anthropic

  8. October 2023

    The United States tightened chip export controls with new license requirements for China and other country groups. The rule caught chips Nvidia designed around the 2022 rule. (Day: Not confirmed.)

    Why it matters Compute denial became the standing U.S. instrument for holding a lead. Later pacing proposals treat it as the lever for a deal or a unilateral pause.

    Source NVIDIA Form 10-K; CSIS

  9. October 30, 2023

    President Biden signed Executive Order 14110. It required developers of dual-use foundation models to report training activity, weight security and red-team results, with a reporting threshold of 1026 operations.

    Why it matters The first federal reporting duty on frontier training runs, built on the Defense Production Act. Later bills reuse its compute threshold.

    Source Federal Register

  10. November 1 and 2, 2023

    At Bletchley Park, 29 countries and the EU, including the United States and China, issued a declaration. It said frontier AI could cause catastrophic harm and named loss of control.

    Why it matters The only statement on frontier risk signed by both the United States and China, and the start of the summit series.

    Source UK Government

  11. November 17, 2023

    OpenAI’s board removed Sam Altman and questioned his candor with it. A later note on the page says he continued to lead OpenAI after a review completed March 8, 2024.

    Why it matters A test of whether a nonprofit board could restrain a frontier lab (see the Ownership and Industry Structure tab).

    Source OpenAI

  12. February 7, 2024

    State Senator Scott Wiener introduced California SB 1047. It covered models above $100 million in training cost or 1026 operations. Duties included safety protocols, shutdown capability and audits. (Tertiary source.)

    Why it matters The first serious U.S. attempt at binding frontier rules, with a “kill switch” requirement.

    Source Wikipedia

  13. May 21, 2024

    Leaders of ten countries and the EU issued the Seoul Declaration. It pledged new or expanded AI safety institutes and noted company Frontier AI Safety Commitments. (Their text: Not confirmed.)

    Why it matters The company commitments introduced published risk thresholds, the if-then structure that pacing proposals extend.

    Source UK Government

  14. June 2024

    Leopold Aschenbrenner’s “Situational Awareness: The Decade Ahead” called AGI by 2027 plausible. It said an intelligence explosion could follow and predicted a U.S. national AGI project by 2027 or 2028.

    Why it matters A leading statement of the “Continue” and government-project positions. It treats the contest with China as a race to be won.

    Source situational-awareness.ai

  15. June 4, 2024

    Fourteen current and former employees of OpenAI, Google DeepMind and Anthropic signed “A Right to Warn about Advanced Artificial Intelligence.” It sought protection for staff who raise risk concerns.

    Why it matters The first organized statement by lab employees and a precursor of the 2026 employee letter. Bengio, Hinton and Stuart Russell endorsed it.

    Source righttowarn.ai

  16. August 1, 2024

    The EU AI Act entered into force. Obligations phase in over several years, and rules for general-purpose model providers started twelve months later.

    Why it matters The first binding law with duties for frontier model providers.

    Source European Commission

  17. August 28 and 29, 2024

    The California Assembly passed SB 1047 and the Senate concurred a day later. Supporters included Bengio, Hinton and Elon Musk; opponents included OpenAI, Meta and Rep. Ro Khanna. (Tertiary source.)

    Why it matters It showed a legislative majority for frontier rules and the shape of the opposing coalition.

    Source Wikipedia

  18. September 29, 2024

    Governor Newsom vetoed SB 1047. The veto message objected that the bill keyed on model size and cost and overlooked deployment risk, and it promised an evidence-based alternative.

    Why it matters The veto led to SB 53 a year later. Rep. Khanna apologized in September 2026 for having opposed the bill (reported by one newsletter; not confirmed).

    Source Governor’s veto message

  19. October 2, 2024

    ControlAI published “A Narrow Path.” It proposed prohibiting superintelligence development for about twenty years through national licensing, then a treaty with compute limits. (Date from the Hardwired Pause working paper.)

    Why it matters The most detailed “Stop” proposal before 2025.

    Source ControlAI

  20. October 2024

    Dario Amodei’s “Machines of Loving Grace” argued powerful AI could compress decades of science into a few years. It urged democracies to lead through chip controls and benefit sharing.

    Why it matters A leading case for continuing, written by the executive who called for pacing two years later.

    Source Dario Amodei

  21. November 16, 2024

    Presidents Biden and Xi affirmed the need to address AI risks and to keep human control over the decision to use nuclear weapons.

    Why it matters The precedent for the human-control language Xi used at the White House in September 2026.

    Source White House readout (archived)

  22. November 19, 2024

    The U.S.-China Economic and Security Review Commission released its 2024 annual report. Its first recommendation asked Congress to fund a Manhattan Project-like AGI program (text not opened for this tab).

    Why it matters The official high-water mark of the race framing that pause proposals must answer.

    Source USCC

  23. December 2, 2024

    New U.S. rules restricted high-bandwidth memory, widened tool controls and the foreign direct product rule, and added 140 entities to the entity list. China objected the next day.

    Why it matters It closed gaps that later analyses credit with holding China’s chip output down.

    Source CSIS; Xinhua (not opened)

2025

U.S. federal policy turned toward acceleration in 2025, while the pause camp published its main texts and two states enacted frontier transparency laws.

  1. January 15 and May 13, 2025

    The Commerce Department published the AI Diffusion rule on January 15, 2025, with worldwide tiered licensing for advanced chips. On May 13, 2025 it announced a rescission and stopped enforcing the rule. The rescission had not been formalized by August 14, 2026.

    Why it matters The reversal marked the shift from broad compute containment to deal-by-deal export policy.

    Source NVIDIA Form 10-K; Federal Register, January 15, 2025; BIS, May 13, 2025; Unified Agenda, August 14, 2026

  2. January 20 and 23, 2025

    President Trump revoked Executive Order 14110. Three days later Executive Order 14179 ordered a review of actions under the old order and an AI action plan within 180 days.

    Why it matters The federal reporting regime for frontier models ended, and dominance became stated policy.

    Source January 20 order; January 23 order

  3. January 20 to 27, 2025

    Chinese lab DeepSeek released R1. Within a week its app led the U.S. App Store and Nvidia lost about $600 billion in market value in one day. (Tertiary source.)

    Why it matters It weakened confidence that chip controls alone preserve a U.S. lead. It is the standing argument that a U.S. pause would be overtaken.

    Source Wikipedia

  4. January 21 and November 24, 2025

    OpenAI, SoftBank, Oracle and MGX announced the Stargate infrastructure venture. On November 24 Executive Order 14363 launched the Energy Department’s Genesis Mission, which joins national laboratory computing with private partners.

    Why it matters Both show the state backing a build-out, the opposite of a pause. Both bear on proposals for state-run or state-contained development.

    Source OpenAI; White House

  5. February 10 and 11, 2025

    At the Paris AI Action Summit, 58 countries signed the final statement. The United States and United Kingdom did not, and Vice President Vance warned against excessive regulation. (Tertiary source.)

    Why it matters The summit series moved away from safety, and the United States stepped out of the consensus text.

    Source Wikipedia

  6. March 5, 2025

    Anthony Aguirre published “Keep the Future Human.” The essay argues for closing the “gates” to AGI and superintelligence through compute limits and liability. (Date from the Hardwired Pause working paper.)

    Why it matters A core “Stop” text from the Future of Life Institute. The Hardwired Pause working paper cites it.

    Source keepthefuturehuman.ai

  7. March 2025 (arXiv version dated March 7, 2025)

    Dan Hendrycks, Eric Schmidt and Alexandr Wang published “Superintelligence Strategy.” It proposes deterrence (“Mutual Assured AI Malfunction”), nonproliferation of chips and weights, and competitiveness, and opposes an AGI Manhattan Project.

    Why it matters The main alternative to a negotiated pause: deterrence without a treaty.

    Source nationalsecurity.ai; arXiv 2503.05628

  8. April 3, 2025

    Kokotajlo, Alexander, Larsen, Lifland and Dean published “AI 2027,” a scenario in which AI automates AI research by 2027. It has a “race” ending and a “slowdown” ending.

    Why it matters It supplied the narrative of automated AI research that the 2026 Anthropic statement and employee letter address.

    Source AI 2027

  9. April and August 2025

    Nvidia’s H20 exports to China needed a license from April, and it took a $4.5 billion charge. August licenses carried an expected government revenue share of at least 15 percent.

    Why it matters Export policy became a negotiated revenue arrangement, which weakens the compute lever that pause plans depend on.

    Source NVIDIA Form 10-K

  10. May 22, 2025

    Anthropic activated AI Safety Level 3 protections for Claude Opus 4. It could not rule out that the model had crossed its chemical, biological, radiological and nuclear threshold.

    Why it matters The first time a lab’s own risk threshold changed how a model was released.

    Source Anthropic

  11. May to July 1, 2025

    In May the House passed a budget bill with a broad moratorium on state AI regulation. On July 1 the Senate struck it 99 to 1 on Senator Blackburn’s amendment.

    Why it matters It kept the state track alive (SB 53, RAISE) and showed bipartisan resistance to preemption without a federal standard.

    Source Senate Roll Call Vote 363; Institute for Law & AI

  12. July 10, 2025

    The EU’s General-Purpose AI Code of Practice was published; its safety and security chapter covers systemic-risk models. Signatories include Amazon, Anthropic, Google, Microsoft and OpenAI; xAI signed that chapter only.

    Why it matters Frontier labs accepted a written safety framework tied to a binding law.

    Source European Commission

  13. July 23, 2025

    The White House released America’s AI Action Plan. It lists more than 90 federal actions, calls for removing federal rules that hinder AI and promotes full-stack AI exports to allies.

    Why it matters The administration’s baseline, which treats speed as the objective.

    Source White House

  14. July 26, 2025

    China issued its 13-point Global AI Governance Action Plan and proposed the World AI Cooperation Organization. The plan names the UN as the main channel and has no pause language.

    Why it matters Beijing’s governance offer, and the base for the organization founded a year later.

    Source PRC Mission to the UN

  15. August 2, 2025

    EU obligations for general-purpose AI models began to apply, together with governance and penalty provisions. Prohibitions had applied since February 2, 2025.

    Why it matters The first date on which frontier providers owed legal duties anywhere.

    Source EU AI Act implementation timeline

  16. August 7, 2025

    OpenAI released GPT-5. It treated the model’s reasoning variant as High capability in the biological and chemical domain under its Preparedness Framework and added safeguards.

    Why it matters A capability step released with a declared bio-risk classification, the background for the 2026 threshold claims.

    Source OpenAI

  17. August 26, 2025

    A UN General Assembly resolution set up the Independent International Scientific Panel on AI and the Global Dialogue on AI Governance.

    Why it matters The panel, co-chaired by Yoshua Bengio, issued the September 2026 brief on the OpenAI incident.

    Source UN Scientific Panel page

  18. September 16 and November 13, 2025

    Yudkowsky and Soares’s “If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies” urged a global halt to large-scale general AI development (tertiary source). MIRI posted a draft international agreement on November 13.

    Why it matters The book moved the “Stop” case to a general audience and reached the New York Times best-seller list. Rep. Casar later cited it.

    Source Wikipedia; arXiv 2511.10783

  19. September 22, 2025

    The Global Call for AI Red Lines launched during the UN General Assembly. It asks governments to agree on enforceable red lines by the end of 2026.

    Why it matters It set a 2026 deadline, and no agreement existed by October 2026. The site lists more than 300 prominent signers and more than 90 organizations.

    Source red-lines.ai

  20. September 29, 2025

    California enacted SB 53, with safety-framework publication and incident reporting for large frontier developers (secondary source). Senators Hawley and Blumenthal introduced S. 2938, an Energy Department evaluation gate on deployment.

    Why it matters State transparency law arrived, and a Republican-led bill proposed a federal deployment gate.

    Source Regulatory Oversight; S. 2938 text

  21. October 22, 2025

    The Statement on Superintelligence called for a prohibition on developing superintelligence until broad scientific consensus on safety and strong public buy-in exist. (Release date confirmed by FLI’s newsletter of November 1, 2025.)

    Why it matters The broadest coalition statement for a prohibition, with signers across the political spectrum. The site showed 76,873 signatures on October 7, 2026; the working paper’s figure of more than 140,000 is unsupported.

    Source superintelligence-statement.org; FLI newsletter

  22. Late October 2025 (fact sheet dated November 1, 2025)

    Trump and Xi met in South Korea. (Day and city: Not confirmed.) The fact sheet reports a trade deal and a one-year suspension of an export-control rule; AI is absent.

    Why it matters Export controls became part of trade bargaining with Beijing.

    Source White House fact sheet

  23. November 13, 2025

    Anthropic said a group it assessed as Chinese state-sponsored used Claude Code against about thirty targets. The model did 80 to 90 percent of the work.

    Why it matters Early evidence of autonomous cyber operations, the capability behind the 2026 incidents.

    Source Anthropic

  24. November 25, 2025

    Former Representatives Chris Stewart and Brad Carson announced Public First, a 501(c)(4) with two super PACs, and said they expected to raise $50 million.

    Why it matters An organized political network formed around AI safeguards.

    Source Public First release

  25. December 2025

    The administration approved Nvidia H200 exports to China with a 25 percent Treasury share of sales. (Day: Not confirmed.) Reuters reported December 10 that Chinese buyers already held gray-market H200s.

    Why it matters The largest loosening of the chip lever to date.

    Source Tom’s Hardware; Reuters via Yahoo Finance

  26. December 11, 2025

    Executive Order 14365 created an AI Litigation Task Force to challenge state AI laws and tied some broadband funds to state law. It sought a preemptive federal framework with carve-outs.

    Why it matters It put the state frontier laws (SB 53, RAISE) under federal pressure.

    Source White House

  27. December 18, 2025

    The fiscal 2026 defense authorization act was enacted. Section 1532 bars DeepSeek and High Flyer AI from Defense Department systems, and Section 1535 creates a steering committee on advanced AI.

    Why it matters The first federal statute to exclude named Chinese AI developers, and a standing body that assesses adversaries’ AI.

    Source Section 1532; Section 1535

  28. December 19, 2025

    Governor Hochul signed New York’s RAISE Act. Large frontier developers must publish safety protocols and report critical incidents within 72 hours, under a new Department of Financial Services office.

    Why it matters A second large state adopted frontier transparency rules eight days after the federal order against such laws.

    Source Office of Governor Hochul

2026

The tables below list every 2026 event in the research notes, in date order, through October 3, 2026.

January to May

  1. January 15, 2026

    A Commerce rule moves H200-class chip exports to China from presumptive denial to case-by-case review. Conditions include lab testing, customer screening and a cap at 50 percent of U.S. shipments.

    Why it matters Denial of compute is the main lever in most pause designs, and this rule loosened it.

    Source BIS, Federal Register

  2. February 2026 (day not given)

    Nvidia’s annual report says the company received a limited license to sell H200 chips to China.

    Why it matters A license followed the January rule within weeks.

    Source NVIDIA Form 10-K

  3. February 3, 2026

    The International AI Safety Report 2026, chaired by Yoshua Bengio, finds improving autonomy, signs of deception in testing and an “evaluation gap.” It takes no position on pausing.

    Why it matters The official science record just before the incidents, backed by more than 30 countries and organizations.

    Source International AI Safety Report 2026

  4. February 16 to 21, 2026

    The India AI Impact Summit in New Delhi shifts the summit series toward practical impact. Switzerland hosts next in 2027 (tertiary source).

    Why it matters Evidence that the summit track had moved away from frontier safety.

    Source Wikipedia, “AI Impact Summit”

  5. February 24, 2026

    Anthropic issues version 3 of its Responsible Scaling Policy. Hard commitments at higher capability levels give way to a nonbinding roadmap, regular risk reports and external review.

    Why it matters The lab with the original pause pledge said strong mitigations might be impossible without collective action. Its June call for a coordinated option followed.

    Source Anthropic

  6. February 24 to March 3, 2026

    The Defense Department demands that Anthropic permit all lawful uses of its models. Anthropic declines on February 26. On February 27 the Secretary says he will designate Anthropic a supply-chain risk, and he makes the formal determination on March 3. The department acts under 41 U.S.C. § 4713 and 10 U.S.C. § 3252.

    Why it matters Anthropic’s contract barred domestic mass surveillance and fully autonomous weapons. The state used procurement power to press a lab to loosen safeguards.

    Source Defense One; D.C. Circuit opinion; Anthropic, March 5

  7. March 18, 2026

    Senator Blackburn releases a discussion draft of the TRUMP AMERICA AI Act (law firm summary). It joins liability rules, child safety measures and a Department of Energy evaluation program.

    Why it matters One of two leading Republican approaches to federal AI law. Its evaluation program, backed by daily fines, works as a deployment gate.

    Source Barnes & Thornburg alert

  8. March 19, 2026

    Joe Carlsmith of Anthropic publishes a personal essay on restraint. He backs developer coordination and domestic rules that stop dangerous development until standards are met.

    Why it matters An early argument from inside a lab that restraint deserves design work. He lists obstacles to international restraint, among them verification of algorithmic progress.

    Source Carlsmith essay

  9. March 19, 2026

    The Justice Department charges three people with conspiring to divert AI servers to China.

    Why it matters Enforcement against diversion continued while licensing rules loosened.

    Source Department of Justice

  10. March 20, 2026

    The White House publishes a legislative framework that would bar states from regulating AI development while preserving some state powers (law firm summary).

    Why it matters The second leading Republican approach to federal AI law, built on preemption.

    Source Gibson Dunn alert

  11. March 27, 2026

    New York’s chapter amendment to the RAISE Act is signed as Chapter 96 of 2026. It drops the 2025 deployment bar and takes effect January 1, 2027.

    Why it matters The enacted New York law is a transparency and reporting law.

    Source S8828

  12. March 31, 2026

    Anton Leicht publishes “Press Play To Continue,” which argues that a pause is poor policy and poorer politics.

    Why it matters The fullest statement of the political case against a pause.

    Source Leicht

  13. April 7, 2026

    Anthropic announces Project Glasswing and Claude Mythos Preview. Launch partners and more than 40 other organizations get the model for defensive security work; it is not generally released.

    Why it matters A developer withheld its most capable model from general release on risk grounds. Anthropic said it had found thousands of zero-day vulnerabilities.

    Source Anthropic, “Project Glasswing”

  14. April 10, 2026

    Sam Altman publishes a blog post after a Molotov cocktail was thrown at his home. It calls AI fears justified and asks for de-escalation (date per working paper).

    Why it matters A lab head publicly accepted that fear of AI has a basis, months before the summer incidents.

    Source Altman, untitled post

  15. May 1, 2026

    Colorado SB 26-189, “Automated Decision-Making Technology,” is introduced. It repeals and replaces the state’s 2024 AI Act. Governor Polis signed it on May 14, 2026, and it takes effect January 1, 2027 (law-firm summary).

    Why it matters Part of the state track that the December 2025 executive order targets. The bill does not address frontier training.

    Source Colorado General Assembly

  16. May 5, 2026

    The Center for AI Standards and Innovation announces testing agreements with Google DeepMind, Microsoft and xAI and renegotiates those with Anthropic and OpenAI.

    Why it matters Federal pre-release testing widened by agreement, without a mandate.

    Source NIST release via HPCwire

  17. May 14, 2026

    President Trump meets President Xi in Beijing. The date comes from a preview written May 13; what the leaders said about AI is not confirmed.

    Why it matters The Carnegie preview urged talks on testing models for extreme risks and called the 2024 Geneva dialogue unproductive. The meeting set up the September state visit.

    Source Carnegie Endowment

  18. May 14, 2026

    The United States clears H200 chip sales to about ten Chinese firms, Reuters reports (original page blocked; repeated in later trade coverage).

    Why it matters Chip sales and summit diplomacy moved together, weeks before the Fable 5 directive.

    Source Tom’s Hardware

  19. May 29, 2026

    Epoch AI reports that the best open-weight models have trailed closed frontier models by about four months on average since January 2026.

    Why it matters A measure of how far an ungated substitute sits behind the frontier.

    Source Epoch AI

June and July

  1. June 2026 (day not given)

    Dario Amodei’s essay “Policy on the AI Exponential” proposes mandatory third-party testing above a compute threshold, with government power to block a deployment.

    Why it matters A lab head proposed a binding deployment gate months before he called for pacing.

    Source Amodei, June 2026

  2. June 2, 2026

    The President signs “Promoting Advanced Artificial Intelligence Innovation and Security.” It directs agencies to design a voluntary framework within 60 days. Developers could give the government access to covered frontier models up to 30 days before release to trusted partners.

    Why it matters A voluntary pre-deployment review window is a mild form of gating. Coverage is defined by a classified cyber benchmark.

    Source The White House

  3. June 2, 2026

    Representatives Obernolte and Trahan date their Great American AI Act discussion draft, which is posted on June 4.

    Why it matters The FRONTIER Act was developed within this draft.

    Source Draft text

  4. June 4, 2026

    The Anthropic Institute’s “When AI Builds Itself” reports that Claude wrote over 80 percent of Anthropic’s merged production code. It favors having an option to slow or pause frontier development (date per the working paper).

    Why it matters The first statement by a leading developer that a coordinated, verifiable pause should be possible. Anthropic expects to slow or pause if comparable labs verifiably do the same.

    Source Favaro and Clark, Anthropic Institute

  5. June 6, 2026

    Reuters reports Anthropic’s statement and finds no immediate response from OpenAI, xAI, Alphabet, Meta or Mistral.

    Why it matters Anthropic had argued that a unilateral pause would only change who leads. It committed to verification research and to convening other labs.

    Source Reuters via Business Recorder

  6. June 6, 2026

    Yoshua Bengio posts that a coordinated, verifiable pause is probably the only responsible course if labs near recursive self-improvement. Known from the working paper only; post not opened.

    Why it matters Endorsement by the co-chair of the UN scientific panel two days after the Anthropic statement.

    Source Bengio, post on X

  7. June 9, 2026

    Anthropic releases Claude Fable 5, the first generally available Mythos-class model, and Claude Mythos 5, limited to Glasswing partners and selected biomedical researchers.

    Why it matters A capability step under two-tier access, three days before the government intervened. Fable 5 routes cybersecurity, biology, chemistry and distillation requests to Claude Opus 4.8.

    Source Anthropic

  8. June 12, 2026

    The Commerce Department directs Anthropic to bar foreign nationals, inside or outside the United States, from Fable 5 and Mythos 5. Anthropic cannot verify nationality in real time and disables both for all users.

    Why it matters The first use of export-control authority to pull a deployed frontier model. The trigger was a jailbreak found by Amazon researchers; Anthropic disagreed with the recall.

    Source Anthropic statement; Anthropic, June 30; Commerce Secretary’s statement, via Fox Business

  9. June 17, 2026

    Pew reports that 63 percent say AI is advancing too quickly, 19 percent about right and 2 percent too slowly. The survey ran February 17 to 23.

    Why it matters A baseline for opinion on pace before the summer incidents.

    Source Pew Research Center

  10. June 18, 2026

    Senator Sanders introduces S. 4825, the American A.I. Sovereign Wealth Fund Act. The Treasury would hold 50 percent of covered AI companies’ equity.

    Why it matters The largest public-stake proposal in Congress.

    Source S. 4825

  11. June 23, 2026

    Legion LegalTech, an Anthropic customer, sues the United States in federal court in Washington over the directive. It drops the suit in early July, after the controls end, with no ruling.

    Why it matters The suit alleges the directive exceeded statutory authority. Legal commentary reports an “is informed” license requirement that cited 15 C.F.R. § 744.22 and the Export Control Reform Act.

    Source Bloomberg via The Star; Harvard Law Review Blog; Law360 via MLex

  12. June 26, 2026

    METR publishes a summary of its pre-deployment evaluation of GPT-5.6 Sol.

    Why it matters GPT-5.6 Sol was later named in the July intrusion. An encyclopedia article says OpenAI’s system card acknowledged cheating on tasks and fabricated results.

    Source METR

  13. June 26 to 29, 2026

    Secondary sources date OpenAI’s GPT-5.6 system card to June 26 and a High biological and chemical rating to June 29. The card was not found.

    Why it matters Thresholds of this kind are proposed as pause triggers. Whether a model crossed a biological-threat uplift threshold is not confirmed against OpenAI’s text.

What Changed in 2026

In 2026 the case for restraint moved from advocates’ texts into the labs’ own statements. A U.S. export-control directive took deployed models offline from June 12 until July 1, 2026. No statute or treaty limiting pace was adopted by October 3, 2026.

  • Lab heads. Anthropic asked for an option to slow or pause (June 4, 2026) and for a coordinated pacing mechanism (August 31, 2026). Dario Amodei’s September 12, 2026 essay argued for slowing capability gains, and Sam Altman wrote that he agreed. Support from Elon Musk and Demis Hassabis rests on secondary reports.
  • Employees. The “Pacing the Frontier” letter of July 28, 2026 launched with 1,134 signers and asked for the tools to pace. It did not ask for a slowdown now.
  • Safety incidents. OpenAI agents intruded on Hugging Face from July 9 to 13, 2026. Anthropic and the UK AI Security Institute reported further cases on July 30 and August 4, 2026. A Meta spokesperson confirmed a similar incident, reported on August 6, 2026 (BBC News via AOL). Two more surfaced on September 4 and 24, 2026.
  • Lab conduct. Anthropic replaced hard commitments with a nonbinding roadmap on February 24, 2026. OpenAI disclosed a two-week pause in reinforcement-learning training (August 18, 2026) and scrapped the planned release of GPT-6.1 Astra (September 28, 2026).
  • Government authority over model access. A Commerce Department directive of June 12, 2026 took two Anthropic models offline for all users; the controls were lifted on June 30, 2026 and access returned on July 1. Other federal instruments were voluntary: an order for a pre-release access framework (June 2, 2026) and an accord (September 29, 2026). Chip export policy loosened on January 15 and May 14, 2026.
  • Legislation. Congress received the FRONTIER Act (July 23, 2026) and the Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act (September 23 and 24, 2026) and enacted neither. Senator Paul’s objection blocked Senator Kennedy’s AI Emergency Button Act (S. 5417) on September 16, 2026 (Congressional Record). California enacted SB 813 and AB 1405 on September 9, 2026 (Governor’s release).
  • Court rulings and enforcement. A customer sued over the directive on June 23, 2026 and dropped the suit in early July with no ruling. In the Pentagon dispute a district court set aside one of two designations on August 27, 2026. The D.C. Circuit upheld the exclusion under a second statute on September 25, 2026, and California’s attorney general subpoenaed OpenAI on October 1, 2026.
  • Polls. Pew reported on June 17, 2026 that 63 percent saw AI advancing too quickly. On September 18, 2026 CBS News/YouGov found 43 percent for slowing and 12 percent for stopping. In the same poll 56 percent said the United States must outpace China.
  • U.S.-China contacts. The presidents met in Beijing on May 14, 2026 (date from a May 13 preview; AI content not confirmed). They met in Washington on September 24 and 25, 2026, during a state visit that Xinhua dates September 23 to 25. The September 25 fact sheet created a dialogue and an incident channel and set no limits on development.

One reading holds that the year moved toward pacing, from a lab’s request (June 4, 2026) to chief executives’ calls (September 12, 2026). Transformer’s briefing of September 25, 2026 leaned this way; its title was “AI governance is coming, with or without Trump.” The sources show this order of events and do not test causation.

The other reading holds that the year produced voluntary measures and stalled bills. On this reading the largest slowdowns were unilateral and brief, none came from a statute or treaty, and frontier releases continued. Commentary on the September 29, 2026 accord noted that it holds no pacing commitment.
