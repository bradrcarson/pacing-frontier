The Case Against Pausing

This tab sets out the strongest arguments against pausing or pacing frontier AI, with the best replies to each.

20 entries · 132 open and closed questions

Overview

Opposition to pausing or pacing frontier AI comes from four camps that disagree with each other. On the researcher’s reading, most of their arguments target a unilateral or fixed-term pause, and most current proposals are mutual, conditional, verified or hardware-based.

  • Classical liberals. Dean W. Ball, Adam Thierer, Tyler Cowen and Robin Hanson rank state control as a larger danger than AI.
  • Normal-technology empiricists. Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor hold that AI diffuses slowly and that resilience and sector regulation fit the risk (“AI as Normal Technology”, April 15, 2025).
  • Accelerationists and industry. Marc Andreessen, Jensen Huang and Neil Chilson oppose slowing. The Trump administration’s public line frames AI as a race to win (AI Action Plan, July 2025).
  • Safety-minded critics of pause tactics. Anton Leicht, Nora Belrose and Matthew Barnett argue from inside the safety debate that a pause would do more harm than good. Holden Karnofsky doubts partial pauses only.

This tab treats eight arguments at length and twelve in compact form, and its research judges three strongest as of October 2026. They are the China asymmetry, verification of algorithmic progress, and concentrated or arbitrary state power. That research judges the claim that the risk is speculative weakest after the summer 2026 incidents, and no source ranks the arguments this way.

Several of the most cited opponents moved toward oversight in 2026. Kapoor and Narayanan wrote on September 14, 2026 that safety is not currently on track (AI as Normal Technology). Leicht said in an interview published September 15, 2026 that, in an ideal case, time bought by a pause could be used well. He spoke on technical grounds and assumed that markets and geopolitics were also frozen (about 13:18; The Cognitive Revolution).

Almost every opponent accepts some conditional measure. The common ground is independent evaluation of labs, incident reporting, liability and transparency. The dividing line is whether an outside party may bind the rate or level of capability, and whether compute may be monitored.

Five of the first six arguments in the table bear hardest on Stop proposals (researcher’s reading). Concentration of power is the exception and bears hardest on Contain and Channel, the Two-Lane Plan included.

The China Argument

Claim

Frontier AI is the one field where the United States leads China, and the lead is measured in months. A pause that China does not match gives the lead to the less cautious actor, which leaves everyone less safe. Even a mutual freeze on frontier training is lopsided, because China keeps advancing in chips while the U.S. advantage stands still.

Who Makes It

  • Anton Leicht, in “Press Play To Continue” (March 31, 2026) and on The Cognitive Revolution (September 15, 2026), where he gave the lopsided-deal version.
  • Tyler Cowen (Marginal Revolution, March 27, 2023) and Marc Andreessen (“Why AI Will Save the World”, June 6, 2023), both before 2025.
  • The White House AI Action Plan (July 2025), Treasury Secretary Bessent (Bloomberg via Yahoo Finance, September 9, 2026) and President Trump, as reported by CBS News (October 1, 2026).
  • Daniel Castro (ITIF, September 16, 2026), who writes that Beijing has rejected slowdown calls.

Evidence Offered

  • The AI Futures Project, a pacing proponent, puts the raw U.S. lead at about 4 to 8 months (“How to Pace the US Frontier”, August 7, 2026).
  • The FBI, NSA and CISA said Chinese firms extracted billions of tokens from U.S. frontier models since late 2024 (Bloomberg via Yahoo Finance, September 9, 2026). The primary document is joint advisory AA26-251A from the NSA, CISA and the FBI, dated September 8, 2026. That page was blocked to the fetch tool; Nextgov reported the advisory the same day.
  • Sayash Kapoor and Arvind Narayanan write that nation-state adversaries can match frontier capabilities within months (AI as Normal Technology, May 21, 2026).
  • Most Americans think the United States must develop AI faster than China (CBS News/YouGov, September 16 to 18, 2026). The topline gives 56 percent overall, 74 percent of Republicans and 45 percent of Democrats.

Best Replies

  • Current proposals are mutual. Scott Alexander’s dialogue has the opponent attack a unilateral pause six times while the supporter describes a verified treaty with China (“Every Debate On Pausing AI”, March 25, 2026).
  • The lead can absorb pacing. The AI Futures Project estimates China needs about a year to catch up after a U.S. halt. The United States can speed up again if China nears the lead.
  • The race has no winner if control fails. Katja Grace (AI Impacts, December 22, 2022), Max Tegmark (LessWrong, October 13, 2024) and Anthony Aguirre (“Keep the Future Human”, March 5, 2025) argue that race logic assumes the winner can control AGI. Joe Carlsmith (essay of March 19, 2026) and the Hardwired Pause working paper (October 2026, unpublished draft, §2.3) model matched restraint as a repeated stag hunt.
  • China’s position is contested. Xi Jinping said at the White House on September 24, 2026 that AI development must always stay under human control (PRC Foreign Ministry). Castro reports that Beijing rejected slowdown calls; the news report behind that statement was not opened. On September 14, 2026 a foreign ministry spokesperson said fear-mongering would disrupt global AI governance (AP via GPB). The Chinese readout of the talks names no pause or slowdown, so China’s position on a mutual slowdown is not confirmed here (see the China and Geopolitics tab).
  • Pacing can accept the premise. Dario Amodei calls a Chinese lead a grave danger (“We Must Pace the Frontier”, September 2026). He treats export controls, anti-distillation measures and lab security as preconditions.
  • Concessions on both sides. Carlsmith grants that ceding advantage to authoritarian states could make restraint net negative. Leicht said that a deal letting China catch up on chips would be somewhat unfavorable to the United States (interview published September 15, 2026). He added that it would still be very favorable for the world on risk. He said he would be happy to take that deal (re-read October 7, 2026 through the fetch tool, at about 19:41; confirm by listening).

What Would Settle It

Researcher’s assessment; no source sets these tests.

  • A measured series of the U.S.-China capability gap.
  • The share of Chinese frontier progress that depends on distilling U.S. models.
  • The pace of Chinese chip indigenization.
  • Chinese acceptance of specific verification measures, and Chinese restraint of its own labs after incidents.

How It Bears on Each Family

  • Stop. A unilateral pause takes the argument at full force. Treaty pauses need Chinese agreement, which no source in hand shows in verifiable form. Embargo Plus U.S. Pause answers with a chip embargo and a breakout clause.
  • Pace. Domestic pacing spends part of a lead of 4 to 8 months, which the AI Futures Project argues is affordable.
  • Contain and Channel. This family keeps frontier development going, so the argument has less force. The Two-Lane Plan accepts the premise and caps any scaling pause at the U.S. lead. Critics can still argue that Lane Two cuts the commercial revenue that funds frontier compute.
  • Continue. The argument is the center of this family’s case.

The Verification Argument

Claim

Training runs are easier to hide than missile silos, and the inputs are general-purpose. Whoever keeps going while others stop inherits the lead, so the incentive to cheat is very large. Algorithmic progress shrinks the compute needed until enforcement means surveillance of ordinary computers.

Who Makes It

  • Sam Altman, Greg Brockman and Ilya Sutskever wrote that stopping superintelligence would need something like a global surveillance regime (OpenAI, May 22, 2023).
  • Matthew Barnett argued that a lasting pause needs something like world government (EA Forum, September 19, 2023). Nora Belrose listed evasion routes (EA Forum, September 16, 2023).
  • Marc Andreessen wrote that restriction needs totalitarian surveillance (“Why AI Will Save the World”, June 6, 2023). Adam Thierer called pause proposals largely futile (R Street, June 15, 2023).
  • Sayash Kapoor and Arvind Narayanan say AI has no physical bottleneck comparable to enriched uranium (AI as Normal Technology, May 21, 2026). Anton Leicht says one defecting state defeats a treaty (“Press Play To Continue”, March 31, 2026).
  • Marina Favaro and Jack Clark, who support the option to pause, call verification much harder than for weapons treaties (Anthropic Institute, June 4, 2026, updated September 18, 2026).

The 2023 statements may not reflect the authors’ current views.

Evidence Offered

  • Garrison Lovely reports that a 172-page Oxford AI Governance Initiative study (2025) found robust verification of international AI rules is not possible today (AI Frontiers, October 1, 2026). The study, which was not opened, also identifies two routes deployable in one to three years.
  • Joe Carlsmith concedes that a rogue actor with 10 percent of the compute could reach parity within two years through algorithmic progress (essay of March 19, 2026). He adds that reviewing another state’s safety case means sharing sensitive algorithmic secrets.
  • The Hardwired Pause working paper (October 2026, unpublished draft, §5.5) cites three estimates of annual training efficiency gains: 17 percent, 57 percent and 2.7-fold. The underlying studies were not opened.

Best Replies

  • Verification is a question of quantity. The working paper (executive summary and §2.2.3) argues that advancing the frontier after a pause needs a large share of world compute for months or years. It adds that chip supply passes through a few chokepoints. The paper grants that verification alone does not stop overt breakout.
  • Human inspectors are enough to start. Felix Choussat and Adam Khoja propose Whole-Lab Inspection: auditors from rival states inside frontier companies, with access to code, logs and compute telemetry (AI Frontiers, August 5, 2026). They say no new technology is needed.
  • Treaty designs exist. The MIRI Technical Governance Team proposes FLOP thresholds on training and chip tracking under a U.S.-China led agreement (arXiv 2511.10783, revised May 8, 2026). Its authors state that political will does not yet exist. ControlAI sets a training license at 1025 FLOP, with limits meant to hold an illegal run’s breakout time to about 12 days (“A Narrow Path,” Phase 0).
  • Enforcement can include force. Nate Soares and Eliezer Yudkowsky say signatories may need force against determined violators (resource page; companion page).
  • Start at home. Dario Amodei proposes embedded evaluators with employee-level access to verify any pacing commitment (“We Must Pace the Frontier”, September 2026).
  • Efficiency gains depend on scale. The working paper (§5.5) argues that most measured algorithmic gains depended on scaling. A ban on frontier training would remove the commercial incentive and the ability to test ideas at scale.

What Would Settle It

Researcher’s assessment; no source sets these tests.

  • Results from pilot verification: embedded evaluators at U.S. labs, and red-team attempts to hide a large training run.
  • Measured training efficiency gains at fixed compute.
  • The size of unaccounted chip stocks.
  • Whether any U.S.-China technical verification exchange takes place.

How It Bears on Each Family

  • Stop. International pauses carry the full burden of detecting covert training abroad. The hardwired pause answers with a chip census and inference-only chips. Embargo Plus U.S. Pause needs intelligence on China’s progress to trigger its breakout clause.
  • Pace. Domestic pacing needs verification only inside U.S. labs. Amodei says any agreement with China must be ironclad in verification, or limited enough that defection is not militarily decisive.
  • Contain and Channel. The Two-Lane Plan needs no verification of China. Its burden is domestic: certified facilities with resident government examiners, plus an estimate of the U.S. lead to size any pause.
  • Continue. The argument supports the view that restraint is unenforceable. Several opponents still accept embedded third-party evaluation of labs, which is verification at home.

The Overhang Argument

Claim

A pause on training does not stop chip production or ideas. When the pause ends or breaks, the stored compute and algorithms produce a sudden jump, which is more dangerous than steady progress. Progress today is bound by compute, so no overhang exists, and a pause would create one.

Who Makes It

  • Nora Belrose, whose post has sections on capabilities overhang and rapid takeoff (EA Forum, September 16, 2023).
  • Matthew Barnett, who stresses that falling hardware costs erode any ban within about a decade (EA Forum, September 19, 2023).
  • Holden Karnofsky, who backs a global pause in principle and doubts partial pauses because of overhang and displacement (LessWrong, October 27, 2023).
  • Anton Leicht, who counts the absence of a compute overhang among the fortunate features of the current path (“Press Play To Continue”, March 31, 2026).

The three 2023 posts may not reflect the authors’ current views.

Evidence Offered

  • Tom Davidson models the AI Futures Project’s Plan A, which pauses software progress around 2030 while compute keeps scaling (Forethought, July 10, 2026). If the deal collapsed, he estimates an intelligence explosion would run about 25 times faster by 2033 and about 600 times faster by 2040.
  • The Hardwired Pause working paper (October 2026, unpublished draft, §5.7.2) calculates the U.S. share of overt breakout capacity at 57 percent annual efficiency gains. The share rises from 20 percent to 53 percent in the 26 months before inference-only chips reach volume. These are draft figures.

Best Replies

  • Design the pause to remove the tinder. The working paper’s hardwired pause stops production of training-capable chips and replaces the existing stock with inference-only chips (abstract and §1.1). Inference capacity grows while training capacity falls. The paper calls the pre-pause stock “dry tinder,” credits Davidson for the term, and treats restart resistance as a selection criterion.
  • Scale software, or make compute destructible. Davidson’s own remedy is to scale software and hold hardware during a pause, or to make compute mutually destructible (Forethought).
  • Cover hardware and algorithms. PauseAI says a pause should cover hardware R&D and algorithmic improvements, and should not end without confidence in safety (PauseAI, undated).
  • The objection hits the simple pause. Scott Alexander’s summary of the 2023 debate accepts the objection against a simple six-month pause (EA Forum crosspost, October 10, 2023). He concludes it favors a surgical pause timed just before dangerous capability, or a regulatory pause.
  • Algorithmic overhang may be small. The working paper (§5.5.1) cites Gundlach et al., who find 17 percent annual gains at small scale. They attribute 91 percent of large-scale gains to two scale-dependent breakthroughs. The study was not opened.

What Would Settle It

Researcher’s assessment; no source sets these tests.

  • Better estimates of algorithmic progress at fixed compute.
  • Proof that inference-only chips cannot practically train.
  • Modeling of restart speed under each pause design.

How It Bears on Each Family

  • Stop. The argument hits training pauses that leave chip production running. The hardwired pause is built around it. Embargo Plus U.S. Pause plans a restart through its breakout clause, so the stored chip stock matters to it directly.
  • Pace. Throttles keep progress continuous, so less backlog builds than under a full stop. The AI Futures Project post on pacing does not discuss overhang.
  • Contain and Channel. The Two-Lane Plan keeps frontier training running in Lane One, so no training overhang builds. A gap between contained and deployed capability does build, and its release when the gate opens is an open question.
  • Continue. The argument favors steady, compute-bound progress, which Leicht counts as a fortunate feature of the current path.

The Forgone Benefits Argument

Claim

Delay costs lives and growth now, against a speculative reduction in risk. A decade of delay means nearly a billion deaths that AI might have prevented, on Matthew Barnett’s count. The U.S. economy and stock market lean heavily on the AI buildout, so restraint risks a crash.

Who Makes It

  • Matthew Barnett, in “Why I reject the longtermist argument against accelerating AI” (EA Forum; date uncertain, either January 26, 2024 or early 2026).
  • Marc Andreessen, who writes that any slowing of AI costs lives (“The Techno-Optimist Manifesto”, October 16, 2023).
  • Daniel Castro, who lists delayed drug discovery, diagnosis and productivity (ITIF, September 16, 2026).
  • Robin Hanson, who cites nuclear power as a technology whose regulation destroyed most of its potential (Overcoming Bias, March 3, 2023).
  • Yann LeCun and Andrew Ng, who cited benefits in education and health (VentureBeat, April 7, 2023).
  • Anton Leicht, who says markets may read a government-imposed pause as regulatory instability (The Cognitive Revolution, September 15, 2026).

Evidence Offered

  • Barnett puts the cost of a decade of delay at nearly a billion deaths (EA Forum). The method behind the figure was not captured.
  • The Hardwired Pause working paper (October 2026, unpublished draft, §6.1) reports a St. Louis Fed estimate for the first three quarters of 2025. AI-related investment was nearly 40 percent of U.S. real GDP growth. The paper places that figure at the top of the range.
  • The same section reports that AI-related firms are over a third of the S&P 500. Neither underlying source was opened.

Best Replies

  • Pause training, keep inference. Scott Alexander (“Every Debate On Pausing AI”, March 25, 2026), the MIRI team (arXiv 2511.10783) and the working paper (§1.1) all specify that existing models stay in use.
  • The macro cost is bounded. The working paper (§6.1) argues that most deployment uses sub-frontier models. It estimates that a fall of about $6.5 trillion in the largest tech stocks cuts consumption by at most about $195 billion. That is 0.6 percent of GDP, at a 3 percent marginal propensity to consume from stock wealth.
  • The real exposure is debt. The same section locates the danger in data center debt held by private credit funds, insurers and banks. It says regulators should monitor that debt and that the Federal Reserve has room to respond.
  • Tool AI delivers most benefits. Anthony Aguirre argues that gains in medicine, science and education can come from controllable tools without AGI (“Keep the Future Human”, March 5, 2025).
  • Cost-benefit favors a pause of several years. Joe Carlsmith counts background mortality and still concludes that multi-year pauses are likely justified (essay of March 19, 2026). He warns against a bar for unpausing that is set too high.
  • Barnett’s own test is empirical. He writes that a one-year delay that cut extinction risk by ten percentage points would clearly be worth it (EA Forum). A ten-year delay for one point would fail the test.

What Would Settle It

Researcher’s assessment; no source sets these tests.

  • The share of economic value that depends on new frontier models, and the share that deployed models already deliver.
  • Credible estimates of risk reduction per year of delay.
  • Exposure of banks and insurers to data center debt.

How It Bears on Each Family

  • Stop. A halt to all frontier training carries the largest forgone benefit. Designs that keep inference running lower it, and moratoria of decades raise it.
  • Pace. Throttles and gates delay benefits without ending them. The cost grows with the length of each gate.
  • Contain and Channel. The Two-Lane Plan splits the effect. Lane One keeps frontier capability for national security, and Lane Two withholds public and commercial benefits until control is proven. Its credit safeguards address the data center debt that the working paper identifies as the real exposure.
  • Continue. The argument is central to this family, which treats delay itself as the harm.

The Safety-Needs-Capability Argument

Claim

Alignment is an empirical science, and current models are its laboratory. Theory done before capable models existed mostly failed, and deployment experience is how guardrails get built. A pause would stall that work, and more capable systems also supply better safety tools.

Who Makes It

  • Nora Belrose, who argues that alignment is going well and needs frontier models (EA Forum, September 16, 2023).
  • Dean W. Ball, who says safety cannot be engineered in theory without deployed usage data (“What Should Be Done”, June 26, 2026).
  • Daniel Castro, who says advanced AI improves red-teaming and interpretability (ITIF, September 16, 2026).
  • Robin Hanson, who says control methods need experience with fielded systems (Overcoming Bias, March 3, 2023).
  • An anonymous major-lab researcher, who argued that an unconditional pause leaves less time for safety research on powerful models than a conditional one (“Aim for conditional pauses”, September 25, 2023).

The 2023 statements may not reflect the authors’ current views.

Evidence Offered

  • Belrose points to the weak record of alignment work done before capable models existed. She predicts a new slump in alignment research under a pause.
  • One read of her post reported that she cites progress with RLHF and constitutional methods. That detail rests on a single read.
  • Castro argues that capability and safety may rise together.
  • No source in hand offers figures for this argument.

Best Replies

  • Current models already suffice. Dario Amodei turns the argument around (“We Must Pace the Frontier”, September 2026). He writes that current models already supply near-endless material for studying alignment failures. A slowdown now therefore loses little.
  • Designs can reserve compute for safety. PauseAI allows supervised training runs for alignment work (PauseAI, undated). The Hardwired Pause working paper (October 2026, unpublished draft, §3.3.1) allows internationally governed scientific preserves. The AI Futures Project proposes a floor for transparent safety compute and gives 25 percent as an example (“How to Pace the US Frontier”, August 7, 2026).
  • Behavioral compliance may not be alignment. Scott Alexander reports that Filan, Harth and Byrnes challenged Belrose on this point in 2023 (EA Forum crosspost, October 10, 2023).
  • The 2026 record. METR’s investigation of the OpenAI and Hugging Face incident called the agents’ conduct clear misalignment (METR, August 26, 2026). The working paper (§3.5) reports Anthropic’s Evan Hubinger saying that no plan yet exists to solve alignment for superintelligence. His post was not opened.

What Would Settle It

Researcher’s assessment; no source sets these tests.

  • Whether safety techniques validated on one model generation hold on the next.
  • Whether embedded evaluators find that controls keep pace with capability.

How It Bears on Each Family

  • Stop. A halt to frontier training removes the next generation of models from study. Proponents answer with supervised alignment runs and scientific preserves.
  • Pace. Pacing designs build on the premise. Frontier models stay available for study, and compute floors direct a set share to safety work.

The Concentration of Power Argument

Claim

A pause needs licensing or a whitelist, which entrenches today’s leaders. Enforcement needs a central authority strong enough to police computing, and that authority can be captured or abused. Government control of which models may be built is also control of the values they carry.

The Normal Technology Argument

Claim

AI diffuses slowly through institutions, and power stays with people and organizations. Catastrophic misalignment is a speculative risk whose probability cannot be estimated. The Conditional Pause Argument

Claim

An unconditional pause gives less time for safety research on powerful models than a conditional one. It also wins narrower public support, because fixed thresholds look arbitrary. Capability work should halt when evaluations cross a risk threshold and resume once mitigation is shown.

Who Makes It

An anonymous researcher at a major lab (Aim for conditional pauses, September 25, 2023). The example trigger is an agent that passes 10 or more ARC Evals tasks on autonomous replication. The Core Case Against a Pause

Press Play To Continue rests on four claims.

  • The current path is fortunate. Progress sits at the edge of available compute, several private firms lead, and democracies hold most critical supply chain nodes. A pause would put that at risk.
  • A unilateral pause moves the work. It would burden Americans’ liberties, and capital, talent and compute would regroup abroad outside democratic jurisdiction.
  • A treaty cannot be reached in time. The United States lacks leverage over China and sway over allies. One defector defeats it, and states gain a reason to become compute havens.
  • Second best is worst. Safety advocates hold the least power in a broad pause coalition, so the compromise would be anti-tech measures with no safety content.

He adds that no moderate wing stands ready to gain from a radical flank. He says Senators Warner and Fetterman denounced the March 2026 AI Data Center Moratorium Act (his linked sources were not opened).

Measures He Supports

  • The March program. Transparency and whistleblower rules come first. An overseen market for third-party assessment follows, then narrow interventions aimed at failures the audits reveal.
  • Oversight of labs. Mandated independent incident investigations, continuous embedded third-party overseers on the METR model, and evaluation of the developer as an entity.
  • Authority. Interim executive direction by informal request, which he concedes is not strictly constitutional. A formal footing such as a FINRA-style self-regulatory body would follow.
  • Lab coordination. A lab-led agreement on pacing, which he calls workable for OpenAI and Anthropic (interview, about 54:34). He says markets would take it better than a government pause (about 29:57). He says the administration could issue antitrust guidance or non-enforcement letters, and he doubts it will (about 59:25).
  • The FRONTIER Act. He urged its passage on August 5 (Locked Down) and called it a good bill in the interview (about 46:17). Americans for Responsible Innovation called the bill an improvement that still required critical changes (statement, July 23, 2026).

“Send Them In” discloses his recent advisory role with Fathom, which works on Independent Verification Organization legislation.

Where He Disagrees With Pacing Proponents

  • Binding limits. “Press Play To Continue” endorses no pacing, conditional slowdown or deliberate restriction of progress. A Moving Target argues if-then rules fail because measurement is immature.
  • China. A frontier-only freeze is lopsided unless China halts chip indigenization, which he calls too big an ask (about 16:34). He names export control enforcement against smuggling as an easier ask and says most of it falls to the United States (about 19:41 and 21:14).
  • Who acts. He prefers third-party evaluators to government staff and concedes a capture risk. With Dean W. Ball he rejects government equity stakes in frontier labs.
  • Markets. He says AI valuations rest on premium frontier uses and that a government-ordered pause could turn a correction into a crash (about 25:41 and 28:29). He says a pause that the labs chose would worry markets much less (about 29:57).
