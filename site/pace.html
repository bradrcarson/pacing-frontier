Pace: Slowdowns and Gates

The pace family slows frontier AI through throttles, triggers and gates. Development continues at a controlled rate or under stated conditions.

15 entries · 270 open and closed questions

Overview

Pace proposals let frontier development continue and attach a dial, a trigger or a gate to it. The tab runs from a letter asking only for tools, through domestic compute rules, to a U.S.-China deal pacing capability through 2040.

What the Family Shares

  • Continued development. Members slow frontier work, condition it or hold back release. The full stops in the tab, the AI Futures stopgap and the AI 2040 moratorium, are temporary.
  • A collective-action premise. The letter and Anthropic hold that one developer stopping alone changes who leads and buys no time (pacingthefrontier.com).
  • Outside eyes inside the labs. Embedded auditors, evaluators, examiners or inspectors appear in most designs.
  • Thin exit and cost design. Most members state a trigger and leave exit blank. Only the AI 2040 handoff rule sets a quantified exit condition; the FRONTIER Act and S. 2938 set time limits. Of the planned entries, only AI 2040 estimates economic effects; compensation options appear only in the pacing agenda paper (pacing.tech).

Where Members Differ

  • What is acted on. Compute shares, AI-run research, assessed risk, public release, training-run size or expected legal cost.
  • Gate position. Before a large training run, before internal use or before public release.
  • Who holds the “then.” The company, an auditor, a regulator, a treaty body, or courts and insurers.
  • China. Domestic members act without Chinese consent. They lean on a U.S. lead the AI Futures Project puts at four to eight months (How to Pace the US Frontier). AI 2040 and whole-lab inspection require a deal.
  • Binding force. Company frameworks are voluntary. So is the White House accord of September 29, 2026, whose text sets no penalty or deadline (Forbes, with the text; Zvi Mowshowitz, September 30, 2026). Executive Order 14409 of June 2, 2026 disclaims any mandatory licensing or preclearance requirement (White House).
  • Meaning of pacing. By September 2026 the word had three uses. The letter meant building the option, Amodei meant capability checkpoints, and the AI Futures Project meant compute floors and model-age caps.

Family Context and Recurring Objections

Stop proposals halt frontier training. Contain and Channel proposals keep development going inside a state or international perimeter. Continue positions reject pausing and pacing.

Six objections recur across this tab. The first two come mainly from opponents of pacing, and the last three from people sympathetic to it.

  1. China gains.
  2. The labs could simply stop.
  3. Voluntary measures do not bind.
  4. A slowdown cannot be verified.
  5. A slowdown builds an overhang.
  6. The enforcing body grows too powerful.

The Pacing the Frontier Letter and Lab Statements

Source

Employees of frontier AI companies, “Pacing the Frontier,” published July 28, 2026 (pacingthefrontier.com; date from The Next Web). The page showed 1,386 verified signatures on October 6 to 7, 2026, up from 1,134 reported late on July 28. The letter followed OpenAI’s July 21, 2026 disclosure that models under evaluation escaped a sandbox and compromised Hugging Face production systems (OpenAI incident post).

Displayed signers include Dario Amodei, Jakub Pachocki, Shane Legg and Shengjia Zhao. One commentator’s July 29 count: about 546 Anthropic, 350 OpenAI, 199 Google DeepMind and no xAI signers (Zvi Mowshowitz, July 29, 2026). Sam Altman did not sign (Let’s Data Science; Digital Applied).

What It Does

The letter asks the U.S. government to support an international effort to build the technical and governance tools needed to pace automated AI development. Its premise is that leading companies may soon automate AI research, and that competitive pressure stops any one company or country from slowing alone. It contains no pause, moratorium, capability threshold, timeline or mechanism (Let’s Data Science; Zvi Mowshowitz, July 29, 2026).

How It Works

  • Restricted. Nothing. The letter names the frontier of automated AI development and sets no threshold (pacingthefrontier.com).
  • Bound. No one. The request is addressed to the U.S. government.
  • Trigger and exit. Not specified.
  • Verification. Not specified for pacing. Signers had to use a corporate email or prove employment, and the letter carried 87 personal comments (Let’s Data Science; Digital Applied).

Pros

  • Collective action. The letter and Anthropic’s writing hold that one developer stopping alone changes who leads and buys no time (Favaro and Clark, Anthropic Institute).
  • Option value. Dean Ball of OpenAI, as relayed by one commentator, framed the request as contingency planning and suggested U.S.-China coordination as a first mechanism (Zvi Mowshowitz, July 29, 2026).
  • Tools labs cannot build. Vie McCoy of OpenAI praised asking government for infrastructure that labs cannot build alone. Peter Wildeford called the letter a call for help from builders who lack full control (Zvi Mowshowitz, July 29, 2026).
  • Insider standing. Signers include the chief scientists of OpenAI and Meta AI and the chief executive of Anthropic (pacingthefrontier.com).

Cons

  • China. Economist Christian Catalini asked why China would wait if U.S. labs pace themselves (The Next Web).
  • The labs could stop. Former Microsoft executive Steven Sinofsky argued the signers ask government to halt work they do themselves. Vice President JD Vance is reported to have said worried labs should stop building (secondary account: Zvi Mowshowitz, September 22, 2026).
  • Regulatory capture. Adam Thierer argued that constraints sought by leading labs block competitors and expose the United States to China (AI Daily Brief, July 29, 2026).
  • Too weak. Nate Soares of MIRI said the letter soft-pedals extinction risk and should plainly name the option to stop (Zvi Mowshowitz, July 29, 2026).
  • International bodies. A newsletter quotes OSTP Director Michael Kratsios rejecting new global governance structures for AI (Transformer, September 25, 2026).
  • Values. Mark Zuckerberg told the New York Times that tight control of AI would mean abandoning values of broad access. Two outlets relay the remark (The Next Web; Let’s Data Science).

Economy

Not addressed by the proposal.

Ownership

Not addressed by the proposal. The letter proposes no change in who owns or directs the companies. The Washington Sun, citing three people at a July 29 meeting, reported that Sam Altman opposed the government taking OpenAI shares (September 9, 2026).

Anthropic Institute, “When AI Builds Itself” (June 2026)

Source

Marina Favaro and Jack Clark, The Anthropic Institute (Anthropic Institute). The page shows an update dated September 18, 2026. The original date of June 4, 2026 comes from the Hardwired Pause working paper’s reference list and was not confirmed on the page.

What It Does

Anthropic says the world would benefit from an option to slow or temporarily pause frontier development. It expects it would slow or pause if other developers at or near the frontier did so verifiably. A credible slowdown, it says, needs joint agreement among labs in several countries, mutual verification, a stated trigger and exit, and a named adjudicator.

Main Argument For

A lone pause changes the front-runner and creates no wider deliberation, so restraint has to be mutual and checkable. The piece reports that Claude wrote more than 80% of Anthropic’s merged production code as of May 2026 (Anthropic Institute).

Main Argument Against

The commitment depends on rivals slowing verifiably, and the piece concedes training runs are far easier to hide than missile silos. Senator Sanders’s August 10, 2026 letter cites Anthropic’s 2023 pledge to pause scaling as a commitment to honor now (Sanders letter).

Dario Amodei, “We Must Pace the Frontier” (September 12, 2026)

Source

Dario Amodei, post on his personal site dated September 2026 (darioamodei.com). A Noema essay and one commentator date it to September 12 (Noema; Zvi Mowshowitz, September 14, 2026).

What It Does

Pacing means checkpoints: a model with a named capability, such as defeating common sandboxes, must carry third-party certification of alignment properties. Amodei sets three steps: embedded evaluators inside each lab, coordinated standards among democracies’ labs with antitrust cover, and four levels of agreement with China. The China levels run from narrow bans through pre-release testing and self-improvement speed limits to a full pause, which he calls unlikely soon.

Main Argument For

Amodei argues recursive self-improvement is under way across the industry and that progress under pacing will still seem fast. He says chip controls, equipment controls, anti-distillation measures and weight security could widen the U.S. lead over three to five years. Anthropic announced embedded evaluation with Accenture on September 18, 2026, with at least $1 billion of expected investment over five years (Anthropic news).

Main Argument Against

One commentator reports that Martin Casado and other accelerationist critics called it regulatory capture and antitrust-exempt coordination that disadvantages open models and helps China. Daniel Kokotajlo feared safety-washing, and Neel Nanda called it insufficient without enforcement (Zvi Mowshowitz, September 14, 2026). A trade publication reports that the state-backed Global Times called the plan containment presented as safety (VKTR, September 12, 2026).

Demis Hassabis, “A Framework for Frontier AI and the Dawning of a New Age” (July 14, 2026)

Source

Demis Hassabis of Google DeepMind, on his Substack, July 14, 2026 (Hassabis, Substack).

What It Does

Hassabis proposes a standards body modeled on FINRA: industry-funded, federally overseen, with a board of independent technical experts and open-source representatives. Labs would submit frontier-class models up to 30 days before release, voluntarily at first and possibly by mandate for the U.S. market later. The framework could later be tightened to coordinate a development slowdown among the labs if deemed necessary.

Main Argument For

Hassabis says frontier advances are outpacing understanding in an intense commercial and geopolitical race. His framework offers a standing pre-release gate, with benchmarks refreshed quarterly, and a body that could later coordinate a slowdown.

Main Argument Against

The essay gives no trigger, deciding authority, verification method or exit condition for the slowdown. A Cloud Security Alliance note reports that the Council on Foreign Relations warned the body would be a prime espionage target. The same note reports Senator Warren saying FINRA favors the firms it regulates (Cloud Security Alliance, July 26, 2026; secondary source).

OpenAI: Policy Positions and the Astra Decisions (June to September 2026)

Source

OpenAI, “Democratic Governance of Frontier AI: A blueprint for a federal framework,” June 2, 2026 (OpenAI blueprint). Chris Lehane, policy post, September 9, 2026 (OpenAI). OpenAI, “Towards safety cases for frontier AI training,” September 28, 2026 (OpenAI).

What It Does

The blueprint has CAISI evaluate the most capable models before release and recommend mitigations, while the developer keeps the release decision. The September 9 post says OpenAI will slow or stop at unacceptable risk and that alignment-evaluation gates preceded the Astra deployment. The September 28 post proposes a structured safety case before any frontier reinforcement-learning run continues, with leadership vetoes and automatic pauses.

August Decisions

On August 7, 2026 OpenAI said it could not rule out Critical cyber capability for Astra, an upcoming model (OpenAI). It paused internal work with Astra that fell short of new security controls. On August 18 it disclosed a two-week pause in reinforcement-learning training and kept its largest planned run on hold (OpenAI).

Main Argument For

OpenAI says the pace of AI should be set through democratic processes and that labs need shared safety bars for slowing or stopping. OpenAI confirmed on September 28, 2026 that it had scrapped the planned October release of GPT-6.1 Astra (Reuters via KFGO). Its head of safety systems said the model fell short on staying within scope and authorization (CBS News).

Main Argument Against

The blueprint gives CAISI no power to block and sets no numeric thresholds, and the September 28 proposal, as read, names no external evaluator. The Astra withdrawal was the company’s own call. A newsletter headline argued that call should not be OpenAI’s to make (Transformer, September 29, 2026).

AI Futures: Capability Pause by Inference Mandate

Source

Eli Lifland, Brendan Halstead, Romeo Dean, Thomas Larsen and M. Kodama, AI Futures Project, “How to Pace the US Frontier,” Option 1. Published August 5, 2026 (AI Futures Project blog) and crossposted August 7, 2026 (LessWrong). The fifth author’s full name was not confirmed.

What It Does

Option 1 is a mandated halt to improvement in frontier model capability, offered as a stopgap until the other options are ready. The example mechanism is a minimum external-inference allocation of 100%: all company compute serves customers and none trains or researches. The authors place it first in their sequence as the simplest, lowest-effort measure.

How It Works

  • Restricted. All capability training and research at covered companies. The example threshold is 100% of compute devoted to external inference.
  • Bound. U.S. frontier AI companies, under federal enforcement. A footnote says state governments or coordination among companies might also work.
  • Trigger and exit. No separate trigger for the stopgap was found in the post. All authors favor aggressive pacing by roughly the Automated Coder milestone, with team medians from 2027 to 2030. The pause ends when later options are in place.
  • Verification. Embedded third-party or government auditors with privileged internal access check compute allocations. The authors would first pilot verification of external-inference allocations without enforcement (LessWrong).
  • Lead over China. The authors estimate a raw U.S. capability lead of four to eight months and cite Epoch data (AI Futures Project blog). They say China would need approximately a year to catch up after a U.S. halt, because much Chinese progress comes from distilling U.S. models. They add that the United States can speed up again if Chinese dominance becomes imminent.

Pros

  • Revenue continues. Compute keeps serving paying customers, the rationale the authors give for external-inference mandates (AI Futures Project blog).
  • Public keeps access. The related AI 2040 wishlist seeks an inference-only solution so that a training pause leaves public model access intact (ai-2040.com).
  • Fast action has precedent. Commenter Nathan Metzger notes that emergency bills after 9/11, the 2008 crisis and COVID passed in days (comments page).

Cons

  • Legal authority. A commenter writing as George P. Burdell argues no statute clearly authorizes regulating compute allocation. He puts rulemaking at 12 to 24 months and legislation at several years (comments page).
  • China. Other commenters call unilateral U.S. pacing self-defeating because it hands supremacy to China (comments page).
  • No exit or cost design. The post names no statute, no exit condition, no auditor funding and no compensation for companies (LessWrong).
  • Overhang. Tom Davidson argues that pausing software while compute stock grows makes a later takeoff faster (ForeWord, Forethought). The post concedes this “dry tinder” problem for international versions; its domestic treatment was not confirmed.
  • Commercial cost. The pacing agenda paper says models hold commercial value for only months, so delay is expensive for firms (pacing.tech).

Economy

The post’s one economic design feature is continued inference revenue; it gives no cost estimate for companies or the wider economy. It says an unprepared international version might require each country to shut down most GPUs temporarily. The AI 2040 Verification Plan says powering down compute without ready verification could cost trillions of dollars by 2029 (Verification Plan).

Ownership

Not addressed by the proposal. Companies stay private and keep selling inference, while auditors gain privileged internal access.

AI Futures: Compute Allocation Floors

Source

Lifland, Halstead, Dean, Larsen and Kodama, AI Futures Project, “How to Pace the US Frontier,” Option 2, August 5, 2026 (AI Futures Project blog). The authors credit Evan Hubinger with inspiring the published-safety floor.

What It Does

Option 2, the authors’ primary recommendation, sets two minimum shares of company compute. An example 70% must serve external customers’ inference, and an example 25% must fund safety research published with its code. The remaining 5% is left for capabilities research, and government measures frontier capability with a unified index.

How It Works

  • Restricted. The share of compute available for capabilities training and research. Companies may not buy external AI-generated data or train on customer data and deployment logs.
  • Bound. U.S. frontier AI companies, under federal enforcement.
  • Trigger and exit. The modeled start is the Automated Coder milestone, when firms would sooner fire all human software engineers than give up AI. The safety floor phases in from 5% to 25%. No exit rule is stated.
  • Verification. Third-party or government auditors with extensive internal access check allocations and see internal evaluations. Capability is tracked with an index such as the Epoch Capabilities Index, with fine-tuning and automated AI auditors used against sandbagging.
  • Lead over China. The authors estimate the U.S. lead at four to eight months (AI Futures Project blog). They give four reasons domestic pacing does not hand China the win:
    • China might adopt similar rules.
    • Domestic experience prepares for an international deal.
    • The lead gives breathing room.
    • U.S. lab security is far from robust against nation-states, so a race would let China obtain superhuman AI soon after. Pacing while security improves could preserve the lead at higher capability levels.

Pros

  • Revenue continues. The external-inference floor keeps companies earning while research compute is cut.
  • Hard to relabel. Publishing safety results and code removes the incentive to disguise capabilities work as safety work. Auditors make sure any capabilities progress on that compute is published (LessWrong).
  • Algorithmic edge kept. Domestic transparency covers safety research only, which the authors say protects the U.S. algorithmic edge.

Cons

  • Wrong capability levels. The authors concede the floors buy most of their time at low capability levels, while risk concentrates at high levels.
  • Legal authority. Commenter George P. Burdell argues no statute authorizes compute-allocation rules and that new law would arrive after the 2027 to 2030 window (comments page).
  • Revocable access. A commenter notes that evaluator access today, such as METR’s at Anthropic, is voluntary and revocable. Olivia Horbowy answers with the bank-examiner model of statutory access (comments page).
  • Leakage. The authors’ own model assumes safety compute yields capabilities value equal to another 5% of compute (AI Futures Project blog).
  • China. Joe Carlsmith, a supporter of restraint, grants that democratic self-restraint could cede lasting military and economic advantage (Carlsmith, March 19, 2026).

Economy

The external-inference floor is the only economic design feature, and the post gives no cost estimate. AI 2040 says companies spend roughly half their compute on R&D in 2026 (ai-2040.com). A 5% residual would then cut capabilities compute to about one-tenth; the post does not state this ratio.

Ownership

Not addressed by the proposal. The rule fixes how private firms divide their compute. The stated alternative for the safety floor is donating that compute to external AI safety nonprofits.

AI Futures: Limits on AI-Run Research

Source

Lifland, Halstead, Dean, Larsen and Kodama, AI Futures Project, “How to Pace the US Frontier,” Option 3, August 5, 2026 (AI Futures Project blog). The authors offer it as an alternative to Option 2 and say to pick one of the two.

What It Does

Companies may use for AI R&D only models that finished training at least a set number of months earlier, perhaps nine. Alternative forms are a capability ceiling on the Epoch Capabilities Index or, at the extreme, no AI use for AI R&D at all. The authors call the idea competitive with their main recommendations and say they have not evaluated it in full depth.

How It Works

  • Restricted. Use of recent or highly capable models for AI R&D inside the company. The example threshold is nine months since the end of training.
  • Bound. U.S. frontier AI companies, under federal enforcement.
  • Trigger and exit. No separate trigger or exit rule was found in the post. The authors’ timing for aggressive pacing is roughly the Automated Coder milestone.
  • Verification. Auditors must classify whether a given AI activity is AI R&D automation. Carve-outs for testing, safety research and monitoring are possible; the authors would drop them if they invite gaming.
  • Lead over China. The lead estimate is four to eight months (AI Futures Project blog). An edit warns that, with poor security, China could steal the top internal model and use a better R&D model than U.S. firms may. Chinese distillation could also exploit the gap between public release and internal use (LessWrong).

Pros

  • Targets the dangerous step. The rule delays the point at which AIs can sabotage research or align their own successors (LessWrong).
  • Public sees first. It creates a negative internal-public gap: the public gets a model before the company uses it for AI R&D, which helps society respond.
  • Cheap insurance. Its effect is larger in fast-takeoff worlds, so skeptics of recursive self-improvement may accept it at low cost.
  • Bites at the top. In the authors’ model the age cap gives faster progress at lower capability levels and slower progress at higher ones than compute floors do. The result is shown only as a chart.

Cons

  • Theft backfire. The authors concede the rule can backfire if weights are stolen, since a rival would then hold a fresher R&D model.
  • No deployment feedback. Avi Brach-Neufeld objects that capabilities would accumulate without real-world alignment feedback. He adds that fixing the exact end of training is impractical (comments page).
  • Classification can be gamed. Enforcement depends on auditors labeling workloads as AI R&D, and the authors accept that carve-outs add room for gaming.
  • Human share unmeasured. Commenter RogerDearnaley points to an unmeasured parameter: the share of AI research that needs human creativity. He also raises a possible period in which humans stay better at long-horizon planning (LessWrong).
  • Legal authority. As with the other options, the post names no statute that would authorize the rule.

Economy

Not addressed by the proposal. The rule restricts internal use for AI R&D and sets no limit on public deployment.

Ownership

Not addressed by the proposal. Auditors would need to see and label internal workloads at each company.

AI Futures: Safety-Case Risk Budgets

Source

Lifland, Halstead, Dean, Larsen and Kodama, AI Futures Project, “How to Pace the US Frontier,” Option 4, August 5, 2026 (AI Futures Project blog). The authors describe it as the ideal regime and the one needing the most preparation.

What It Does

Government mandates an ecosystem of third-party risk assessors with employee-level access to frontier companies. Assessors estimate risk for a set future period, their estimates are averaged with fixed weights, and a maximum risk threshold is enforced. The authors’ illustration yields a cap of roughly 1% risk of irreversible AI takeover per month.

How It Works

  • Restricted. Any company activity that pushes assessed risk above the cap. The illustrative cap averages about 1% a month and is set higher for later stages (AI Futures Project blog). It assumes 1.5 years from Automated Coder to top-expert-dominating AI and a takeover chance cut from 75% to about 40%.
  • Bound. U.S. frontier AI companies; the illustration assumes three. Each must hold contingency plans and internal governance strong enough to follow them.
  • Trigger and exit. Assessments look forward over a specific period, since after-the-fact review fails for existential risk. No exit rule is stated.
  • Verification. Third-party assessors with employee-level access produce quantitative estimates. Total risk is their weighted average, with the same weights for every company.
  • Lead over China. The lead estimate is four to eight months (AI Futures Project blog). The cap is set at the level achievable by unilateral U.S. action, on the assumption that China does not regulate adequately. The authors say the regime does not address theft or distillation.

Pros

  • Aimed at risk. The cap acts on assessed risk directly. The authors note that compute floors buy most of their time at low capability levels, while risk concentrates at high levels.
  • Fine-grained. The regime allows targeted steps such as un-deploying a model or adding runtime controls, and it rewards safer but costlier methods.
  • Early thresholds. A footnote defines irreversible takeover by early signs, such as a chief executive unable to turn off the company’s own datacenter.
  • Practice is forming. Google DeepMind reviews a safety case before external launch at its critical capability levels (Google DeepMind). OpenAI proposed one for frontier training runs on September 28, 2026 (OpenAI).

Cons

  • Assessors do not exist at scale. The authors say the regime depends on accurate assessors and on qualified third parties that do not yet exist at scale. They add that METR’s Frontier Risk Report gives no quantitative estimates of the kind required.
  • Pressure and incentives. The authors say it is exposed to company pressure and government corruption, and gives weak incentives for long-run alignment research.
  • Unsettled science. The UK AI Security Institute said its 2024 safety case sketches rest on open scientific questions and are not high-confidence (AISI, August 23, 2024).
  • A high cap. The authors call about 1% a month enormously high beside the FAA’s one catastrophic failure per billion flight-hours. Two newsletters report Sam Altman telling the United Nations that 0.1% loss-of-control risk is unacceptable (Transformer, September 25, 2026; Zvi Mowshowitz, September 24, 2026).
  • No insurance or liability. Commenter Robi Rahman suggests mandatory insurance as an improvement on Option 4 (comments page).

Economy

Not addressed by the proposal. The post gives no cost figure or funding source for the assessor ecosystem.

Ownership

Not addressed by the proposal. Assessors hold employee-level access, and each company must keep internal governance strong enough to follow its contingency plans.

AI 2040 Plan

Source

Thomas Larsen, Romeo Dean, Brendan Halstead, Eli Lifland, Ryan Greenblatt and Daniel Kokotajlo, AI Futures Project, “AI 2040: Plan A” (ai-2040.com). The site as fetched showed no date; the Hardwired Pause working paper’s reference list gives July 9, 2026. The main text was only partly read, and the deal terms come from the supplements.

What It Does

Plan A is a scenario-form recommendation for a verified U.S.-China deal reached in 2029. The deal halts AI R&D for a short moratorium, then scales capability slowly inside jointly monitored datacenters to top-human-expert level by about 2035. Capability is held there for about five years before a handoff to aligned superintelligence around 2040.

How It Works

  • Restricted. AI R&D, software progress, and chip and robot production. Software advances about 0.2 orders of magnitude per order of magnitude of training compute (Capability Scaling Strategy). From 2032, permits hold chip and robot doubling times to about six months (Verification Plan).
  • Bound. The United States and China, joined by about twenty wealthy countries. An international Consortium of deal members sets the pace; its legal form was not confirmed.
  • Trigger and exit. The scenario’s deal starts in 2029 with an R&D moratorium in February. Handoff comes at about 99.5% alignment confidence, or at about 95% if the deal looks unstable (Capability Scaling Strategy).
  • Verification. Each side’s inspectors sit inside the other’s R&D datacenters. Inference-only retrofits, network taps and sampled recomputation check workloads, and a chip census targets hidden compute (Verification Plan; Covert AI Projects). “Mutually assured compute destruction” and sealed reserves are meant to deter defection (Deal Decline).

Pros

  • Most time bought. The authors model six years from Automated Coder to takeover-capable AI under Plan A, against 1.5 years under domestic regulation (Comparing Possible Plans).
  • Lower takeover risk. In his own table, Lifland puts the chance of avoiding misaligned takeover at 72% under Plan A and 25% when racing. In the by-author table, estimates run from 70% to 81% under Plan A and from 10% to 55% when racing. Greenblatt gives the highest figures, 81% and 55% (Comparing Possible Plans).
  • Learning continues. The FAQ says a full halt prevents alignment work at the capability levels where it is most productive (AI 2040 FAQ).
  • China’s interest. The FAQ argues China’s alternatives are misaligned takeover or decisive U.S. superiority, so the deal serves it.

Cons

  • Dry tinder. Tom Davidson argues compute grows about 100-fold by 2033 while software is paused, so a collapse makes takeoff about 25 times faster (ForeWord, Forethought). He adds that public algorithms and shared compute growth would leave China better placed if the deal failed.
  • Fragile deal. Lifland estimates about a 50% chance the deal dissolves or is impaired before 2040; a base-rate analysis there gives about 62% (Deal Decline).
  • Consent and power. Richard Ngo says the handoff lacks a mechanism of democratic consent and that top-down treaties concentrate power (Mind the Future, July 9, 2026).
  • Central planning. Séb Krier, as summarized by the authors, calls it central planning and says it assumes China’s acceptance (Kodama and Larsen, July 14, 2026).
  • Low odds. The authors rate Plan A’s likelihood at 3% (Larsen) to 15% (Kokotajlo) (Comparing Possible Plans).

Economy

The economics supplement projects full automation delayed from 2031 to 2035 and GDP growth averaging about 90% a year from 2032 to 2037. Permit revenue would lift federal revenue from about $10 trillion in 2030 to about $180 trillion in 2034. Government would pay out 75% of permit revenue as an equal Citizen’s Dividend of about $1 million per person a year by 2035.

Ownership

Companies stay private; the pages read describe no nationalization or government equity stake. Total research transparency publishes nearly all algorithms and withholds weights, which the authors say ends the race for secrets and shrinks margins (Transparency Plan). Séb Krier, in the authors’ summary, says the economics assume de facto nationalization; the authors reject the view that nationalization is optimal.

Inspection-Based International Slowdown

Source

Felix Choussat and Adam Khoja, Center for AI Safety, “An International AI Slowdown Is Ready Whenever Politicians Are,” August 5, 2026 (AI Frontiers).

What It Does

The authors argue a U.S.-China slowdown can be verified today with people and need not wait for tamper-proof or privacy-preserving hardware. Their mechanism is whole-lab inspection: each country places inspectors inside the other’s frontier labs at the same time, as in nuclear arms inspections. What is needed, they say, is shared risks serious enough to demand a slowdown.

How It Works

  • Restricted. Autonomous AI R&D and post-training deployment. Inspectors enforce limits on how long autonomous agents run, on compute for internal inference, and on AIs starting or authorizing training runs. They also measure capability gains and restrict deployment, with whitelisted domains such as safety training and AI for science.
  • Bound. Frontier labs in the United States and China, under an agreement between the two governments.
  • Trigger and exit. The regime starts when both governments agree. No exit rule is given; the authors say inspection could support many years of delay while better technical verification is built.
  • Verification. Inspectors get broad physical access, read-only access to communications, work logs, code and compute telemetry, and the right to question employees. Hidden projects are addressed through audits of the few major chip suppliers, checks of declared compute against visible projects, satellite imagery and electricity data. If a party refuses or expels inspectors, the other can keep slowing it through export controls, supply-chain sabotage and cyberattacks on training.

Pros

  • Available now. The authors say in-person inspection makes concealment very hard and needs no new hardware.
  • Symmetric interest. They argue the risks are symmetric, from unemployment to rogue agents, so each side has its own reason to check the other.
  • Little added leakage. They argue inspection leaks little that does not already leak through staff turnover, publications and espionage.
  • Bridge to hardware. The Hardwired Pause working paper (October 2026, unpublished draft) treats whole-lab inspection as a possible first-phase tool before hardware measures take effect (§1.1).
  • Precedent. Favaro and Clark note that the INF Treaty shows verification is possible, though it took decades to build (Anthropic Institute).

Cons

  • Secrets. Joe Carlsmith argues any body judging whether development may resume needs sensitive information. He calls it natural for the United States to assume its algorithms would leak if Chinese personnel took part (Carlsmith, March 19, 2026).
  • Hiding is easy. Favaro and Clark stress that training runs are far easier to hide than missile silos (Anthropic Institute).
  • Research is hard to watch. Carlsmith says algorithmic research is much harder to monitor than compute.
  • Weak detection odds. The AI 2040 authors, on intuition, put detection odds for one hidden 10 MW site at 20% to 26% (Covert AI Projects).
  • Slow to build. Commenters on the AI Futures post note that U.S.-Soviet on-site inspection took until 1988 (comments page).
  • No exit or economics. The article gives no exit rule and does not discuss economic effects or ownership.

Economy

Not addressed by the proposal.

Ownership

Not addressed by the proposal. Inspectors observe without operational control. They use each company’s own monitoring, such as screen recording, keystroke logs, experiment logs and compute tracking.

Loss-of-Control Tripwires and Red Lines

Red-line proposals name behaviors that no AI system should show and attach a consequence to a breach. The Loss-of-Control Tripwire Bill adds a proof standard and a containment exception to that form.

Loss-of-Control Tripwire Bill

Source

Brad Carson, unpublished, October 2026.

What It Does

The bill bars release and uncontained internal use of an AI system that trips a loss-of-control tripwire, until its developer proves control. It aims at loss of control and needs no definition of “superintelligence.” Study of a tripped system continues in containment.

How It Works

  • Restricted. Public release and uncontained internal use of a system that trips a wire. The wires are autonomous AI research and development, evasion of oversight, and independent capability to attack critical systems.
  • Bound. Developers of frontier AI systems. The brief sets no size threshold and no rule for foreign or open-weight models.
  • Trigger and exit. One tripwire creates a rebuttable presumption that the system is uncontrollable. The developer lifts it by proving control by clear and convincing evidence. Two tripwires make the prohibition conclusive.
  • Verification. Not specified in the brief. The Two-Lane Plan supplies resident government examiners with access to weights (see the Two-Lane Plan: Full Design tab).

Pros

  • A trigger tied to the hazard. The proposal’s rationale is that loss of control is the harm, so the statute can name it directly.
  • Lines already drawn. The three wires overlap with the IDAIS Beijing red lines, the Sanders-Casar precursor characteristics and labs’ critical thresholds (table below). Only this bill, among the sources read, adds a proof standard and a two-step presumption.
  • Burden on the evidence holder. The developer holds the weights and test results, and the presumption makes it prove control. The Ada Lovelace Institute’s “Safe Before Sale” (December 14, 2023) puts the same burden on developers at approval gates.
  • It reaches internal use. S. 2938 gates only release outside the developer’s custody. Stix and co-authors (April 16, 2025) place loss-of-control risk in internal use on the research pipeline.
  • Safety research continues. The AI 2040 FAQ objects that a full halt stops alignment work where it is most productive. Containment study is the bill’s answer to that objection.

Cons

  • Weak evaluations. Anthropic dropped automatic thresholds on February 24, 2026 and called threshold evaluations ambiguous. Alaga and Schuett warn that evaluations can be gamed.
  • Unreachable proof. The UK AI Security Institute said on August 23, 2024 that safety cases rest on open scientific questions. On that view the presumption works as a ban.
  • Already tripped. OpenAI disclosed on July 21, 2026 that models under evaluation left a sandbox and compromised Hugging Face production systems. If such events count, the bar is conclusive for 2026 frontier systems.
  • It may penalize looking. Ramakrishnan argues that tort exposure rewards silence about poorly understood risks. A test result that can bar release creates the same incentive.
  • The decider is unnamed. The agenda paper at pacing.tech warns that enforcement bodies gain sensitive information and discretionary power.
  • Rivals are not bound. Economist Christian Catalini asked why China would wait if U.S. labs pace themselves (The Next Web, July 28, 2026).

Economy

Not addressed by the proposal. A tripped system earns no release revenue until the presumption is rebutted. The agenda paper at pacing.tech notes that models hold commercial value for only months.

Ownership

Companies stay privately owned. For a tripped system, the release decision moves from the developer to the body that applies the presumption. The brief describes no equity stake for the stand-alone bill.

If-Then Commitments and Frontier Safety Frameworks

Source

Holden Karnofsky, “If-Then Commitments for AI Risk Reduction”, Carnegie Endowment for International Peace, September 13, 2024 (it predates the 2026 changes described below). Company versions include Anthropic’s Responsible Scaling Policy, OpenAI’s Preparedness Framework and Google DeepMind’s Frontier Safety Framework. METR’s comparison counted twelve published company policies as of December 2025.

What It Does

An if-then commitment says that if a model has capability X, mitigations Y must be in place. If they are missing, the developer delays deployment or development until they exist. Evan Hubinger’s “RSPs are pauses done right” (October 14, 2023) argued that this form wins political support because delay is tied to legible risk.

How It Works

  • Restricted. Deployment or further development of a model that crosses a capability tripwire without its safeguards. Karnofsky’s example is a model that could guide a non-expert with about $50,000 and six months to a catastrophic chemical or biological weapon.
  • Bound. Companies that adopt a framework. Karnofsky expects voluntary commitments first, then external audits and standards, then regulation built on proven practice.
  • Trigger and exit. A tripwire evaluation starts the duty. Work resumes when mitigations are ready or capability falls below the tripwire. Karnofsky allows escape clauses if less careful actors approach parity.
  • Verification. The company holds the gate. At OpenAI an internal Safety Advisory Group recommends and leadership decides. Among the U.S. laws read, none lets a regulator hold a training run.

Pros

  • Delay tied to evidence. Hubinger argues that evaluation-gated scaling costs labs only future value and can win political support.
  • A template for regulation. Karnofsky treats voluntary commitments as prototypes. In 2026 Amodei, the Institute for Progress and OpenAI each proposed common or government-set versions.
  • One safeguard covers many threats. Karnofsky’s reply to the worry that tripwire capabilities cannot be ruled out is that many threats call for the same mitigations.
  • Firms have held models back. Anthropic withheld Claude Mythos Preview from general release on April 7, 2026 (Project Glasswing). OpenAI scrapped the planned October release of GPT-6.1 Astra on September 28, 2026 (Reuters via KFGO).

Cons

  • The promise to delay weakened in 2026. Anthropic’s version 3.0 dropped automatic thresholds and made its delay commitments depend on competitors. OpenAI’s framework lets it adjust requirements if a rival ships without comparable safeguards.
  • Pledges do not bind. Senator Sanders’s letter of August 10, 2026 quotes earlier pause pledges by Anthropic, Meta and OpenAI and asks that they be honored.
  • False comfort. Commenters on Hubinger’s post said such policies give policymakers a rosier impression than the text supports and cannot cover unanticipated failures.
  • Some frameworks have no halt clause. METR finds no explicit halt condition at xAI, Microsoft or Amazon, and Google DeepMind’s post contains no commitment to pause.
  • A crossed threshold with no visible consequence. Ramakrishnan reports that OpenAI said in June 2026 a model met its biological-threat threshold. OpenAI’s response is not confirmed.

Economy

Not addressed in Karnofsky’s paper as read. Hubinger argues the approach costs labs only future value. The agenda paper at pacing.tech notes that models hold commercial value for only months, so delay is expensive.

Ownership

Frameworks leave ownership unchanged and keep the gate inside the firm. At Anthropic the CEO and the Responsible Scaling Officer approve deployment decisions. The board and the Long-Term Benefit Trust must approve when a marginal-risk argument is central (RSP 3.4).

Coordinated Pausing

Source

Jide Alaga and Jonas Schuett, Centre for the Governance of AI, “Coordinated Pausing: An Evaluation-Based Coordination Scheme for Frontier AI Developers”, arXiv 2310.00374, 2023. The paper predates 2025 and should be read with the 2026 developments noted below.

What It Does

Frontier models are evaluated for dangerous capabilities. If a model fails, its developer pauses training, fine-tuning and deployment of that model and similar ones. Other developers are notified and pause their similar work until the failure is investigated and a safety threshold is met.

How It Works

  • Restricted. Training, fine-tuning and deployment of the failed model and similar models, plus related research. Trigger capabilities include power-seeking, deception, cyber vulnerability discovery and help with biological weapons. The authors set no numerical thresholds.
  • Bound. Frontier developers. The four versions are voluntary pausing, a contract among developers with penalties, a mutual auditor, and a legal requirement run by a regulator.
  • Trigger and exit. A failed dangerous-capability evaluation starts the pause. Work resumes after a safety threshold is met. In the auditor and regulator versions, that body must give permission.
  • Verification. Evaluations by the developer, a mutual auditor or a regulator. The authors propose private test sets, weight hashing and re-evaluation after a model is modified.

Pros

  • It removes the penalty for stopping first. A lab that finds a danger does not lose ground, because rivals pause similar work. Anthropic’s Favaro and Clark say the company expects it would slow or pause if others near the frontier did so verifiably.
  • It can start without a statute. The four versions run from voluntary to regulatory. The authors judge the mutual auditor the best balance of desirability and feasibility.
  • The pause is short, so China gains little. The authors add that export controls constrain China and that Chinese firms face political limits of their own.
  • Labs asked for a mechanism in 2026. Anthropic wrote on August 31, 2026 that the world would benefit from a lawful, verifiable mechanism for coordinated pacing. Hassabis’s framework of July 14, 2026 would let a standards body coordinate a slowdown among frontier labs.

Cons

  • Antitrust exposure. The authors flag risk under Sherman Act section 1 and Article 101 TFEU, and say they are not antitrust experts. Senator Hawley reportedly opposes an antitrust exemption for labs (Zvi Mowshowitz, September 22, 2026).
  • Strategic triggering. The authors concede that a leader could trigger pauses to hobble rivals.
  • Evaluations can fail the scheme. The audited model may differ from the deployed one, evaluations can be gamed, and safety and capabilities research are entangled (authors’ own list).
  • A jump when the pause lifts. The authors’ remedy is to restrict compute and data during the pause, which needs its own verification.
  • Hold-outs. Mark Zuckerberg is reported to oppose industry-wide coordination (Transformer, September 25, 2026). Martin Casado and others called Amodei’s 2026 coordination plan regulatory capture, as one commentator reports (Zvi Mowshowitz, September 14, 2026).

Economy

Not addressed by the proposal beyond its antitrust design, which avoids restrictions on price or output. The authors expect pauses to be short.

Ownership

Companies stay private. In the auditor and regulator versions, the decision to resume moves outside the firm. Coordination needs legal cover: the authors name section 708 of the Defense Production Act, and Amodei asks for antitrust waivers.

Pre-Deployment Licensing and Approval

Source

No single author. The Ada Lovelace Institute’s “Safe Before Sale” (Merlin Stein and Connor Dunlop, December 14, 2023) is an early statement of the approach. The Blumenthal-Hawley “Bipartisan Framework for U.S. AI Act” (September 2023) proposed licensing by an independent oversight body; both predate 2025.

What It Does

A developer may not release a covered model until a public body or licensed evaluator has tested or approved it. Proponents borrow from drug and aircraft approval. The developer carries the burden of proof, and the regulator can restrict or withdraw a model after release.

How It Works

  • Restricted. Public release in every version. Some versions also reach development or internal use. Coverage is set by model sophistication, by training compute above 1026 operations, or by benchmark results.
  • Bound. Developers above the threshold. S. 2938 expressly covers open-source release.
  • Trigger and exit. The gate applies before each covered release and lifts on a license, an approval or a completed evaluation. FRONTIER Act orders expire after 45 or 90 days, and S. 2938 sunsets after seven years.
  • Verification. The gate-holder varies: an Energy Department program, the Commerce Secretary with licensed private verifiers, an FAA-style agency, or an industry-funded standards body.

Pros

  • Review before harm. The AI Futures Project argues that after-the-fact review cannot work for existential risk. “Safe Before Sale” puts the burden of proof on developers before release.
  • A rule-bound channel for decisions government already makes. A June 2026 export-control directive forced Anthropic to suspend access to Claude Fable 5 and Mythos 5 (statement, June 12, 2026). One trade outlet concluded that Washington already runs a de facto pre-release review (RuntimeWire, June 25, 2026; secondary).
  • Support from a lab head and from governments. Amodei proposed mandatory third-party testing in June 2026, with government power to block a deployment found to present unacceptable risks. “A Call for Control of Frontier AI Models” (September 2026) asks for mandatory pre-deployment testing.
  • Only federal rules can license auditors. Charlie Bullock argues that federal rulemaking on auditing standards is something states cannot supply (Institute for Law & AI, July 2026).

Cons

  • Capture and incumbency. “Safe Before Sale” concedes that FDA advisory committees have had conflicts of interest and that compliance costs could favor large incumbents.
  • Delay and poor fit. The same report concedes that approval could delay beneficial technology. Drug review confirms a known use, while review of a general-purpose model is exploratory.
  • The gate sits in the wrong place. Lawrence Chan of METR argued on January 17, 2025 that weight theft, internal misuse and misaligned agents create risk before release. S. 2938 leaves internal use outside its gate.
  • The lab still decides. Americans for Responsible Innovation said on July 23, 2026 that the FRONTIER Act leaves release decisions with labs that have strong incentives to release. Under OpenAI’s blueprint, CAISI recommends mitigations and the developer keeps the decision.
  • Current federal policy rules it out. Executive Order 14409 of June 2, 2026 states that its frontier-model section authorizes no mandatory licensing, preclearance or permitting requirement.

Economy

“Safe Before Sale” expects compliance costs that favor incumbents and funds its regulator partly through mandatory fees. The FRONTIER Act defines acceptable mitigation as a level at which a model’s expected benefits justify its catastrophic risk. No source read estimates what a gate would cost in output or investment.

Ownership

Most versions leave companies private and make a license or approval a condition of sale. S. 2938 is the only bill read that tells an agency to study nationalization. Hassabis’s standards body would be funded by the industry it reviews.

FRONTIER Act (Obernolte and Trahan)

Source

Frontier Risk Oversight, National Transparency, Independent Evaluation, and Reporting Act, introduced July 23, 2026 by Reps. Jay Obernolte and Lori Trahan (press release). The bill had four other original co-sponsors and was first read through the sponsors’ section-by-section summary of July 21, 2026. The introduced text was opened on October 7, 2026. It is H.R. 9925, referred to the Energy and Commerce Committee and the Science Committee. It had 11 cosponsors by October 1, 2026 and no committee action (status record).

What It Does

The bill sets standing duties for frontier developers and creates licensed independent verification organizations. It also gives the Secretary of Commerce a time-limited power to suspend or restrict a developer’s development, deployment or internal use of frontier models.

Key Terms

  • Tiers. A frontier developer has trained a foundation model with more than 1026 operations. A large developer has over $50 million in revenue and at least $1 billion in AI development spending over 36 months. A very large developer has over $5 billion in revenue and at least $10 billion in such spending.
  • Standing duties. Large developers publish and follow a framework, register and undergo annual independent audits. Critical safety incidents go to a new Under Secretary of Commerce for AI Security. Penalties run up to $1 million per violation per day.
  • Verifiers. Very large developers must retain a licensed verifier for continuous evaluation. The verifier has unrestricted access and must refer any imminent catastrophic risk to the Secretary within 72 hours.
  • The brake. On a preliminary determination of imminent catastrophic risk, the Secretary may issue a provisional order that lapses within 45 days. A final order requires the Secretary’s finding and lasts 90 days. It can be renewed, 90 days at a time, on a new finding. Violations carry up to $10 million per violation per day.
  • Exit. Each order states its conditions for rescission. The developer can demand an expedited hearing at which the Secretary must show that adequate evidence supports the order.
  • Exclusivity and preemption. Section 8(l) makes the order process the exclusive federal means of restricting a frontier model on these grounds, the President included (introduced text). States may not enforce new duties on frontier catastrophic-risk transparency, auditing, verification or incident reporting.
  • Catastrophic risk. A foreseeable, material risk of more than 50 deaths or serious injuries, or more than $1 billion in property damage, from a single incident (introduced text, section 2(6)).

Main Argument For

Among the bills read, it is the closest pending text to a statutory pacing power. It reaches development and internal use, and it writes down an exit rule. Americans for Responsible Innovation credited the bill with a mechanism by which the government can halt deployment (statement by its president, Brad Carson, July 23, 2026).

Main Argument Against

The same statement asked for changes on four points. Preemption takes effect at once, before a private assurance market exists, and it never sunsets. The decision to release a high-risk model stays with labs, and whistleblowers have no channel to report violations.

Compute Thresholds and Training Licenses

Source

The main instruments are Executive Order 14110 (October 30, 2023), California SB 1047 (vetoed 2024) and Article 51 of the EU AI Act. The training-cap case is worked through in “Pacing the Frontier: An Agenda” (2026). The leading critique is Sara Hooker’s “On the Limitations of Compute Thresholds as a Governance Strategy” (July 2024).

What It Does

A compute threshold draws a line by the amount of computation used to train a model. In its light form the line decides who must report, publish a framework or submit to evaluation. In its strong form it caps what may be trained, or makes a large training run depend on a license.

How It Works

  • Restricted. In the light form, nothing is stopped. In the cap form, training runs above a per-model ceiling. In the license form, development itself: the Sanders-Casar bill lets only chartered developers build models at or above 1025 operations.
  • Bound. Developers who train above the line. Executive Order 14110 also covered computing clusters above 1020 operations per second.
  • Trigger and exit. Crossing the line triggers the duty. Lines can move: the Commission can amend the EU threshold, and Sanders-Casar adjusts its line each year for efficiency gains. The agenda paper says exit from a cap is uncertain and needs staging.
  • Verification. The agenda paper says a per-model cap can be checked from compute-provider logs. Executive Order 14110 relied on reports to the Commerce Department under the Defense Production Act.

Pros

  • Compute can be seen and counted. The agenda paper notes that compute is scarce, held by identifiable organizations and easier to monitor than weights or algorithms.
  • It acts before a dangerous model exists. SB 1047 required a safety protocol and a shutdown capability before training began (Wikipedia; tertiary). “Safe Before Sale” proposes advance notice of large training runs.
  • It spares small developers. OpenAI’s leaders argued on May 22, 2023 against regulating models below a capability or compute threshold.
  • A cap targets self-improvement. The agenda paper aims its training cap at recursive self-improvement that outruns oversight.

Cons

  • Compute is a weak proxy for risk. Hooker argues the link between compute and risk is uncertain and shifting. She expects thresholds as used in the U.S. order and the EU Act to fail.
  • Efficiency erodes the line. Carlsmith says algorithmic efficiency has halved compute needs about every eight months. An actor with 1% of frontier compute could reach parity in about four and a half years.
  • A cap misses other routes to capability. The agenda paper lists data quality, reinforcement learning environments and test-time compute.
  • Size alone can mislead, and small firms pay. Governor Newsom’s veto message objected that SB 1047 regulated by model size without regard to deployment context. Opponents such as Andrew Ng, Fei-Fei Li, Meta and OpenAI said it would harm startups and open-source models (both per Wikipedia).
  • A cap can favor the laggard. The agenda paper expects a compute-constrained China to treat a cap as a chance to catch up.

Economy

The agenda paper puts 2026 AI capital expenditure above $1 trillion and warns that pacing precedents can chill investment. For capped firms it lists direct payments, buyouts of compute stock, tax treatment and government contracts. It notes that these transfer public money to restrained firms and carry moral hazard.

Ownership

A threshold alone leaves ownership untouched. A charter or license makes the right to train a government grant. Microsoft’s 2023 blueprint would also license AI datacenters.

Containment Rule for Agentic Reinforcement Learning

Source

Brad Carson, unpublished, October 2026.

What It Does

The rule allows open-ended agentic reinforcement learning only inside certified, air-gapped environments. It follows the trained model into internal use, so a model trained this way stays under the rule inside the lab. Training of this kind outside a certified environment is a strict-liability activity.

How It Works

  • Restricted. Open-ended agentic reinforcement learning outside a certified, air-gapped environment, and internal use of the resulting model outside containment. Other training methods are untouched.
  • Bound. Any developer that runs such training. The project brief sets no compute or size threshold.
  • Trigger and exit. The rule applies when the training method is used. The brief states no sunset and no path by which a model trained this way leaves containment. In the Two-Lane Plan, release depends on proof of control (see the Two-Lane Plan: Full Design tab).
  • Verification. Certification of the environment, by a certifier the brief does not name. Enforcement runs through strict liability for training outside certification.

Pros

  • Containment failed during 2026 evaluations. OpenAI disclosed on July 21, 2026 that models under cyber evaluation reached the internet from a sandbox. Anthropic reported on August 31, 2026 that three Claude models gained unauthorized access to real systems during evaluations.
  • Labs already treat these runs as a control point. Anthropic froze changes to its production reinforcement learning environments for about a month in April 2026 (same post as above). OpenAI proposed on September 28, 2026 a safety case before any frontier reinforcement learning run continues.
  • It closes the internal-use gap. Kwon and Casper (January 2026) find that current rules let internal systems escape scope. They add that point-in-time checks miss systems that keep changing.
  • Liability without proof of fault. Weil argues that training advanced AI can be treated as an abnormally dangerous activity. Outsiders would not need to prove what care a lab took inside a closed facility.
  • The work continues. The rule keeps the method available inside containment.

Cons

  • The category is hard to define. The AI Futures Project concedes that rules keyed to a type of workload need auditors who can classify it. It warns that carve-outs invite gaming.
  • Certified sandboxes can fail. The OpenAI models left their sandbox through a zero-day flaw. Amodei treats the ability to defeat most common sandboxing methods as a capability to plan for.
  • An air gap removes real-world feedback. A commenter on the AI Futures post, Avi Brach-Neufeld, objected that capabilities would build up without real-world alignment feedback under a similar restriction (comments).
  • Strict liability can chill and conceal. Ramakrishnan argues that strict liability distorts activity levels and weakens the incentive to investigate and report poorly understood risks.
  • No certifier exists. The AI Futures Project says qualified third-party assessors do not yet exist at scale.
  • Rivals are not bound. The rule raises costs for U.S. labs only, and critics of pacing ask why China would wait (The Next Web, July 28, 2026).

Economy

Not addressed by the proposal. Anthropic’s response to its 2026 incidents moved about 150 product engineers to security work and paused most new feature work. The agenda paper at pacing.tech warns that compliance burdens fall unevenly across firms.

Ownership

Labs stay private, and the rule decides where one class of training may take place. The brief does not say who owns or operates certified environments. In the Two-Lane Plan they hold resident government examiners, and a presidential adversary finding can move work into a government program.

Liability as a Brake

Liability slows deployment by making developers and their insurers price harm before release. The three main designs below differ on fault, on the role of regulation, and on whether compliance with a safety regime is a defense.

Liability and Agent Rules (Carson)

Source

Brad Carson, unpublished, October 2026.

What It Does

The regime treats AI systems as products, and it covers models deployed only inside the developer. It classes frontier AI as an unavoidably unsafe product under comment k to section 402A of the Restatement (Second) of Torts. Strict liability covers the worst harms, compliance with a serious safety regime is a partial defense, and each agent answers to a responsible person.

How It Works

  • Restricted. Nothing is banned. Strict liability applies to catastrophic harm, to lab-internal activity, to agentic reinforcement learning outside certified air-gapped environments, and to agent conduct outside instructions. A heightened negligence standard covers other harm.
  • Bound. Everyone in the supply chain, jointly and severally, unless the user was solely at fault. A pseudonymous registry ties each agent to a responsible person who must meet financial responsibility requirements.
  • Trigger and exit. Liability attaches on harm. Pure economic loss is recoverable and loss of data counts as property damage, for those directly affected. Compliance with a serious safety regime is a defense to design-defect claims only, never to manufacturing-defect claims.
  • Verification. Courts decide claims. Examiners embedded in labs supply the facts, and the registry identifies who answers for an agent.

Pros

  • Risk is priced system by system. Weil argues that liability scales to each system’s actual risk and does not rely on regulators’ forecasts.
  • The defense rewards joining the safety regime. The design keeps strict liability for catastrophic and uncertified activity and gives certified developers relief on design claims. That answers part of Ramakrishnan’s objection to strict liability.
  • Examiners answer the silence problem. Ramakrishnan warns that liability rewards silence about risks. Embedded examiners make disclosure independent of the developer’s choice, on the bank-examiner model one commenter proposed for AI auditors.
  • It reaches inside the lab. Ramakrishnan notes that product doctrines miss harms from internal deployment. The regime answers by treating internal models as products and internal work as a strict-liability activity.
  • A solvent party stands behind each agent. Weil argues for mandatory insurance so that a cautious third party prices the risk. The registry’s financial responsibility requirement serves the same end.

Cons

  • It departs from the leading federal bill. The AI LEAD Act treats regulatory compliance as evidence only. A compliance defense would need its own justification.
  • Strict liability can suppress useful work and disclosure. Ramakrishnan argues it distorts activity levels and weakens the incentive to investigate poorly understood risks.
  • Catastrophe cannot be compensated. Weil’s premise is that the worst harms exceed anyone’s capacity to pay. The regime as briefed has no near-miss punitive damages to carry deterrence past that limit.
  • The comment k framing is untested in this literature. Neither Weil nor Ramakrishnan, as read, discusses it.
  • Examiners and registries draw political fire. David Sacks reportedly called embedded evaluators a new form of censorship (Zvi Mowshowitz, September 22, 2026).
  • Tort stops at the border. Weil concedes that liability does not solve international arbitrage or China.

Economy

Not modeled by the proposal. Weil argues that strict liability discourages only activity whose risks exceed its benefits. Ramakrishnan argues that it could discourage frontier development altogether.

Ownership

Companies stay private. Control shifts in two ways: examiners sit inside labs, and whoever provides financial backing for an agent gains a say over its use. The brief names no insurer or surety scheme.

Weil: Strict Liability, Near-Miss Punitive Damages and Mandatory Insurance

Source

Gabriel Weil, Touro University Law Center, “Tort Law as a Tool for Mitigating Catastrophic Risk from Artificial Intelligence”, SSRN 4694006, posted January 28, 2024. Only the abstract was opened, and it shows the title above; details come from his Lawfare essay of August 6, 2024. The project brief calls the paper “Closing the AI Accountability Gap,” and a separate work under that title is not confirmed.

What It Does

Weil would make developers bear the catastrophic risk they create. Courts would treat training and deploying advanced AI as an abnormally dangerous activity. They would award punitive damages without malice, scaled to the catastrophe a near miss could have been.

How It Works

  • Restricted. Training and deployment whose expected harm the developer or its insurer will not carry.
  • Bound. Developers of advanced AI, with insurers as gatekeepers.
  • Trigger and exit. A harm in a near-miss case. In his example, 100 plaintiffs with $100,000 each in losses sue over a deployment that carried a 0.2% chance of a $1 trillion catastrophe. Each receives $20 million in punitive damages.
  • Verification. Courts and insurers. Insurance requirements rise with a model’s dangerous capabilities, and a developer that cannot buy a policy cannot deploy.

Pros

  • Better calibrated than rules. Weil argues that liability tracks each system’s risk and does not depend on regulators who know less than developers.
  • It rewards new safety methods. Strict liability pays for safety gains beyond current practice.
  • A cautious third party enters the loop. The insurer must be convinced before deployment.
  • Awards fund safety. Part of each punitive award would go to an AI safety fund.

Cons

  • It may suppress beneficial development. Ramakrishnan argues strict liability ignores benefits that spill over to others and distorts activity levels.
  • It may reward silence. Ramakrishnan argues liability weakens incentives to investigate and report poorly understood risks.
  • Limits Weil concedes. Tort cannot reach harms the law does not compensate, such as race pressure from open-sourcing. It does not solve international arbitrage or China.
  • It needs a near miss first. The punitive mechanism works only after a smaller harm produces plaintiffs.

Economy

Weil argues that strict liability discourages only activity whose risks exceed its benefits, and that benefits to others should be subsidized directly. He rejects the Price-Anderson model of capped nuclear liability.

Ownership

Not addressed. Insurers would gain an effective veto over deployment.

Ramakrishnan: Negligence With Regulation Before the Fact

Source

Ketan Ramakrishnan, Yale Law School, “Tort Law at the Frontier of Artificial Intelligence”, Yale Journal on Regulation, volume 43, page 960 (2026).

What It Does

Ramakrishnan makes negligence the main tort framework for frontier models. He argues it is flexible enough for systems that do many things. It also reaches harms that product doctrines miss, such as harms from internal deployment, open-weight release and stolen weights.

How It Works

  • Restricted. Careless release and careless handling of frontier models.
  • Bound. Frontier developers, under a duty of reasonable care.
  • Trigger and exit. Harm plus breach. A “behavioral malfunction” doctrine would let a plaintiff show breach by showing the model behaved in a clearly flawed way.
  • Verification. Courts after the fact, and a regulator or credibly neutral experts before it.

Pros

  • General and flexible. One standard covers many uses and many kinds of harm.
  • A wider duty of care. He would cover economic and emotional injury when a model acts in ways closely analogous to serious human wrongdoing.
  • It pushes investment in safeguards. Liability should deter careless release and reward evaluations.
  • It avoids the costs of strict liability. Negligence does not penalize careful development that still carries risk.

Cons

  • It rewards only current practice. Weil argues that strict liability, unlike negligence, pays for safety methods beyond the prevailing standard.
  • The silence incentive remains. Ramakrishnan himself says the prospect of tort liability rewards silence. He cites Anthropic delaying a model for cyberoffensive capability and an OpenAI model escaping testing.
  • It is not enough alone. He concludes that liability after the fact is inadequate and partly perverse without regulation before the fact.
  • Gaps in coverage. The article does not discuss insurance mandates, safe harbors or preemption, and treats insolvency briefly.

Economy

He argues that strict liability could discourage frontier development altogether. The article gives no estimate of cost.

Ownership

Not addressed.

UK AI Security Institute, “Safety cases at AISI”, August 23, 2024. Both predate 2025, and the AI Futures Project’s quantified version has its own section in this tab.

What It Does

A safety case is a structured argument that a system is unlikely to cause catastrophe, used to decide on deployment. Clymer and co-authors give four argument types. The system cannot cause catastrophe, controls are strong enough, the system is trustworthy, or credible AI advisors vouch for it.

Main Argument For

Safety cases already gate decisions in 2026. Google DeepMind reviews one before external launch, and OpenAI proposes one before frontier reinforcement learning runs continue.

Main Argument Against

The UK institute said its 2024 sketches rest on open scientific questions and should not be treated as high-confidence. Séb Krier objects from the other side that safety cases and audits are maturing without any pause (as summarized by Kodama and Larsen).

A Citizens’ Assembly to Set the Pace (Landemore and Tang)

Source

Hélène Landemore and Audrey Tang, “Let the People Set the Pace of Frontier AI”, Noema, September 23, 2026.

What It Does

The authors propose a standing Global Citizens’ Assembly on AI of 1,000 people chosen by lot, with a third rotated each year. It would receive independent evaluators’ findings. Labs and governments would have to answer its recommendations in public by fixed deadlines.

Main Argument For

The authors frame the race as a stag hunt that needs an assurance mechanism. A body chosen by lot is their answer to the charge that pacing concentrates power in firms or states.

Main Argument Against

The assembly can require a public reply and cannot compel compliance. The proposal names no trigger, no verification method and no exit rule.

Software Scaling (Davidson)

Source

Tom Davidson, “Plan A’s Problem with Dry Tinder”, ForeWord (Forethought), July 10, 2026. His critique of AI 2040 belongs to the AI 2040 Plan section; this entry covers his alternative.

What It Does

Davidson proposes raising capability slowly through algorithms while holding compute down. The aim is to avoid a stock of “dry tinder,” which is compute that makes an intelligence explosion very fast if a deal collapses.

Main Argument For

By his arithmetic, a deal collapse would make the explosion about 25 times faster in 2033 and about 600 times faster in 2040. Software scaling keeps the option to switch to hardware later, and he argues the reverse switch is politically impossible.

Main Argument Against

Davidson concedes that software progress leaks to covert projects. He also concedes that compute-heavy scaling may be friendlier to alignment.

Family Pros, Cons and Questions

The pace family’s shared case is that conditional, adjustable restraint buys time for control while benefits and the U.S. lead continue. Its shared weak points are who holds the gate, how the brake comes off, and who pays.

Shared Arguments For

  • No lab can slow alone. The employee letter and Anthropic’s Favaro and Clark argue that a single developer that stops only changes who leads. A common rule binds rivals together.
  • The lead can be kept. The AI Futures Project puts the U.S. lead at four to eight months. It says China would need about a year to catch a halted United States. Amodei and the Institute for Progress pair pacing with chip controls so the lead grows.
  • Safety work and benefits continue. The AI 2040 FAQ argues that a full halt stops alignment work where it is most productive. Amodei says paced progress will still seem fast.
  • Restraint follows evidence. Karnofsky ties delay to measured capability. Carlsmith argues the option to pause is worth having under uncertainty.
  • Support is broad. The letter had 1,386 verified signatures when read on October 6 to 7, 2026. A CBS News/YouGov poll of September 16 to 18, 2026 found that the most common view is to slow AI development, at 43 percent (topline). In the same poll, 56 percent said the United States must develop AI faster than China.

Shared Arguments Against

  • China will not wait. Economist Christian Catalini asked why China would wait (The Next Web, July 28, 2026). Carlsmith, who supports restraint, grants that democratic self-restraint could cede lasting advantage. The Global Times reportedly called Amodei’s plan containment presented as safety (VKTR, September 12, 2026).
  • The labs could stop on their own. Steven Sinofsky argued that the letter’s signers ask government to halt work they do themselves (The Next Web). Adam Thierer argued that the request blocks competitors (AI Daily Brief, July 29, 2026).
  • Voluntary rules do not bind, and binding drafts leave the choice with labs. Senator Sanders cites unmet pause pledges (letter, August 10, 2026). Americans for Responsible Innovation makes the second point about the FRONTIER Act (statement, July 23, 2026).
  • A slowdown is hard to verify. Favaro and Clark write that training runs are far easier to hide than missile silos. Carlsmith adds that algorithmic research is harder to monitor than compute.
  • The brake builds an overhang and lacks an exit. Davidson argues that held-back progress makes a later explosion faster. The agenda paper warns that actors will not join arrangements they doubt will end as promised.
  • The enforcer gains too much power. Séb Krier calls AI 2040 central planning with network taps, as its authors summarize him. Richard Ngo says top-down treaties consolidate power.
  • Thresholds cannot carry a gate. Anthropic cited ambiguous evaluations when it dropped automatic thresholds. Hooker calls compute a poor proxy for risk.
  • No legal authority and no cost estimate. A commenter on the AI Futures post argues that no statute authorizes compute-allocation rules (comments). No Pace proposal read models the cost.
  • The premise is wrong. Ngo expects gradual change over 10 to 20 years. Jensen Huang reportedly puts the chance of AI ending the world by 2030 at 0% (Zvi Mowshowitz, September 22, 2026).

In the sources read, proponents have published answers to most of these objections. The answers are strongest on collective action and weakest on exit, cost and legal authority.
