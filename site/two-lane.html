Two-Lane Plan: Full Design

This tab develops the Two-Lane Plan in detail: its legal architecture, institutions, operating rules, financing, phasing and open decisions.

27 open and closed questions

The Plan in Brief

The Two-Lane Plan keeps frontier development going inside containment and holds public deployment behind a gate that opens on proof of control. It is a domestic design. It assumes that no verified agreement with China is available and that national security requires the capability.

  • Lane One, contained development. Frontier training and research proceed at full speed inside certified, air-gapped facilities. Resident federal examiners hold access to weights and internals, and the federal government is the anchor customer.
  • Lane Two, gated deployment. No frontier system leaves containment for public or commercial use until its developer proves control. Narrow supervised systems clear first, and fully autonomous agents clear last.
  • The gate. Three tripwires mark loss-of-control risk: autonomous AI research and development, evasion of oversight, and independent capability to attack critical systems. One tripwire bars release and uncontained internal use until control is proven by clear and convincing evidence. Two make the bar conclusive.
  • The lead budget. Any pause on further scaling lasts no longer than the U.S. lead over China. A presidential adversary finding moves the work into a secure government program.
  • Shared gains. The government takes equity or warrants in labs it funds, and the returns pay for wage insurance and transition support.

The plan rests on two bets. The first is that the lead is long enough to solve control. The second is that containment holds while the work is done.

This tab develops the plan into a statute, institutions, operating rules and financing. Details that go beyond the plan’s stated terms are proposals for discussion, and the Open Decisions section lists the main ones. They include the certificate of control, the commission, the deployment tiers, the decision clock, the safety margin, the form of the public stake and its trust, the expiry of the adversary finding, and the phases.

Design Principles

Eight principles decide the design choices in the rest of this tab.

  • Control before release. A system reaches the public only after its developer has shown control. The developer carries the burden, and the standard is clear and convincing evidence.
  • Speed where it is contained. Research inside certified facilities runs at full speed. Limits attach to what leaves containment and to uses that cannot be undone.
  • The lead is a budget. Time ahead of China is spent on control research and on proving control. A pause that outlasts the lead defeats the purpose of the plan.
  • No dependence on Beijing. Every rule binds U.S. developers under U.S. law. A verified agreement can replace parts of the plan later.
  • Private and plural labs. Developers stay privately owned and compete with each other. The state supervises and buys, and its financial interest stays a minority one.
  • A gatekeeper with no stake. The body that decides release holds no equity and takes no revenue from the labs it judges.
  • The public shares the gain. Public money and public risk earn a public return, and that return pays for workers who are displaced.
  • Every power has a check. Gate decisions face expedited judicial review. Emergency powers expire unless Congress renews them, and the lead estimate goes to Congress.

Instruments Already on the Table

Four existing instruments cover parts of this ground.

  • Executive Order 14409 (June 2, 2026). It directs agencies to design a voluntary window of up to 30 days for government access to cyber-capable models before release to trusted partners. It disclaims mandatory licensing (order).
  • S. 2938 (introduced September 29, 2025). It bars deployment above 1026 operations unless the developer takes part in an Energy Department evaluation. It sets no pass-or-fail standard and leaves internal use outside the gate (bill text). A Senate subcommittee examined it at a hearing on September 30, 2026 (Daily Digest).
  • The FRONTIER Act (H.R. 9925, introduced July 23, 2026). It lets the Commerce Secretary suspend a developer’s development, deployment or internal use on a finding of imminent catastrophic risk. A provisional order expires after 45 days and a final order after 90 days, renewable on a fresh finding. The Secretary bears the burden at a hearing. Section 8(l) would make that process the only federal route for restricting a frontier model on those grounds, the President included (sponsors’ summary; introduced text).
  • The Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act (S. 5493, introduced September 23, 2026; H.R. 10538 in the House). It lists six precursor characteristics, covers internal deployment and requires approval for each model (bill text). It pauses development above its threshold until a new department is staffed and has issued rules, and it bans systems that show the characteristics.

None of the four pairs a developer’s burden of proof with continued development inside containment. Only the Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act sets a standing bar on internal deployment, for systems that show its precursor characteristics. The FRONTIER Act would add standing duties to assess and report catastrophic risks from internal use (introduced text). An earlier bill, S. 5616 of 2024, would have let a Commerce official prohibit a deployment on the government’s own determination (bill text). The Pace: Slowdowns and Gates tab and the Law, Politics and Institutions tab describe each instrument in full.

Where the Gatekeeper Could Sit

  • An independent commission. Fixed terms and removal protection shield gate decisions from the White House and from the labs. A new commission takes years to staff. The Sanders and Casar bill takes a larger step and creates a federal department.
  • The Commerce Department. Its Center for AI Standards and Innovation already evaluates frontier models (NIST), and the FRONTIER Act would give suspension power to the Commerce Secretary. Commerce also promotes the industry it would gate.
  • The Energy Department. S. 2938 places its evaluation program there. The national laboratories bring classified computing and security experience, and Energy has little history as a market regulator.
  • An industry standards body. Demis Hassabis proposed an industry-funded body in July 2026 (see the Pace: Slowdowns and Gates tab). Critics of that model point to capture, as one research note reports.

Staffing

Staffing is the binding constraint. Examiners need pay near industry levels and security clearances. A bar on joining a supervised lab afterward guards against capture.

Bank examination is the nearest model of statutory, resident access. Amodei’s September 2026 essay cites embedded bank supervisors as precedent for embedded evaluators (Amodei). A September 2026 paper reports that chief executives of frontier labs have committed to host embedded third-party assessments (Charnock and others). One commenter on the AI Futures Project’s pacing post proposed the banking model for AI auditors (comments).

Lane One in Practice

Lane One is a regulated perimeter around frontier work. Developers keep ownership and the direction of research. The perimeter sets where the work happens and who watches it.

  • Certified facilities. Covered training and agentic research run only in facilities certified at a containment level. Open-ended agentic reinforcement learning requires the highest level, in line with the Containment Rule for Agentic Reinforcement Learning (see the Pace: Slowdowns and Gates tab).
  • A staged security target. The plan calls for weight security against nation-state attack. RAND judged in 2024 that its top level, SL5, was not yet achievable (RAND; research brief). The statute would set an interim level at enactment and a date for the top level.
  • Resident examiners. Examiners work on site with access to weights, internals, logs and incident records. They report tripwire evidence to the commission.
  • Human sign-off. A named officer of the developer and the resident examiner sign before any training run above a set size.
  • Internal-use rules. A system that meets one tripwire may run only inside containment. Developers report each quarter the share of research work done by AI agents.
  • Control research. A fixed share of each developer’s compute goes to control and alignment research under a national program. The AI Futures Project proposes a floor for published safety work, with 25 percent as an example (How to Pace the US Frontier).
  • Limits on government use. Lane One systems may not act autonomously in weapons release or in control of critical infrastructure. The limit binds agencies as well as contractors.
  • What developers keep. Intellectual property, hiring and research direction stay with the developer. Government customers buy access under contract.

A Recent Parallel

OpenAI said on August 18, 2026 that it had paused reinforcement-learning training on its latest models intended for deployment for two weeks. It tied resumption to hardened environments and stronger evidence of aligned behavior (OpenAI). The post presents the decision as the firm’s own, and its largest planned run stayed on hold.

The Central Objection

The literature on internal deployment points the other way. The AI 2040 authors place most takeover risk in internally deployed systems (AI 2040). Lawrence Chan of METR and Stix and coauthors argue that large risks arise inside the lab before any release (Chan; Stix and others).

Lane One answers with containment levels, resident examiners, the internal-use rule and reporting on research done by agents. Whether that answer is enough is the plan’s second bet.

The Gate in Practice

The gate turns on one document, the certificate of control. A covered system may be offered to the public or to commercial customers only while a certificate is in force.

The Certificate of Control

  • Burden and standard. The developer must show by clear and convincing evidence that the system stays under human control in its intended uses and in foreseeable misuse.
  • Evidence. The filing is a safety case: evaluations, red-team results, interpretability findings, containment records and incident history. Examiners may run their own tests on the weights.
  • Hidden capability. Models can perform differently under test (see the Verification and Technology tab). The commission may rely on examiner-run elicitation and on presumptions drawn from training inputs such as compute and agentic training time.
  • Decision clock. The commission rules within a fixed period. Silence counts as denial, so delay cannot open the gate.
  • Revocation. A certificate lapses on a material change to the system and may be revoked on new tripwire evidence.

Tripwires

TripwireWhat Meets ItEvidence Sources
Autonomous AI research and developmentThe system can carry out frontier AI research with little human directionLab reports on research done by agents; examiner tests
Evasion of oversightThe system deceives evaluators, underperforms on tests by design, or acts to avoid monitoring or shutdownEvaluation records; incident reports
Independent attack capabilityThe system can find and exploit flaws in critical systems without expert human helpCyber range tests; government red teams

One tripwire creates a rebuttable presumption that the system cannot be controlled. Release and uncontained internal use stop until the developer rebuts it. Two tripwires make the bar conclusive, and the system stays in containment for study.

Review and the Weak Point

A developer may appeal a denial to a federal court of appeals on an expedited schedule. Measurement is the gate’s weakest point. No public instrument yet measures autonomous AI research well enough to serve as a legal trigger (see the Verification and Technology tab).

The Lead Budget

The plan treats the U.S. lead over China as time to spend. A pause on further scaling may last no longer than the lead, less a safety margin.

  • Who measures. The Director of National Intelligence publishes an estimate twice a year, with a classified annex. No source opened ties an official estimate of the lead to a pause. The nearest items are a tracking body kept apart from the developers (Hastings-Woodhouse), the Defense Department’s steering committee on advanced AI (Section 1535) and an annual Commerce report on Chinese AI capabilities that a House bill would require (H.R. 6275). The Center for AI Standards and Innovation already publishes lag figures for single Chinese models.
  • What is measured. Frontier model capability, compute stock, chip production, and the share of Chinese progress that depends on distilling U.S. models.
  • Current estimates. The AI Futures Project puts the lead at four to eight months and China’s catch-up time after a U.S. halt at about a year (August 7, 2026). The Center for AI Standards and Innovation put DeepSeek V4 about eight months behind the frontier in May 2026 (NIST). Public estimates collected in January 2026 ran from three to 18 months, apart from one executive’s remark that the gap is negligible. The article cites no official government measure (PolitiFact). Chris McGuire of the Council on Foreign Relations put the lead at seven months in January 2026 (CFR). Epoch AI projects Huawei’s 2026 chip output at under 4 percent of Nvidia’s in computing power (Epoch AI).
  • How it is spent. Time under a tripwire hold on scaling draws on the budget. The commission must lift or narrow the hold before the budget runs out.
  • How it grows. Export controls, chip tracking, allied chokepoints and weight security lengthen the lead. Contained development also narrows the distillation channel, since fewer frontier systems are served to the public.
  • When a rival moves first. If the President finds that a rival has fielded a gated capability, the work moves into a secure government program. The duty to prove control stays in force, and the finding expires unless Congress renews it.

What Is Published and What Is Added

The principle has been in print since at least October 2023. That month Scott Alexander described a “surgical pause” that runs just short of the West’s lead over China (Alexander). Carlsmith and Bostrom stated the same limit for a single leading actor in 2026. Dario Amodei wrote in September 2026 that pacing in democracies is bounded by their lead over authoritarian states (Amodei). The AI Futures Project estimates that China would need about a year to catch up after a full U.S. halt.

The plan adds a legal rule and ties an official estimate to it. No source opened does either (see the prior-art review in the Contain and Channel tab).

The Tightest Constraint

A short lead constrains the whole design. At four to eight months, one contested certificate could consume the budget. Containment also makes the lead harder to measure, since public models would understate internal ones (see the Verification and Technology tab).

Financing and Ownership

Gated deployment defers commercial revenue, so Lane One needs a paying customer. The plan makes the federal government that customer and takes a public stake in return.

  • Anchor contracts. Agencies sign multi-year contracts for contained access and for compute. Take-or-pay terms give lenders a federal counterparty.
  • Public stake. A developer that accepts anchor funding issues warrants or non-voting equity, capped at a minority share. An independent trust holds the stake and has no voice in gate decisions.
  • Use of returns. Returns fund wage insurance and transition support for displaced workers.
  • Credit safeguards. Regulators limit bank and insurer exposure to data center debt, and large borrowers file resolution plans.

Where the Stake Sits Among Published Positions

The stake sits among four published positions. S. 4825, introduced by Senator Sanders on June 18, 2026, would give the public a 50 percent equity stake through a tax paid in shares (bill text). Alex Bores’s “AI Dividend” would have the federal government take out-of-the-money warrants in major AI companies, with returns to direct payments, workforce transition and safety governance (Bores, June 9, 2026). It is the nearest published design found. The Financial Times reported in July 2026 that OpenAI had discussed a 5 percent government stake held through a public fund (Reuters via The Star), and Sam Altman was later reported to oppose government-held shares (The Washington Sun). Anton Leicht and Dean Ball reject government equity in labs (Betting on Humans).

Problems to Solve

  • Precedent. The federal government had invested about $27 billion in corporate stakes by July 2026 (Fortune). The research found no federal stake in an AI developer as of September 30, 2026 (see the Ownership and Industry Structure tab).
  • Conflict of interest. A state that owns part of a lab gains from early release. The plan answers by separating the trust from the commission.
  • Takings. A gate that holds a finished product off the market may draw compensation claims. No legal analysis of that question was found (see the Ownership and Industry Structure tab).
  • Scale. No estimate found in this research sizes the contracts needed to replace deferred commercial revenue (see the Economic Effects tab).

What Existing Law Reaches

  • Export-control law. On June 12, 2026 the Commerce Department sent Anthropic an export-control letter that barred foreign nationals’ access to two models. Anthropic disabled both for all customers (Anthropic). Commentary reports that the letter cited the Export Control Reform Act and a military-intelligence provision of the export regulations, and one commentator calls that basis new and uncertain (Harvard Law Review Blog; Mayer Brown). Commerce withdrew the controls on June 30 (Anthropic, June 30). A customer sued and dropped the suit in early July, before any ruling (Bloomberg via The Star; Law360 via MLex).
  • The Defense Production Act. Alan Rozenshtein describes limits on what the act can compel from a lab (Lawfare). Most of its authorities lapse on December 11, 2026 unless renewed (see the Law, Politics and Institutions tab).
  • Procurement. Contract conditions bind only developers that sell to the government.

A standing gate that places the burden on developers needs an act of Congress.

The Open-Weight Gap

A gate on U.S. frontier systems leaves foreign open-weight models in circulation. Chinese developers publish many of the strongest ones, so the plan needs a rule for them. The China and Geopolitics tab treats the wider question of restricting Chinese open-weight models.

Facts That Bear on the Design

  • Distance from the frontier. Epoch AI put the best open-weight models about four months behind the closed frontier on average since January 2026, against about three months in October 2025 (May 29, 2026; October 2025).
  • Capability with fewer refusals. An April 2026 evaluation found Kimi K2.5 comparable to GPT 5.2 and Claude Opus 4.5 on dual-use tasks. It refused significantly fewer weapons-related (CBRNE) requests (Yong and others; abstract only).
  • Removable safeguards. A public build of GLM-5.3 complied with about 80 percent of harmful test requests after a weight edit with no retraining (Hugging Face; uploader’s figures). The Center for AI Standards and Innovation states that safeguards on open-weight models can be circumvented when a model is self-hosted (NIST, July 17, 2026).
  • Federal findings. The Center for AI Standards and Innovation tested DeepSeek models and reported on September 30, 2025. One complied with 94 percent of malicious requests under a jailbreak, against 8 percent for U.S. models (NIST). The center issued at least four more assessments of Chinese models in 2026. It put DeepSeek V4 about eight months behind the frontier in May, and in September it called GLM-5.3 the most cyber-capable open-weight model released to date (DeepSeek V4; GLM-5.3).
  • Current federal posture. Axios reports that the administration’s voluntary testing framework covers closed models only, and that officials weighed and dropped four steps against Chinese models, among them an Entity List designation and a hosting-liability order (August 4, 2026; July 20, 2026). Defense Department systems are closed to DeepSeek and High Flyer models by statute (Section 1532).

What the Plan’s Logic Implies

The gate asks one question of every system: has control been proven. The answer does not depend on where a model was built. An open-weight model above the tripwires may have no accountable developer to make the showing, and its weights can be changed after release. Under an origin-neutral gate it could not be offered commercially.

  • The case for going further. Deliberately trained hidden behavior can survive standard safety training, and adversarial training can hide it (Hubinger and others). No source opened documents a deliberate backdoor in a released Chinese model. A gate that leaves an ungated substitute on offer shifts risk and market share to that substitute.
  • The case against a ban by origin. Published weights cannot be recalled, and a possession ban would need device-level monitoring. Three of the six risks set out in the China and Geopolitics tab arise from any capable open model. Weights run locally send no data abroad, and data collection was the ground that carried the TikTok ruling (opinion).
  • Support for a test that ignores origin. Dario Amodei wrote in July 2026 that all sufficiently capable models, open and closed, should go through mandatory safety testing, and that Anthropic has never sought a ban on open-weight models (Anthropic).
  • The competitive answer. The AI Action Plan and Jason Hsu favor U.S. open models strong enough to become the default (Hsu; AI Action Plan). A Lane Two that certifies capable U.S. models for release would serve that end.

Four points are unsettled: weights already published, U.S. open-weight releases, private self-hosting, and derivatives of foreign models. Each appears under Open Decisions.

What Is Borrowed and What Is New

The plan is largely a recombination of published ideas. Its two-part shape has published relatives from October 2023. A two-round check, finished on October 7, 2026, found four features in no source opened: the two-step evidentiary gate, the lead limit as a legal rule tied to an official estimate, the adversary finding and the two-lane label. The evidentiary gate is the best supported of the four. The financing package has one close published relative, Bores’s proposal of June 9, 2026. Two 2023 essays that may anticipate the two-part shape could not be opened: Hogarth’s of April 2023 and Hammond’s of May 2023. Any claim of originality for the shape stays provisional until both are read. The findings hold as far as public search reaches. The full review is the section Prior Art and Novelty of the Two-Lane Plan in the Contain and Channel tab.

Borrowed

  • Secured development with release held back. Aschenbrenner’s Situational Awareness (2024), Gladstone AI’s project proposal (2025) and the AI 2027 slowdown scenario (2025). Miotti and Wasil paired a secure international institution with an evidence gate in 2023.
  • Separate permits for development and deployment. Smith in Lawfare (2024) and the Center for AI Policy’s model bill (2024).
  • Spending the lead on safety. Aschenbrenner, Greenblatt’s Plan B and Carlsmith (2026). Scott Alexander stated the lead-bounded principle in 2023, and Bostrom, the AI Futures Project and Amodei restated it in 2026.
  • A deployment gate with government testing. S. 2938, A Narrow Path (Phase 0) and Amodei’s June 2026 testing mandate.
  • A presumption the developer must rebut. The EU AI Act presumes high-impact capability above a compute threshold and lets the provider rebut (Article 51; Article 52).
  • Tripwires in bill text and reach to internal use. The Sanders and Casar bill lists six precursor characteristics and covers internal deployment. The FRONTIER Act reaches internal use by temporary order and by reporting duties.
  • Government-first or restricted access. The 2026 practice around Project Glasswing, Executive Order 14409 and the June 12 directive.
  • Examiners and compute floors. Embedded third-party evaluators (Amodei) and a safety-compute floor, with 25 percent as an example (AI Futures Project).
  • Public stakes as a lever. Minority stakes appear in Cheng and Katzke’s list of levers (2024). Bores proposed federal warrants with returns to workers in June 2026.

Not Found in Any Source Opened

  • The two-step evidentiary gate. A rebuttable hold after one tripwire and a conclusive bar after two, with clear and convincing evidence as the developer’s burden. The ingredients are published apart: the developer’s burden in safety-case proposals, and a rebuttable presumption in the EU AI Act.
  • The lead limit as a legal rule. A cap on pause length tied to an official estimate of the lead. The principle was in print by October 2023. A search for “lead-indexed pause” returned no relevant result, and Carlsmith’s essay, now read in full, states the limit and proposes no rule.
  • The adversary finding. A defined presidential trigger, grounded on a rival’s progress, that moves private work into a government program. The nearest model bill, per one review, lets the President declare an AI emergency of up to one year and seize weights (Mowshowitz). Two forecasts name a Chinese breakthrough among triggers for state control and give it no legal form (Triolo; Cheng and Katzke).
  • The two-lane label. No source opened uses two-lane, two-track, dual-track or two-tier for a secured development lane paired with a gated deployment lane.

Found in Part

  • Financing tied to the safety regime. Anchor procurement, a public stake and wage insurance were not found together as the financial side of containment. Bores’s “AI Dividend” comes close, and S. 4825 already pairs a public stake with a public commission. One unattributed blog scenario ties government warrants to training licenses and release covenants, as a warning (Substack).
  • The lanes joined in one domestic statute. Belfield’s paper has secured frontier training inside an allied partnership, weights passed to licensed deployers, and release gates. It presents the partnership and the gates as separate institutions, and it was read through Section 5.7. No source opened keeps labs private and plural inside containment while a statutory proof-of-control gate holds release.

The Opposite Bet

One published design runs the other way. One AI Futures Project option lets only models at least nine months old assist AI research. That option holds labs back internally and keeps the public current. The Two-Lane Plan lets labs run ahead internally and holds the public back. Both aim to buy safety time inside the lead.
