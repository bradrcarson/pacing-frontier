How to Use Historical Cases

Historical cases show which designs for restraint have survived contact with rivals. They cannot settle whether a pause of frontier AI is feasible or wise.

  • What a case can show. Restraint has happened more often than the view that technology is inevitable allows, and domestic politics drove much of it (Maas 2022). The Hardwired Pause working paper (October 2026, unpublished draft) draws five lessons (§2.2). They include observable units, imperfect monitoring, benefits for signatories and funded institutions.
  • What a case cannot show. Maas says his survey does not show that restraining advanced AI is desirable, easy or wise. He adds that history gives no blueprint (Maas 2022).
  • Known distortions. Restraint is undercounted, because unbuilt technologies become footnotes and abandoned projects are later assumed unworkable (Maas 2022). Analogies breed false confidence, get selected by interested parties, match on surface features and age as technology changes (Maas 2023).
  • Restraint or non-viability. Maas warns that the two are easily confused (Maas 2022). Human cloning is the example: animal cloning is inefficient and causes defects (NHGRI), so the share owed to norms is hard to measure.
  • Features that make a case comparable. Scharre and Lamberth test arms control by desirability and feasibility: military value, clear lines, verifiability and the number of necessary parties (CNAS 2022). Vermeer’s RAND study adds consensus about risk, physical assets, resource barriers and public or private deployment (RAND 2024). The working paper adds scale: whether a significant capability fits in a small building (§2.2.2).
  • Where frontier AI sits. Among the cases in this tab, none combined high commercial value, high military value and low observability and still produced durable restraint. That is this project’s reading; no source states it in this form.
  • How the families use history. As far as the page summaries show, the 2025 and 2026 pause proposals lean on nuclear and chemical precedents and seldom discuss failures. Scher and coauthors on the NPT and the working paper on INF and the JCPOA are exceptions. Historical writing by the Continue family was not opened (Not confirmed).
  • Data caveat. Figures in this tab came through a tool that returns machine summaries of pages. Dates and figures should be rechecked against the originals before publication.

Nuclear Arms Control

Verification arrived in stages, from none in 1958 to on-site inspection in 1987. The two most intrusive regimes, INF and the JCPOA, still ended by political decision.

  • Baruch Plan (1946). The United States proposed an international authority to own every weapons-capable atomic facility, with inspection, which the Soviet Union refused (State Department). On December 30, 1946 the UN Atomic Energy Commission approved its report 10 to 0, and the Soviet Union and Poland abstained (Australian cable; report reprint). The report went to the Security Council, and the State Department’s summary says the abstentions denied the unanimity needed to adopt the plan (State Department).
    • Relevance. Zaidi and Dafoe find that existential threat makes radical schemes thinkable, with fragile support (GovAI summary).
    • Limits. One state held the technology in 1946; private firms in rival states hold frontier AI (project reading).
  • Test Moratorium and Limited Test Ban (1958 to 1963). After a 1958 experts’ conference found a ban verifiable, the Soviet Union, United States and Britain stopped testing by reciprocal declaration. No verification was agreed (Arms Control Association (ACA)), and the Soviet Union resumed testing on September 1, 1961, after an announcement on August 30 (State Department; Department of Energy). The treaty of August 5, 1963 banned atmospheric, space and underwater tests, checked by national technical means (State Department).
    • Relevance. The Hardwired Pause working paper models itself on the 1958 experts and treats the test bans as binding norms (§§1.2, 2.2.3).
    • Limits. The experts’ 160 to 170 control posts were never built (Console 2025). Underground testing stayed legal, as algorithm research would under a data center limit (project reading).
  • NPT and IAEA Safeguards (1970). The treaty had 190 parties as of May 2025, and non-weapon states accept IAEA safeguards (ACA; IAEA). Iraq evaded safeguards limited to declared sites, and the 1997 Additional Protocol widened access (ACA).
    • Relevance. Baker finds no known major failure where both safeguards instruments apply (Baker 2023).
    • Limits. Scher and coauthors note that India, Pakistan, Israel and North Korea still acquired weapons (Scher et al. 2025). The 2026 Review Conference was the third straight failure (Arms Control Today).
  • SALT I and the ABM Treaty (1972). The United States and Soviet Union signed on May 26, 1972 and limited missile defenses to two sites each (State Department). Verification was by national technical means; the United States withdrew effective June 13, 2002 (ACA). SALT II (1979) was never ratified, though both sides pledged to observe it.
    • Relevance. Baker reports early agreement on remote monitoring and suggests satellite and power monitoring of data centers (Baker 2023).
    • Limits. Silos are countable from orbit. Data center contents are hidden (project reading).
  • INF Treaty (1987). The United States and Soviet Union destroyed 2,692 ground-launched missiles with ranges of 500 to 5,500 kilometers. Inspections ended on May 31, 2001, and the United States withdrew on August 2, 2019 over an alleged Russian violation (ACA).
    • Relevance. Scher and coauthors borrow its party-run verification (Scher et al. 2025).
    • Limits. The working paper uses it as its cautionary case: intrusive verification did not prevent collapse (§2.2.3). China, a non-party, was a cited concern.
  • Comprehensive Test Ban Treaty (1996). By July 2024, 178 states had ratified, but nine required states had not, so the treaty is not in force (ACA). Its monitoring system detected all six North Korean tests (Baker 2023).
    • Relevance. The working paper treats the monitoring system as a model for a pause authority (§3.6).
    • Limits. A training run has no global signature (project reading). In February 2026 U.S. officials alleged a concealed Chinese test in 2020; China denied it (VCDNP).
  • Iran Nuclear Deal (2015). Iran and seven partners agreed limits on centrifuges, enrichment and stockpile under continuous IAEA monitoring (ACA). The United States withdrew in May 2018, and UN sanctions returned on September 27, 2025 (CRS).
    • Relevance. The working paper uses it to show that sunset clauses make agreement possible (§2.2.1).
    • Limits. Many states pressed one state. A U.S.-China agreement would have no outside enforcer (project reading).

Biological and Chemical Restraint

The inspected chemical convention verified destruction of all declared stocks; the uninspected biological convention was violated at scale. Science-led pauses were short and ended in regulated resumption, except for heritable genome editing.

  • Biological Weapons Convention (1975). The treaty bans biological agents and toxins for hostile purposes, with 188 parties in 2025 and no inspection mechanism (ACA; Nuclear Threat Initiative (NTI)). The Soviet Union kept an offensive program after ratifying, while NTI credits the treaty with the dismantling of more than 20 programs. Its support unit had four staff and about $2.1 million in 2025, against about $94 million for the chemical weapons body (NTI).
    • Relevance. The Hardwired Pause working paper concludes that agreements need verifiable units and a funded institution (§§2.2.2, 2.2.5). The United States rejected a verification protocol in July 2001 on security and commercial-secrecy grounds (ACA). That previews objections to inspecting AI labs (project reading).
    • Limits. The working paper ties the gap to scale. A pathogen program fits in a small building; frontier training needs very large chip fleets for now (§2.2.2).
  • Chemical Weapons Convention (1997). The 193 parties accept declarations, routine inspections of military and industrial sites, and challenge inspections (ACA). As of December 31, 2025, all 72,304 declared tonnes were verified destroyed (OPCW). Syria joined in 2013, and sarin and chlorine attacks followed in 2017 and 2018 (ACA).
    • Relevance. Scher and coauthors model challenge inspections on it (Scher et al. 2025). It is the main precedent for inspecting a large dual-use private industry (project reading).
    • Limits. It verified declared stocks and did not stop undeclared use. The 1925 Geneva Protocol, with no institution, was violated by Italy and Iraq (working paper, §2.2.5).
  • Asilomar (1974 to 1975). A 1974 letter from a committee led by Paul Berg asked scientists to halt certain recombinant DNA experiments. About 140 participants met in February 1975 and replaced the moratorium with graded containment rules. Those became NIH guidelines in 1976 (Berg 2008).
    • Relevance. Grace cites it as proof that researchers have organized a moratorium and then rules (Grace 2022). Vermeer treats genetic engineering as the best model for accessible technology with serious risk (RAND 2024).
    • Limits. Berg doubts it could be repeated now that many scientists work for companies. Yudkowsky and Soares argue that AI will need legal restriction (online book resources).
  • Gain-of-Function Funding Pauses (2014 to 2017; 2025 to date). On October 17, 2014 the White House paused new federal funding for certain influenza, SARS and MERS experiments (White House 2014). NIH lifted the pause on December 19, 2017, after about three years against a planned one (NIH 2017). Executive Order 14292 of May 5, 2025 halted funding again, and a July 28, 2026 notice announced a federal funding ban (CRS; NIH 2026).
    • Relevance. Per a page summary, Carlsmith cites these restrictions as evidence that governments can prohibit research categories at home. He adds that AI’s commercial incentives are stronger (Carlsmith 2026).
    • Limits. The lever was federal grant money. Frontier AI is privately financed, and the pause bound no other country (project reading).
  • Heritable Genome Editing (2018 to date). After He Jiankui announced gene-edited babies in 2018, a Chinese court sentenced him to three years in prison (Nature). About 30 countries already prohibited the practice, and scientists called for a moratorium in March 2019 (Lander et al. 2019). The WHO recommended a registry and whistleblowing mechanism in 2021, and three societies sought a ten-year moratorium on May 27, 2025 (WHO; joint statement).
    • Relevance. Grace uses it against the claim that China would never restrain a technology (Grace 2022).
    • Limits. The societies say no medical need exists because alternatives do. Frontier AI has large demand and no agreed substitute (project reading).

Naval Arms Limitation

On this project’s reading, the interwar naval treaties are the closest precedent for a negotiated pause. Rival powers halted construction of the era’s decisive weapon, scrapped ships and fixed ratios without inspectors. The limits held about 14 years and ended when Japan walked out.

  • What was restrained. Article IV of the Five-Power Treaty capped capital ship tonnage at 525,000 tons each for the United States and Britain and 315,000 for Japan. France and Italy got 175,000 each (treaty text). The State Department’s summary gives 500,000 and 300,000, and says the treaty halted capital ship construction and required older ships to be scrapped (State Department).
  • Who agreed. The five powers met in Washington from November 1921 to February 1922. Japan had sought a 10:10:7 ratio and accepted 5:5:3. The bargain included a freeze on Pacific fortifications and companion treaties on East Asia (State Department).
  • How compliance was checked. Scharre and Lamberth class the treaty as having no formal verification (CNAS 2022). The treaty text requires each party to tell the others about replacement ships: names, keel dates, displacement and completion dates (treaty text). A machine-summarized reading of the full text found no inspection clause.
  • What happened. Unrestricted ship classes produced a cruiser race after 1922, and a 1927 conference failed. The London treaty of 1930 capped cruisers for the United States, Britain and Japan and extended the capital ship holiday to 1936 (State Department). Japan walked out of the 1935 to 1936 conference, and the treaty of March 25, 1936 provided mainly for consultation (Britannica).
  • Arguments for relevance.
    • Scher and coauthors cite the scrapping of dozens of capital ships as precedent for limits set below current capability (Scher et al. 2025).
    • Great powers limited their decisive weapon, and Scharre and Lamberth name cost as a primary motive (CNAS 2022).
    • The cruiser race is a precedent for the worry that a compute cap pushes effort into algorithms and unregulated chip classes (project reading).
  • Arguments against relevance.
    • Battleships took years to build in known yards and had no civilian market. A frontier model trains in months inside an ordinary-looking data center and can be copied (project reading).
    • Tons and gun caliber were a fixed unit of account. Scher and coauthors cite a threefold yearly fall in the compute needed for a given capability (Scher et al. 2025).
    • The limits came with a political settlement in East Asia. An AI pause without a wider U.S.-China understanding would lack that support (project reading).
  • What the ending shows. The ratio lasted while the least satisfied party tolerated it. On this project’s reading, a lead-indexed pause that freezes a U.S. advantage has the structure Japan rejected. The Two-Lane Plan’s version is unilateral, so the parallel applies only if China were asked to accept it.
  • Across the families. The draft agreement by Scher and coauthors, a Stop proposal, cites the scrapping as precedent. The cruiser race bears on Pace proposals built on compute limits (project reading). The walkout is available to Continue arguments, though no such use was found.
  • Not confirmed. Cheating by signatories and the number of ships scrapped. Article XXIII keeps the treaty in force until December 31, 1936, subject to two years’ notice (treaty text). The date of Japan’s notice of termination was not confirmed. No article applying the naval treaties to AI was found.

Environmental and Resource Moratoria

On this project’s reading, these are the most successful restraint cases in the tab, and each succeeded under conditions that transfer poorly. Restraint held in proportion to how little it cost the parties that mattered.

  • Montreal Protocol (1987). Adopted on September 16, 1987, the protocol phases out nearly 100 ozone-depleting chemicals and has universal ratification (UNEP OzonAction; Ozone Secretariat). Developing countries got later deadlines, and a fund created in 1991 has disbursed more than $3.9 billion across more than 8,600 projects. The parties meet yearly, can adjust the schedules, and extended the regime to hydrofluorocarbons in 2016.
    • Compliance checks. Reporting and non-compliance procedures were not described on the pages opened (Not confirmed).
    • Outcome. UNEP reports 98 percent of ozone-depleting substances phased out against 1990 levels (UNEP OzonAction).
    • Relevance. The Hardwired Pause working paper lists it as a positive institutional example, in a paragraph still in draft (§2.2.5). AI Impacts lists chlorofluorocarbons among plausible cases of profitable technologies given up (AI Impacts).
    • Limits. Substitutes existed, and the phase-out was gradual with compensation. A frontier pause offers no full substitute, though the working paper’s inference-only chips attempt one (project reading).
  • Commercial Whaling Moratorium (1982, effective 1985/86). The International Whaling Commission decided in 1982 to pause commercial whaling on all species from the 1985/86 season (IWC). Members that lodged an objection or reservation are not bound. Norway and Iceland whale commercially on that basis, and Japan left in 2019 and resumed.
    • Compliance checks. Not described on the page opened.
    • Outcome. The pause remains in place after four decades. A catch-limit procedure adopted in 1994 has never been applied (IWC).
    • Relevance. The case supports critics’ worry that a temporary AI pause would lack an exit. The states with the strongest interest objected or left, the expected pattern for a pause that binds only the willing (project reading).
    • Limits. No source applying the case to AI was found.
  • Antarctic Mining Ban (1991, in force 1998). The Madrid Protocol, signed on October 4, 1991, prohibits mineral resource activity other than scientific research (Antarctic Treaty Secretariat). Until 2048 it can change only by unanimous agreement of the consultative parties. From 2048 a change needs a majority including three-quarters of the 26 original consultative parties, and lifting the ban requires a binding minerals regime.
    • Compliance checks. Not described on the page opened.
    • Outcome. In force.
    • Relevance. It is a drafting model for exit conditions: a review date, a supermajority, and a replacement regime before the ban lifts (project reading).
    • Limits. The ban forgoes an activity that is not yet commercially pursued. Scharre and Lamberth explain the related ban on weapons in Antarctica by low military value and a clear focal point (CNAS 2022).
  • Exit design. Madrid wrote its exit rule in advance. The whaling moratorium did not and has never ended. The Embargo Plus U.S. Pause breakout clause and the Two-Lane Plan’s lead-indexed pause are rules of the first kind (project reading).
  • Across the families. A Stop proposal, the Hardwired Pause, cites Montreal as an institutional example. Montreal’s adjustable schedules resemble a Pace instrument, and the whaling case is available to Continue arguments about exits (project reading).
  • Not confirmed. The role of industry and substitutes in Montreal, its trade measures against non-parties, the 1988 Antarctic minerals convention and whale catch numbers.

Abandoned and Restrained Technologies

Outside arms control, restraint came mostly from domestic politics: regulators, funders, referendums and public opposition. The same cases show restraint reversing when politics change.

  • Supersonic Flight Over Land (1973 to 2025). The Federal Aviation Administration banned civil supersonic flight over the United States in 1973 because of the sonic boom (Dourado and Hammond 2016). Executive Order 14304 of June 6, 2025 ordered repeal within 180 days; completion is Not confirmed (White House).
    • Relevance. Dourado and Hammond argue the rule froze development for more than 40 years. Advocates can cite its effect and critics its cost (project reading).
  • Civilian Nuclear Power (1970s to date). Heninger finds that U.S. reactors begun in the late 1970s took 2.4 times as long and cost 9 times as much as 1960s reactors. U.S. new starts halted in the late 1970s, Italy closed its reactors after a 1987 referendum, and Germany completed its phase-out in 2023 (AI Impacts). U.S. starts resumed in 2013 at Vogtle, since completed, and at Summer, later abandoned (World Nuclear Association); the Energy Information Administration dates Vogtle construction from 2009 (EIA).
    • Relevance. AI Impacts concludes that regulation can suppress a technology without a ban and that blocked deployment slows development (Korzekwa 2023). Korzekwa says his write-up is more confident than his beliefs. Hanson cites the case as regulation that destroyed most of a technology’s potential (Hanson 2023).
    • Limits. Korzekwa notes that nuclear risk scales with deployment, while one dangerous AI system could do global harm with little deployment.
  • Human Reproductive Cloning. No claim of a cloned human has been substantiated, and animal cloning is inefficient (NHGRI). The legal mechanism was not established in the sources opened.
    • Relevance. Grace lists cloning among technologies that societies declined to pursue (Grace 2022).
    • Limits. Maas warns that restraint and non-viability are easily confused (Maas 2022).
  • Geoengineering Experiments (2010 to date). A nonbinding 2010 decision under the Convention on Biological Diversity advises against geoengineering that may affect biodiversity, with a small-scale research exception (CBD). Heninger reported in 2023 that funders rarely pay for it and that neither of two attempted stratospheric experiments went ahead (AI Impacts). Harvard’s SCoPEx was canceled in March 2024, while sulfur dioxide balloon releases, marine cloud trials and British-funded field work went ahead (Keutsch Group; SRM360; ARIA, April 2026).
    • Relevance. AI Impacts concludes that preventing research can prevent implementation. It estimates forgone value at $1 trillion to $10 trillion a year by mid-century (AI Impacts).
    • Limits. Korzekwa notes that AI has a large research community and that no actor could clearly capture the forgone value (Korzekwa 2023).
  • Blinding Laser Weapons (adopted 1995, in force 1998). Protocol IV to the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons prohibits lasers designed to blind permanently, and their transfer, with no verification mechanism (ACA). The parties adopted it in Vienna on October 13, 1995, and it entered into force on July 30, 1998 (UN Treaty Collection). The same record listed 111 parties on October 7, 2026.
    • Relevance. Scharre and Lamberth rate it a successful preemptive ban and favor rules aimed at an intended effect (CNAS 2022).
    • Limits. Scher and coauthors note that it does not prevent accidental creation, and the danger threshold for AI is unknown (Scher et al. 2025).
  • Antipersonnel Landmines (1999). The Ottawa Convention had 164 parties as of November 2024, and over 50 million stockpiled mines have been destroyed. The United States, Russia and China are outside, and in November 2024 the United States decided to supply such mines to Ukraine (ACA).
    • Relevance. Scharre and Lamberth rate the ban largely successful (CNAS 2022).
    • Limits. The norm spread without the leading powers and does not constrain them (project reading).
  • Weapons in Space (1967). The Outer Space Treaty, with 115 parties as of June 2024, bars weapons of mass destruction in orbit and military bases on the moon. Conventional and anti-satellite weapons stay unrestricted (ACA).
    • Relevance. The Hardwired Pause working paper cites it for the power of norms and mutual predictability (§2.2.3).
    • Limits. The ban is narrow (project reading).
  • Across the cases. Domestic instruments did the work. On this project’s reading, domestic proposals have more precedent here than a treaty does. That covers the Two-Lane Plan’s gated deployment, the Containment Rule for Agentic Reinforcement Learning and the liability regime.

State-Directed Technology Programs

The crash programs show that the U.S. government can move a frontier technology fast. In each it was the sole customer, pursued one goal and feared a rival. The applied literature splits on whether a national AI project would win a race or provoke one.

  • Manhattan Project (1942 to 1946). The program cost $2.2 billion over five fiscal years, or $22 billion in 2008 dollars. Its peak year took about 0.4 percent of GDP, and Congress was kept uninformed (CRS).
  • Apollo (1960 to 1973). CRS counts $19.4 billion over 14 fiscal years ($97.9 billion in 2008 dollars), with a peak year near 0.4 percent of GDP (CRS). The Planetary Society counts $25.8 billion ($309 billion in 2025 dollars); the gap reflects what is included (Planetary Society).
  • Why they worked. CRS credits a single concrete goal, government as sole customer, urgency from a perceived threat and priority funding. It contrasts energy research, which has several goals and needs commercial adoption (CRS).
  • Sematech (1987). Fourteen U.S. chipmakers formed the consortium in 1987 in response to Japanese dominance in memory chips, and it began operations in 1988 (Irwin and Klenow 1994; MIT Technology Review). The federal subsidy, about $100 million a year through DARPA and matched by members, ended with a final appropriation in fiscal year 1996 (Irwin and Klenow 1994; Sematech final report). Its final report counts $848 million from the U.S. government and $863 million from member companies (Sematech final report). A GAO status note says Sematech would seek no federal matching funds after fiscal year 1997 (GAO). One magazine account credits it with a shorter miniaturization cycle and records a critic who called it an exclusive club (MIT Technology Review).
  • Programs dropped. Maas lists two state computing programs that were ended: DARPA’s Strategic Computing Initiative in the early 1990s and the Soviet OGAS network (Maas 2022).
  • Wartime mobilization. Carlsmith, citing Yudkowsky and Soares, uses Allied mobilization to show that very large efforts are possible (Carlsmith 2026; per a page summary). No source on the War Production Board or the Defense Production Act was opened (Not confirmed).
  • Relevance to a contained-development lane.
    • Speed came from one goal and one customer. A federal compute and procurement program, as in Lane One of the Two-Lane Plan, could reproduce both (project reading).
    • Sematech is the closer precedent for an anchor customer: public money coordinated an industry without owning it (project reading).
  • Limits.
    • Private firms build frontier AI, unlike the Manhattan Project, so companies and investors hold stakes (Hardwired Pause working paper, §2.2.4).
    • Zaidi and Dafoe find that secrecy cut both ways in the 1946 control talks (GovAI summary). A secret national program would make international verification harder (project reading).
    • A peak of 0.4 percent of GDP is small beside private AI spending. A February 2026 CNBC report said large technology firms’ AI spending could approach $700 billion that year (working paper reference; not opened). By late July 2026 the four largest hyperscalers guided to more than $735 billion in capital spending for the year (Platformonomics). The open question for a state program is therefore control (project reading).
  • Arguments against a national crash project. Katzke and Futerman argue that the assumptions behind a race also imply higher risks of great-power war and loss of control (arXiv abstract). Hendrycks, Schmidt and Wang propose deterrence, nonproliferation and competitiveness in place of a “Manhattan Project for AGI” (landing page).
  • Across the families. National-project proposals in the Contain and Channel family invoke the Manhattan Project; none was opened for this tab. On the Stop side, Yudkowsky and Soares use wartime mobilization as proof of capacity, as Carlsmith reports. No use by Pace or Continue authors was found.
  • Not confirmed. Manhattan Project contractor roles, employment and secrecy rules. The 2024 U.S.-China commission recommendation of a Manhattan-style AGI program and the 2025 “Genesis Mission” order were not opened.

Lessons and Limits of the Analogies

On this project’s reading of the cases, restraint held when the restrained object was visible, few parties mattered and an inspecting institution existed. Frontier AI meets those conditions mainly through its hardware, a chokepoint both sides say is eroding.

What Recurs

  • Observable units. Agreements center on what can be seen (Hardwired Pause working paper, §2.2.2). The uninspected biological convention was violated, and the inspected chemical convention verified destruction of declared stocks.
  • The parties that matter. Scharre and Lamberth find that which states join matters more than how many (CNAS 2022). INF ended partly over China, and the whaling states objected or left.
  • Staged, imperfect verification. Baker finds that every strong nuclear system followed a weaker one (Baker 2023). Mayhew and coauthors call sufficient confidence a political standard (Mayhew et al. 2026).
  • Value, substitutes and exits. Low-value weapons were easy to ban (CNAS 2022), and Montreal paid those who lost. Madrid wrote its exit in advance, the whaling moratorium did not, and JCPOA sunsets drew attack.
  • Focusing events. The Cuban Missile Crisis preceded the 1963 test ban (State Department). Zaidi and Dafoe warn that support born of threat is fragile (GovAI summary).
  • Duration. The unverified 1958 moratorium and the first gain-of-function pause each lasted about three years; INF lasted 31. Carlsmith expects AI restraint to be feasible for a few years (Carlsmith 2026). The comparison is a project reading.
  • Trigger and exit design. U.S. market-wide circuit breakers halt trading at S&P 500 declines of 7, 13 and 20 percent, for preset periods (SEC). The trigger is a public price. The three tripwires in the Loss-of-Control Tripwire Bill must be measured by evaluations, which models may game (project reading).

Where AI Differs

  • General purpose. Scharre and Lamberth liken a total ban on military AI to a ban on industrialization (CNAS 2022).
  • A moving line. Scher and coauthors report that the compute needed for a given capability falls about threefold a year (Scher et al. 2025). Thresholds must fall with it.
  • Software. Models cannot be inspected from outside and can be copied or stolen (Lamberth and Scharre 2023). The working paper adds that AI systems could target a treaty body (§3.6).
  • Private developers and unknown thresholds. Firms hold the frontier (working paper, §2.2.4). No one knows what training scale is dangerous (Scher et al. 2025).
  • Proponents’ reply. Frontier training still needs very large chip fleets (working paper, §2.2.2). Three countries control about 90 percent of chipmaking equipment (Lamberth and Scharre 2023). Grace and Carlsmith model mutual restraint as a possible equilibrium (Grace 2022).
