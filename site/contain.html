Contain and Channel

The contain-and-channel family keeps frontier development going under state control, inside a secure perimeter, through a joint project or toward safer kinds of systems.

12 entries · 202 open and closed questions

Overview

Every proposal in this family keeps frontier development going and changes who controls it, where it happens, what is built or who uses it. The members disagree on where control sits, on what is constrained, on China’s role and on ownership.

What the Family Shares

  • Development continues. Stop proposals halt frontier training, Pace proposals throttle or gate it, and Continue positions reject restraint. This family keeps training going under new conditions, and several members expect it to speed up. A few add brakes, such as Aguirre’s compute caps and Buterin’s hardware “soft pause.”
  • A lead is treated as time. Aschenbrenner, Amodei and the Two-Lane Plan each treat the U.S. lead over China as a resource. Davidson and Hadshar, who criticize centralization, grant that a larger lead leaves more time to pause (Forethought).
  • Three objections recur. Proponents give a standard answer to each.
    • Concentration of power, raised against consolidated projects. The answer is institutional checks and distributed keys.
    • Unobservability, raised against deterrence and against any deal. The answer is transparency and earlier red lines.
    • Leakage, raised against rules that bind only some actors. The answer is liability, compute controls or export controls.

Where the Members Differ

  • Where control sits. A firm, a state, a club of states or a global body.
  • What is constrained. Who may build, where building happens, what is built, or who may use the result. The Two-Lane Plan constrains where building happens and who may use the result. It leaves the builders private (see the Two-Lane Plan: Full Design tab).
  • China. Most members act without Chinese consent. The international designs and the stability measures in the deterrence strategy need some Chinese participation. AI 2040: Plan A, a compact entry under Additional Entries Found in Research, rests on a verified U.S.-China deal (AI 2040).
  • Ownership. The range runs from unchanged private ownership (entente, deterrence) through merged or public labs (Aschenbrenner, Schneier and Sanders) to internationally held equity (MacAskill, Bostrom).

Two-Lane Plan

Source

Brad Carson, unpublished, October 2026. The full design is in Two-Lane Plan: Full Design. The first write-up is The Two-Lane Plan for Frontier AI.

What It Does

Frontier development continues at full speed inside certified, air-gapped facilities under resident federal examiners. Nothing reaches the public until the developer proves control. The plan treats the U.S. lead over China as time to spend on control, and it assumes no deal with Beijing is available.

How It Works

  • Restricted. Public and commercial deployment of frontier systems, and uncontained internal use once a tripwire is met. Development inside containment continues.
  • Bound. U.S. frontier developers above a coverage threshold, and federal agencies as users.
  • Trigger and exit. Three tripwires: autonomous AI research and development, evasion of oversight, and independent capability to attack critical systems. One bars release until control is proven by clear and convincing evidence, and two make the bar conclusive. Any pause on scaling ends when the U.S. lead is spent. A presidential adversary finding moves the work into a secure government program.
  • Verification. Resident examiners with access to weights and internals, facility certification, lab reports on research done by AI agents, and an official estimate of the lead.

Pros

  • Keeps the capability. Proponents argue that national security users get frontier systems while the public is shielded from systems nobody can yet control.
  • Needs no deal. Every rule binds U.S. actors, which answers the China argument against a pause (see The Case Against Pausing tab).
  • Puts the burden on the developer. S. 2938 sets no pass-or-fail standard, and the FRONTIER Act leaves the burden with the government.
  • Narrows distillation. Fewer frontier systems are served publicly, which shrinks a channel Anthropic documented in February 2026 (Anthropic).
  • Shares the gain. A public stake funds wage insurance and transition support.

Cons

  • Risk moves inside. The AI 2040 authors say internally deployed systems carry most takeover risk (AI 2040). Lawrence Chan of METR and Stix and coauthors argue that large risks arise before any public release (Chan).
  • The lead may be too short. The AI Futures Project estimates four to eight months (August 7, 2026).
  • Security may be out of reach. RAND judged in 2024 that its top security level was not yet achievable (research brief; RAND).
  • Power concentrates. Davidson and Hadshar’s tentative best guess is that a single Western project is probably net bad, because of power concentration (Forethought). The adversary finding invites the same objection.
  • An ungated substitute remains. Foreign open-weight models stay on offer (see the China and Geopolitics tab).
  • Nearby authors rank it lower. Greenblatt and the AI Futures Project place comparable plans below an international agreement (Greenblatt; comparison of plans).

Economy

Gated deployment defers commercial revenue, and anchor contracts replace part of it. No published analysis sizes the effect (see the Economic Effects tab).

Ownership

Labs stay private and plural. The government takes equity or warrants in labs it funds. The Full Design tab proposes a minority stake held apart from the body that decides release. An adversary finding would move work into a government program.

Prior Art and Novelty of the Two-Lane Plan

The Two-Lane Plan is largely a recombination of published ideas. Its two-part shape, secured frontier development with public release held behind a gate, has published relatives from October 2023 onward, and a 2026 practice for cyber-capable models resembles it informally. A second round of searches and full readings, completed on October 7, 2026, tested six features of the plan against essays, papers, bills and statutes. Four were not found in any source opened: the two-step evidentiary gate, the lead limit written as a legal rule tied to an official estimate, the adversary finding, and the two-lane label. The financing package has one close published relative, Bores’s proposal of June 9, 2026. Two 2023 essays that may anticipate the two-part shape could not be opened. The result holds as far as public search reaches, and the limits are listed at the end of this section.

How Nearby Authors Would Classify the Plan

The notes infer where published typologies would place the plan.

  • Greenblatt’s Plan B. Greenblatt puts the chance of that situation at 10 percent. He estimates takeover risk at 13 percent under it, against 7 percent under his Plan A, an international deal.
  • Lifland’s Plan C+. All five authors whose estimates the supplement lists rate it below the international deal on avoiding misaligned takeover. Three of the five rate it below Plan B, and two rate it above (see the AI 2027 Slowdown Branch section).
  • The surgical pause and “burning a lead.” The plan’s lead budget writes Scott Alexander’s and Carlsmith’s idea as a rule.
  • Other labels. Private, plural labs under examiners resemble Cheng and Katzke’s soft nationalization. Triolo’s second and third stages cover the same ground as a forecast.

The notes’ own description is a synthesis that turns secured-project and spend-the-lead ideas into a domestic regulatory design. Its evidentiary gate was found in no source opened.

Objections the Literature Points To

The notes call the plan’s central bet its most exposed choice: full-speed internal development under containment while public release waits.

  • Internal deployment carries the risk. The AI 2040 authors say internally deployed systems carry most takeover risk and rank limiting the internal-external gap as their top transparency measure. Lawrence Chan at METR (2025) argues that holding or internally using a powerful model can pose large risks to the public before any release. Stix and others add undetected accumulation of power behind closed doors.
  • The opposite bet is published. One AI Futures Project option lets only models at least nine months old assist AI R&D, a “negative internal-public gap.”
  • Centralized projects. Davidson and Hadshar (2024) give a tentative best guess that a single Western project is probably net bad, because of power concentration. National Project

Source

Leopold Aschenbrenner, “The Project,” Chapter IV of Situational Awareness: The Decade Ahead, May 29, 2024 (Aschenbrenner). Related: Recommendation 1 of the U.S.-China Economic and Security Review Commission (USCC), November 2024 (USCC), and “America’s Superintelligence Project,” Gladstone AI, April 2025 (Gladstone AI, read in part).

What It Does

Aschenbrenner predicts that the U.S. government will run AGI development by 2027 or 2028, and he argues that it should. Leading labs and cloud providers join one secured effort under a government chain of command. The USCC text is narrower: multiyear federal contracts for private firms and a defense priority rating for AI items. Gladstone AI wants security agencies inside the project from the start.

How It Works

  • Restricted. Access, while development speeds up. A few hundred core researchers move to a secure location, and civilian applications wait until the situation stabilizes.
  • Bound. The leading U.S. labs and cloud providers, with cleared staff. Forms run from a defense-contractor relationship to a joint venture or a merger that Aschenbrenner describes as voluntary.
  • Trigger and exit. A forecast start in 2027 or 2028, with no end date. A limited search found no merged or federally owned frontier lab through October 2026. The Genesis Mission (Executive Order 14363, November 24, 2025) is an Energy Department science platform without control over private frontier development.
  • Verification. Secure facilities, vetting and continuous monitoring of staff, government physical security at data centers, and intelligence help against weight theft. Congress oversees through appropriations and Senate confirmation of key officials.

Pros

  • No private chief executive should hold a capability of this military weight, and government has checks that lab governance lacks (Aschenbrenner).
  • Private security cannot withstand state espionage, and commercial incentives push labs to race (Aschenbrenner). Gladstone AI adds that frontier labs are probably penetrated by the Chinese Communist Party already.
  • Only a state can enforce nonproliferation. Aschenbrenner pairs a coalition of democracies modeled on the Quebec Agreement with an Atoms for Peace bargain for other states.
  • A larger U.S. lead leaves more time to pause later, a point two critics grant (Davidson and Hadshar).

Cons

  • The assumptions that justify a race also imply that it is extremely dangerous. It raises the risk of great-power conflict and loss of control, and it endangers liberal democracy (Katzke and Futerman, abstract page only).
  • Centralization probably does more harm than good because it concentrates power and could prompt China to accelerate (Davidson and Hadshar, who stress deep uncertainty). The AI Futures Project adds a loss of public transparency.
  • The USCC proposal looks like a trial balloon, and the Manhattan analogy may not fit a general-purpose technology (Ball).
  • An AGI race is one both sides lose, and the USCC report wrongly presents AGI as controllable (Tegmark).
  • A concentrated bid for dominance invites sabotage by rivals (Hendrycks, Schmidt and Wang; the expert version was read in part on October 7, 2026).

Economy

Aschenbrenner expects trillions of dollars in appropriations for chips and power and a wartime economic footing, followed by a pluralistic civilian market. Ball reads the USCC’s priority rating as first call for the government on GPUs and data centers. Bostrom’s 2025 model puts the global wealth Gini at about 0.96 for a U.S.-only nationalized project and about 0.89 to 0.92 under open investment.

Ownership

In Aschenbrenner’s account the labs merge and cloud providers join a consortium in which private companies remain. Staff come under a clearance regime, and investor compensation did not appear in the reading of his chapter. The USCC text leaves firms private, and neither Aschenbrenner nor Gladstone AI prescribes literal nationalization.

Nationalization and Soft Nationalization

Source

Deric Cheng and Corin Katzke, “Soft Nationalization: How the US Government Will Control AI Labs,” Convergence Analysis, August 27, 2024 (EA Forum copy). Paul Triolo, “Will Washington and Beijing Nationalize AI Labs?” Cairo Review of Global Affairs, June 17, 2026 (Triolo). Bruce Schneier and Nathan E. Sanders, The Guardian, August 12, 2026 (Schneier and Sanders).

What It Does

Cheng and Katzke predict, without endorsing it, that Washington will tighten control of frontier labs in stages through levers short of ownership. Triolo reads 2026 events as that process under way. Schneier and Sanders argue for outright public ownership if markets abandon OpenAI and Anthropic. Palantir’s Alex Karp reportedly said the labs may need nationalizing because their risks are unlimited (Startup Fortune; secondary report, wording not confirmed).

How It Works

  • Restricted. Cheng and Katzke list thirteen groups of levers. Examples are pre-approval of large training runs, customer whitelists, compute permits, staff clearances, containment requirements and golden shares. They state that the government will not slow frontier development without an international agreement.
  • Bound. Private frontier labs, with their investors and staff. Under Schneier and Sanders, model research becomes national laboratories under congressional oversight and compute becomes a regulated utility.
  • Trigger and exit. Cheng and Katzke’s highest scenario is full acquisition with an equity buyout after a bioweapon-capable model. Triolo’s triggers are capability thresholds, lost government visibility, military dependence on frontier models and geopolitical shocks. The trigger for Schneier and Sanders is market collapse, and none of the sources describes an exit.
  • Verification. Triolo describes three layers of control: visibility through reporting and evaluations, conditional permission over releases, and operational integration through classified work and procurement. Tools used in 2026 included a Pentagon supply-chain-risk designation of Anthropic and a 19-day export-control suspension of two models (Defense One; Anthropic).

Pros

  • Lighter levers can meet the state’s security goals at lower cost than seizure (Cheng and Katzke).
  • Public ownership replaces shareholder incentives with public accountability (Schneier and Sanders).
  • Only government offers a single chain of command and state-grade security (Aschenbrenner).
  • Private firms cannot carry unlimited risk, and customers would otherwise sue (Karp, as reported).

Cons

  • State leverage can be turned against a lab’s own safeguards. In February 2026 the Defense Secretary demanded an all-lawful-uses term for Anthropic’s models and threatened the Defense Production Act (Defense One; Wikipedia, tertiary). Jeremy Kahn reads that threat as soft nationalization (Fortune, March 3, 2026).
  • Voluntary frameworks can harden into soft mandates, as Davis Wright Tremaine warned about the June 2, 2026 executive order on pre-release access.
  • Treating frontier AI as weapons-adjacent can become self-fulfilling, and Beijing may already see U.S. labs as security instruments (Triolo).
  • Nationalization concentrates power and reduces public transparency (Davidson and Hadshar; AI Futures Project). The risk of expropriation also deters private investment (Bostrom).
  • The frontier firms are trillion-dollar companies whose AI units cannot be cleanly carved out, and legal and shareholder resistance is expected (Cheng and Katzke).

Economy

Cheng and Katzke report a belief that full nationalization would hurt the U.S. lead. Under softer control they expect investors to face dilution, profit regulation and narrower exits. Schneier and Sanders start from a market verdict that the labs cannot earn a return, and no source here addresses the global economy.

Ownership

Under soft nationalization firms keep formal ownership and lose autonomy over research priorities, customers, compute and deployment. The shareholding levers run from minority stakes and golden shares to full acquisition, and Schneier and Sanders add civil-service pay for staff. Anthropic stayed investor-owned through this period, with a reported $65 billion round at a $965 billion valuation in May 2026 (Wikipedia, tertiary).

Entente: A Coalition of Democracies

Source

Dario Amodei, “Machines of Loving Grace,” October 2024 (Amodei 2024), and “The Adolescence of Technology,” January 2026 (Amodei 2026; about three-quarters read).

What It Does

A coalition of democracies secures the AI supply chain, scales quickly and blocks or delays adversaries’ access to chips and chipmaking equipment. Its military superiority is the stick and a share of AI’s benefits is the carrot, so that more countries join. Frontier development stays in private labs that work closely with democratic governments. The 2026 essay adds limits on what democracies themselves may do with AI.

How It Works

  • Restricted. Sales of chips, chipmaking tools and data centers to China, which Amodei compares to nuclear weapons sales to North Korea. For democracies, mass surveillance and mass propaganda are out of bounds, and fully autonomous weapons need extreme care. Development inside the coalition continues at speed.
  • Bound. Chip and equipment exporters in the United States and allied states, and democratic governments through self-restraint. Labs face transparency laws such as California SB 53 and New York’s RAISE Act, with exemptions for small firms.
  • Trigger and exit. The strategy starts now and has no end date. The 2024 essay hopes the democracies’ position of 1991 can be made lasting. In 2026 Amodei’s own company was designated a Pentagon supply-chain risk after it refused an all-lawful-uses contract term (Defense One).
  • Verification. Coalition governments enforce export controls. No negotiated agreement with China is proposed. Amodei says norms against AI-enabled totalitarianism are needed and that political winds have turned against international cooperation.

Pros

  • A pause is infeasible because the technology advances regardless of any one company, and autocracies would continue. Export controls buy time (Amodei 2026).
  • Chips are the most important single lever (Amodei 2026). The United States and its democratic allies hold most of the chip supply chain’s value (O’Keefe).
  • A coalition that reached predominance could defend itself and contain autocracies, and shared benefits give other states a reason to join (Amodei).
  • The plan binds its own side. It names AI companies as a power-concentration risk that ordinary corporate governance cannot handle (Amodei 2026).
  • A Western lead can be spent on safety, a point Davidson and Hadshar grant (Forethought).

Cons

  • Both sides lose if either finishes, because the world is closer to building AGI than to controlling it (Tegmark, October 13, 2024). His alternative is national safety standards that permit only Tool AI until control is shown.
  • The reasoning that justifies racing for decisive advantage also implies conflict risk, loss-of-control risk and damage to democracy (Katzke and Futerman, abstract page only).
  • Limits that apply to everyone equally are preferable to one side seeking dominance (Buterin, January 5, 2025).
  • A bid for unilateral dominance would be met with sabotage (Hendrycks, Schmidt and Wang, abstract page only).
  • Amodei grants that AI does not structurally favor democracy and that victory is not assured.

Economy

The 2024 essay says powerful AI could compress 50 to 100 years of biological progress into 5 to 10. The 2026 essay says AI could displace half of entry-level white-collar jobs within one to five years. It sees sustained annual GDP growth of 10 to 20% as possible and mentions progressive taxation and stronger safety nets.

Ownership

Unchanged. Private labs stay investor-owned and work in partnership with democratic governments. Amodei supports transparency laws and public commitments by the companies.

Deterrence and Nonproliferation

Source

Dan Hendrycks (Center for AI Safety), Eric Schmidt and Alexandr Wang, “Superintelligence Strategy,” March 7, 2025, revised April 14, 2025 (arXiv 2503.05628; nationalsecurity.ai). Reply to critics: Hendrycks and Adam Khoja, “AI Deterrence Is Our Best Option,” AI Frontiers, September 22, 2025 (Hendrycks and Khoja). The abstract and the site summary were read for this entry. On October 7, 2026 the expert version was read as far as its appendix, for the prior-art check only.

What It Does

The strategy has three parts: deterrence, nonproliferation and competitiveness. Under Mutual Assured AI Malfunction (MAIM), any state’s aggressive bid for unilateral AI dominance is met by rivals’ preventive sabotage. The authors say this already describes the strategic position of the AI superpowers. They reject both a hands-off posture and a national bid for monopoly.

How It Works

  • Restricted. A destabilizing bid for dominance by any state, and the spread of chips, model weights and dangerous capabilities to rogue actors. Ordinary development by private firms continues in each state.
  • Bound. Rival states, which deter each other. Firms are bound through export controls on AI chips, weight security and safeguards against misuse.
  • Trigger and exit. A rival’s aggressive bid sets off an escalation ladder: espionage and covert cyber sabotage, then overt cyberattacks, then kinetic strikes on data centers. The regime has no end date.
  • Verification. Each side watches the other through intelligence. Proposed stability measures are escalation protocols, transparency and verification, data centers sited away from population centers, and AI-assisted inspections. Chips would carry firmware features such as geolocation.

Pros

  • Deterrence already describes the position of the AI superpowers, by the authors’ account, so it can operate before any agreement exists.
  • Fully automated AI research could give one side an overwhelming lead within months to years (Hendrycks and Khoja).
  • Deterrence does not need precise thresholds. Transparency and verification allow earlier and broader red lines with a margin (Hendrycks and Khoja).
  • MAIM is preemptive disruption, so attribution matters less than under retaliatory deterrence, and escalation ladders reduce uncontrolled preemption (Hendrycks and Khoja).
  • The Hardwired Pause working paper (October 2026, unpublished draft) treats this kind of deterrence as a possible complement that discourages violations of a pause.

Cons

  • Mutual assured destruction deterred use, and MAIM tries to deter development, which has no clear brink. Strikes on data centers are acts of war, and the regime creates first-strike incentives. Halting costs a state about as much as being sabotaged, so the threat may not bite (Rehman, Mueller and Mazarr, RAND, March 13, 2025).
  • Deterrence needs red lines that are monitorable and communicated. Automated AI research is too vague a line, and sabotage delays more than it denies (Abecassis, MIRI, April 11, 2025).
  • Compute and data center metrics miss algorithmic advances. Errors then run both ways: needless sabotage or an undetected breakout (Arnold, AI Frontiers, August 14, 2025).
  • The two states share no understanding of the regime, and attribution is hard (Wildeford and Delaney, 2025; exact date not shown).
  • One analyst puts about 25% on MAIM working descriptively: geopolitical dynamics delay AI development by six months or more (Delaney, IAPS; 2025 by inference). Kinetic action is far outside current norms, and China might simply fast-follow.
  • Hendrycks and Khoja concede that red lines cannot be as empty as AGI and that intelligence services may see the stakes late.

Economy

The competitiveness pillar calls for domestic chip manufacturing, military adoption of AI, legal frameworks for AI agents and immigration for AI talent. Effects on the U.S. and global economy, including the cost of sabotage or strikes, were not addressed in the material read.

Ownership

Not addressed in the material read, and nothing in it changes ownership. Private firms continue within each state, and the authors argue against a single concentrated project.

  • A CERN for AI risks a budget too small for Europe’s needs, slow EU processes, bureaucratic capture and weak commercialization (the blueprint’s own list).
  • For a China that neither joins nor is denied chips, the designs offer exclusion from AI trade (MacAskill) or accept a weaker scheme (O’Keefe).

  • Economic incentives favor open-ended autonomous systems, and military speed may require unified command (commenters on Drexler’s 2023 post).

Source

The concept is Toby Shevlane’s, in “Structured access: an emerging paradigm for safe AI deployment” (arXiv 2201.05159, January 13, 2022; abstract page read). What It Does

Developers offer controlled, arm’s-length use of a system, usually through the cloud, and keep the software itself. That lets them limit uses and stop users from removing limits by modification or reverse engineering. The goal is to keep dangerous capabilities out of wide availability while safe uses continue.

How It Works

  • Restricted. Who may use a model, and for what. General users face refusals and automated monitoring, and vetted users reach more capable or more permissive models. Anthropic held Claude Mythos Preview back from general release.
  • Bound. The developer binds its own users. In June 2026 the Commerce Department bound a developer through an export control directive.
  • Trigger and exit. Labs have gated on cyber capability, and Anthropic aims to deploy Mythos-class models at scale once safeguards mature. The June 12 directive followed a reported bypass of Fable 5’s safeguards. The Commerce Secretary withdrew the controls on June 30, after a classifier fix and commitments by Anthropic, and Fable 5 returned on July 1.
  • Verification. Identity verification and customer vetting gate the higher tiers. The June 2 executive order adds a classified cyber benchmark that defines which models are covered.

Defensive Acceleration

Source

Vitalik Buterin, My techno-optimism (November 27, 2023) and d/acc: one year later (January 5, 2025). The parent idea is differential technology development. Jonas Sandbrink, Hamish Hobbs, Jacob Swett, Allan Dafoe and Anders Sandberg set it out (SSRN 4213670, posted October 9, 2022; abstract page read).

What It Does

Differential technology development changes the order in which technologies arrive, with protective ones first and dangerous ones later. Buterin’s d/acc applies that to the present and favors decentralized defensive technology over both unrestrained acceleration and central control. By January 2025 he had added two restraints: liability rules and a hardware “soft pause.”

How It Works

  • Restricted. In the 2025 version, uses exposed to liability and, in an emergency, industrial-scale compute. Such hardware would carry a trusted chip that needs weekly signatures from three international bodies, at least one non-military.
  • Bound. Liability falls first on users, then on deployers and developers, because users cannot cover catastrophic damages. The soft pause binds owners of industrial-scale AI hardware worldwide.
  • Trigger and exit. At a critical moment the soft pause could cut worldwide compute by about 90% to 99% for one to two years (Buterin, 2025). Buterin calls both measures temporary stopgaps, and the notes record no test for the moment.
  • Verification. The chip enforces the pause, because hardware stops without the signatures. Defensive work has no compliance check.

Pros

  • It avoids both a single controlling entity and an unregulated race in which one actor pulls ahead (Buterin, 2023).
  • Limits that apply to everyone equally are safer than a U.S.-led race to decisive advantage (Buterin, 2025).
  • Timing can be shifted with tools that exist: research funding, regulation, philanthropy and corporate responsibility measures (Sandbrink and co-authors).
  • A hardware pause avoids legal exemptions and consumer surveillance (Buterin, 2025).
  • It combines with other plans. The research notes read the Two-Lane Plan’s cyber and bio hardening as d/acc inside containment.
  • In 2026 defenders received early access to frontier cyber capability, with credits, through Glasswing and Trusted Access for Cyber.

Cons

  • Buterin concedes that acceleration of the good is insufficient if superintelligence is three to five years away, so the bad must be slowed.
  • The approach needs foresight about a technology’s effects and the ability to shift timing, limits its authors state.
  • Defensive public goods undercut ordinary business models and need public-goods funding (Buterin, 2025).
  • The 2026 defensive access ran through two firms and a government directive, which is centralized (a reading in the research notes).
  • The notes cite Garfinkel and Dafoe (2019) as a caution on offense-defense forecasts (War on the Rocks; not opened).
  • No published critique of d/acc was opened, so the standard objections are Not confirmed.

Economy

d/acc leaves most development running and speeds defensive technology in four areas: resilient infrastructure, biodefense, cyber defense and information defense. A soft pause would cut worldwide compute by about 90% to 99% for one to two years. No source in the notes estimates that cost or the effects by country.

Ownership

Ownership does not change, and no central controller exists. Buterin prefers open-source and public-goods models, paid for through quadratic funding, retroactive funding and his “deep funding.” In 2023 he suggested that open-source requirements for allowed models might be safer than licensing that concentrates power.

Plan R: AI Safety by ASICs

Source

Roko, “Plan R: AI Safety by ASICs”, LessWrong, September 25, 2026. Roko extends it in “Plan R+, Diversity, Escrow and Political Rights for ASICs” (September 26) and “Skeuomorphic AI Safety” (September 27). Most objections below come from the first post’s comment thread, read on October 9, 2026. Comment dates were not recorded.

What It Does

Roko traces much of AI’s civilization-scale risk to organizations that build very powerful or self-replicating systems, which may outstrip the rest of civilization. The same organizations own an unbounded financial claim on the resulting surplus. Plan R splits the two properties between two kinds of entity and burns every deployed model into a chip. Roko calls the approach “AI Safety by skeuomorphism”: AI changed to behave more like humans, which the world is already set up to handle.

How It Works

  • Restricted. AI research and development organizations train and test frontier models. They cannot issue equity or use their own models. Physically enforced latency on their air-gapped perimeter keeps data from moving in or out in less than about 12 hours. Deployment companies run inference and build products. They may not train new models or hold general-purpose AI chips (Plan R+).
  • Bound. Frontier developers and owners of advanced compute everywhere, through an international agreement. Roko thinks one feasible because the industry now exists in only two countries, which the post does not name. Outside the research organizations, all high-spec compute would take the form of ASICs.
  • Trigger and exit. The post sets no start date and no exit. A model that passes its evaluations is burned into an ASIC, and a run of R units is sold. The original is then deleted or encrypted. For added safety, Roko suggests a lag. An ASIC would work on real-world tasks for about three months before a research organization could use it. He says the lag would put a “speed limit” on the Singularity.
  • Verification. The post names no inspector or agency. Research organizations would face some unusual scrutiny, and Roko suggests mandatory write-once logs as one example. In a reply to a commenter, he adds that each chip would be a sealed unit that must be accounted for.
  • The Plan R+ extension. Roko grants that a deceptively misaligned model could pass the tests. Plan R+ answers with about 1,000 “lineages” per generation, trained on varied inputs. Each lineage gets about 10,000 chips, released in stages. A lineage that misbehaves would be recalled, or at least its unreleased chips held back. AIs whose lineage completes without misbehaving would gain political representation and property.

Pros

  • Separating the two properties removes the incentive to race, by Roko’s account. He argues that nuclear and bioweapon technology stay mostly safe because their holders have no financial reason to push their luck.
  • A rogue AI finds little compute to capture. Roko writes that there would be no compute overhang to seize, and a rogue AI would be left with CPUs and perhaps GPUs. He adds that GPUs might be changed so they cannot run AI models, though more work is needed.
  • No lab can use its own models in its research. Roko says this “anti-dogfooding” rule would stop any one AI company from “going nuclear” on its own.
  • Development and deployment continue, and Roko says the plan needs no pause or ban. He hopes the split, with help from ASICs, can also leave a very productive AI industry that delivers most of AI’s benefits.
  • Roko judges it more acceptable to the major powers than a halt. He rates it well above a total shutdown of AI research (“Plan S”) and “Plan A” from their point of view. The post does not define Plan A. The AI Futures Project uses both labels, with Plan S an indefinite halt (Lifland).
  • Inspection can confirm which model a chip runs. Only weight-fixed chips allow that check (Verification and Technology tab), and Plan R puts every deployed model on one.
  • The hardware exists at small scale. A commenter, joec, cites a demo of Llama 3.1 8B burned into an ASIC. It ran at about 17,000 tokens a second, against about 500 on a B300. The Verification and Technology tab records Taalas’s HC1, which fixes the weights of that model.

Cons

  • Frontier models may be too large to hardwire. A commenter who designs ASICs, MBaert, says a fully unrolled design is not feasible for frontier models because they are too big. MBaert estimates about 500 distinct chips for a model of about 10 trillion weights, with output near 34 tokens a second.
  • MBaert prefers a fixed-architecture chip with cryptographically signed weights, backed by remote monitoring and shutdown. The authenticated-weight chip in the Hardwired Pause working paper is similar (project reading).
  • Vladimir_Nesov writes that hardcoded weights do not really work for the biggest models. He thinks ternary weights with quantization-aware training could help, and sees the need for thousands of tapeouts as more of a crux.
  • Chips tied to one model age fast. A June 18, 2026 post by Gloria Z, a MIRI Technical Governance fellow, judges weight-fused ASICs unlikely to meet industry needs. The post says frontier architectures change often and load varies. Google reportedly dropped a weight-fixed first chip for a similar reason (Verification and Technology tab). In “Skeuomorphic AI Safety”, Roko replies that the commercial inconvenience may be what keeps the arrangement stable.
  • It still needs an international deal and inspectors. Daniel Kokotajlo says Plan R needs many elements of Plan A or Plan S, such as a deal and inspectors who count chips. He asks how research organizations would be financed and regulated. He also asks what would stop former staff of a deployment firm, who hold its equity, from founding a research firm to supply it. Roko replies that basically anything other than the default (“Plan D”) needs some kind of compute governance.
  • It asks for extraordinary cooperation. Maxsimal says its biggest problem is the extreme level of civilizational cooperation it requires. Maxsimal adds that a misaligned model could corrupt a production model before it is burned in.
  • Burned-in weights may still be read out. The commenter cqb writes that burned-in weights alone do not stop extraction, and encryption would add many transistors per weight. Roko answers that only research organizations would hold GPUs. Taking chips apart would be illegal, and he says teardown might be extremely hard.
  • Enforcement reaches ordinary computers. The commenter _rpd argues that every attempt to separate inference from other computing is unworkable, and that AI researchers would become “thought criminals.” Roko replies that limits on memory, storage and fast networking would make ordinary computers almost useless for language models. RAM might be capped at about 4 GB.
  • The nuclear analogy is disputed. Vaniver thinks the nuclear arms buildup came mainly from “unbounded claim” dynamics, since a first strike might let a state rule the world. Vaniver grants that IOUs may move the takeoff up from 2030 to 2027 or 2028. In Vaniver’s view, AIs may soon pay for their own compute, so dismantling finance would still leave exponential takeoff. Mlxa credits nuclear stability to mutual second-strike capability.
  • No published response outside LessWrong was found in two searches on October 9, 2026.

Economy

The post gives no cost estimate. Outside the research organizations, general-purpose AI chips would give way to model-specific ones. Plan R+ would remove most AI-capable GPU compute from the rest of the world. For scale, the Hardwired Pause working paper puts the cost of existing accelerators at about $500 billion (§5.3.2, unpublished draft). Each new model needs its own chip run, and Taalas reports about two months per model on its platform (Verification and Technology tab). Makers of general-purpose accelerators would lose the deployment market, and makers of model-specific chips would gain it (project reading). A commenter, joec, suggests firms might move frontier inference to ASICs for cost reasons if capabilities stall. The posts do not address effects on other economies.

Ownership

Plan R and Plan R+ state the split directly. Frontier AI companies become research organizations that cannot issue equity and deployment companies that can. The research side would be run like a cross between a nuclear weapons lab and a global systemically important bank. Roko floats debt financing and says he will have to think more about it. Deployment companies pay the research side for models and stay on Silicon Valley rules. In a comment, Roko suggests barring research staff and financiers from stakes in deployment firms. He cites the Sarbanes-Oxley Act as a precedent. None of the three posts says what happens to current investors in the frontier labs.

AI 2040: Plan A

Source

Thomas Larsen, Romeo Dean, Brendan Halstead, Eli Lifland, Ryan Greenblatt and Daniel Kokotajlo, AI Futures Project (AI 2040, summary). The Hardwired Pause working paper’s reference list dates it July 9, 2026, and the site shows no date.

What It Does

A verified U.S.-China deal from 2029 blocks fully automated AI R&D, pauses scaling at top-human-expert level in 2035 and resumes it in 2040. Research is almost fully public, many companies in many countries stay at the frontier, and data centers carry self-destruct switches. Inference data centers sit in Faraday cages, and R&D data centers have a 1 MB/s cap on outbound data (Security in Plan A).

Main Argument For

Five authors’ estimates of avoiding misaligned takeover run from 70% to 81%, and Lifland rates the plan highest on distribution of power (Comparing Possible Plans). The authors’ economic model shows about 90% average annual real output growth in 2032 to 2037 and an equal Citizen’s Dividend (Economics of Plan A).

Main Argument Against

Tom Davidson calls the paused software and growing compute “dry tinder” (Davidson). Compute grows about 100x by 2033, so a collapsed deal would mean a far faster intelligence explosion. Eli Lifland puts the cumulative chance of deal decline by 2040 at 48% to 51% (Deal Decline).

Whole-Lab Inspection

Source

Felix Choussat and Adam Khoja, Center for AI Safety, An International AI Slowdown Is Ready Whenever Politicians Are, AI Frontiers, August 5, 2026.

What It Does

U.S. and Chinese inspectors get broad physical access to frontier AI companies and read-only access to communications, work logs, code repositories and compute telemetry. Low-tech human inspection is enough, as in nuclear arms control. Staff turnover, hacking and espionage would still expose the projects of a state that refused (the authors).

The design assumes U.S. and Chinese willingness. Critics, as the authors report, say verification needs technology that does not yet exist.

Family Pros, Cons and Questions

The family makes one bet. Frontier development continues, and safety comes from who controls it, where it runs, what is built or who may use it. Its members draw three recurring objections: concentration of power, unobservability and competitive leakage.

Shared Arguments For

  • The capability stays in hand. Amodei holds that a pause is infeasible and that autocracies would continue regardless (Amodei 2026). Aschenbrenner calls superintelligence the most important defense project since the bomb (The Project).
  • Most members can start without a treaty. A national project, the entente, structured access and Tool AI standards need no agreement with China. Tegmark presents Tool AI as a national strategy that needs no treaty (Hopium Wars).
  • A lead can be spent on safety. Davidson and Hadshar grant that a bigger U.S. lead means more time to pause later (Forethought). The AI Futures Project’s Plan C has the leading company spend part of its lead that way.
  • Security at state level needs the state. RAND finds that securing weights against the most capable states takes years and probably government help (RAND). Gladstone AI argues that security cannot be added later (Gladstone AI).
  • Restricting use has worked in practice. In 2026 two labs ran identity-verified access tiers and one withheld its strongest model. A Commerce Department directive pulled two deployed models for 19 days (Anthropic).
  • Channeling is being financed. LawZero reports a joint commitment of up to $300 million from Canada and Germany, announced September 16, 2026 (LawZero).

Shared Arguments Against

  • Concentration of power. Davidson and Hadshar’s tentative best guess is that one Western project is probably net bad. AI 2027’s authors flag that their Oversight Committee could entrench itself (summary). Bostrom calculates a global wealth Gini of about 0.96 for a U.S.-only nationalized project, against about 0.89 to 0.92 under open investment (Bostrom).
  • The race continues. Tegmark argues that both sides lose if either finishes, because the world is closer to building AGI than to controlling it. Katzke and Futerman argue that the assumptions behind a race also make it extremely dangerous (arXiv 2501.14749).
  • Unobservability. RAND, MIRI and Arnold argue that rivals cannot reliably see a capability red line being crossed. The research notes extend the point to any tripwire defined inside a lab.
  • Competitive leakage. Rules that bind only some actors lose to those left unbound, because economic and military incentives favor agents (commenters on Drexler).
  • The state’s 2026 record. In February 2026 the Pentagon pressed Anthropic to drop its own limits on military use (Kahn, Fortune). Anthropic says the June recall lacked a transparent statutory process.
  • Lower odds by the forecasters’ own count. The AI Futures Project gives domestic regulation plus non-sabotage steps 40% to 60%. A verified international slowdown gets 70% to 81% (Comparing Possible Plans).
  • Security may be out of reach. AI 2040’s authors rate SL5 viable only with extreme effort and possibly impossible (Security in Plan A).
